# BACKGROUND AND PROGRAM CONTEXT
## Program Overview: Advancing Justice Uganda
The Legatum Foundation is implementing “Advancing Justice Uganda” in partnership with six Civil Society Organizations (CSOs), with the overarching ambition of improving access to justice, strengthening accountability across the justice chain, and reducing systemic bottlenecks that drive congestion and unfair delays. The program will be implemented from 2026-203, in districts of Bushenyi, Sheema, Rubirizi, Kampala, Nebbi, Koboko, Yumbe, Lira, Pader, Kitgum, Omoro, Mbale, Tororo and Soroti.
The 2026–2030 Uganda program aims to-Goal
Reduce people entering prisons unnecessary
Reduce time spent on remand for these in prisons through access to legal assistance and increased use of non-custodia sentencing options
Increase the chance of successful reintegration of incarcerated through engagement with these in prison and community
Contribute to better functioning of JLOS system through strategic advocacy
Achieve the above in a way that proves cost effective scalable and replicable models
## Problem Statement - Uganda justice bottlenecks
Uganda’s justice system faces a set of interlocking constraints that jointly produce delays, congestion, and avoidable incarceration. The system is characterized by excessive pre-trial detention, severe case backlogs, and the systematic denial of fair and timely hearings, spanning the three core pillars of the justice chain: policing, the judiciary, and prisons.
A central bottleneck relevant to this evaluation is the upstream inflow of petty cases into formal justice institutions. Minor disputes and low-level offences (i.e, many of which could be handled through alternative or community-based mechanisms) often enter the formal system, contributing to unnecessary arrests, longer remand durations, and increased pressure on courts and prisons.
This “pipeline” problem is not only about efficiency; it is also about legitimacy and access. When the formal system is overloaded, the consequences are typically borne most heavily by those with fewer resources (e.g., limited access to legal representation, higher costs of case-follow-up, and greater vulnerability to long remand). The program’s baseline framing explicitly links these dynamics to chronic overcrowding and prolonged remand, driven by constraints such as delayed investigations, limited legal aid, and slow court processing.
Even strong downstream legal aid and prison-based interventions will struggle to decongest the system sustainably if petty cases continue entering at scale. The prevention pathway addresses congestion closer to its source by shifting eligible disputes toward timely, local resolution and ensuring appropriate routing (i.e., diversion for petty matters; referral for serious cases), consistent with the program’s core logic.

## 3.2. Program objective 2: Norms Change
This objective seeks to shift public and institutional attitudes toward incarcerated individuals and the criminal justice system, moving from a “guilty until proven innocent” mindset to one grounded in dignity, respect, and responsibility. Using behavioral science and human-centered design, Legatum will work with a behavior change specialist to develop and pilot communication approaches that reshape how justice actors and the public perceive people who have experienced incarceration. Lessons from the pilot will inform a program-wide communication strategy.
The approach will include: (i) Storytelling to humanize incarceration by sharing lived experiences and post-release contributions of formerly incarcerated individuals; (ii) Champions of Change, recognizing justice officials who demonstrate exemplary, rights-based practice through transparent annual awards; and (iii) Accountability and capacity building, including dialogues with justice actors, targeted trainings, public performance reporting, and strengthened human rights and case management committees.
Together, these efforts will improve service quality within the Justice, Law and Order Sector, reduce stigma against former prisoners, and minimize systemic bottlenecks that limit fair and speedy access to justice.

Evidence and learning from program implementation will feed into advocacy and network efforts, reinforcing norm change and generating momentum for sustained, system-wide reform.

IMPACT EVALUATION
The intervention prioritized in this evaluation is a structured training and post-training support package for Local Council Courts (LCCs)/Local Council leaders, delivered using an official training manual (with updates aligned to the Ministry of Local Government handbook). By strengthening LC capacity to classify, mediate, and resolve petty cases locally, while improving appropriate referral for serious matters, the intervention aims to divert eligible cases before they reach police stations and courts, reducing pressure on formal institutions and mitigating avoidable detention and delay.
Not all LCs can be treated at the same time due to logistical constraints; the intervention is expected to roll out over multiple years. This phased implementation is a defining feature for the evaluation strategy (e.g., waitlist / randomized phase-in feasibility).
A key design feature is a Training-of-Trainers (ToT)/mentoring component intended to diffuse knowledge beyond directly trained LCsCDFU, trained leaders are expected to mentor additional LCs; ToTs are equipped with phones to support tracking and reporting.
This diffusion element is programmatically valuable, but it also creates evaluation-relevant risks of spillovers/contamination that must be explicitly managed in the research design and measurement strategy (and later reflected in the evaluation questions and estimands).
The intervention’s core target is petty cases entering the formal system, which will be measured primarily through administrative sources, including LC registers, police registers, and court registry records. Partners anticipate relying on 2024/25 register data as the key historical pre-period baseline for trends and benchmarking. The classification of eligible petty cases will follow the Local Council Act and the LC mandate (ensuring alignment between program operations, the training content, and evaluation outcomes).
Does LC/LCC training and structured support reduce the flow of petty cases entering the formal justice system (police and courts), relative to a credible counterfactual?
This question will be answered using a causal identification strategy suited to community-level governance interventions and designed to account explicitly for phased implementation and potential spillovers arising from the Training-of-Trainers (ToT) diffusion component.
To ensure that impact estimates translate into implementable learning, the evaluation will be structured around a compact set of secondary questions that explain how and for whom the intervention works, and whether it improves outcomes without creating harms or inequities.
On mechanisms, the evaluation will assess whether the intervention changes the upstream “processing” of disputes in ways consistent with the theory of change, specifically whether training improves (i) classification and triage (distinguishing cases within LC jurisdiction from those requiring formal handling), (ii) mediation capacity and resolution quality (ability to reach durable agreements without escalation), and (iii) referral appropriateness and coordination (serious cases being referred promptly and appropriately, while petty cases are resolved locally).
On heterogeneity, the evaluation will test whether impacts vary systematically across contexts and delivery models. This will include differences by baseline dispute volume and formal-system reliance, geographic accessibility and proximity to police/courts, and the, and the structure of ToT mentoring.
On safeguards and equity, the evaluation will examine whether diversion is achieved in ways that preserve procedural fairness and protect vulnerable groups. In particular, it will assess whether LC processes improve or worsen perceived legitimacy and fairness, and whether sensitive categories of disputes are handled in a gender-sensitive manner with appropriate referral to the formal system when required, rather than being inappropriately “absorbed” into local informal resolution.
The research centers on the Local Council Court (LCC) training “prevention pathway” (i.e., strengthening community-level dispute resolution so that petty cases are addressed locally and do not escalate into police or court caseloads). The intervention is implemented through two delivery partners with distinct geographic footprints, targeting strategies, and training modalities, which have direct implications for evaluation design (e.g., unit of assignment, exposure/spillovers, and sequencing).

Mediation/ADR practice and appropriate referral handling,
Record-keeping and case tracking (explicitly supported in CDFU’s phone-enabled ToT tracking model).

Also, the built-in ToT diffusion mechanisms mean the rollout plan must also consider contamination risks (trained LCCs mentoring untrained LCCs earlier than planned), reinforcing the need for explicit exposure tracking and process monitoring alongside impact measurement.
## Theory of change and hypotheses
PROBLEM STATEMENT: Uganda’s Justice system is characterized by excessive pre-trial detention, severe case backlogs, and the systematic denial of fair and timely hearings. Across the three core pillars of the justice system: policing; the judiciary; and prisons; authority and decision-making have become increasingly disconnected from the communities they are meant to serve. As a result, minor disputes and low-level offences that could be resolved through alternative or community-based mechanisms such as the Local Council Courts end up in the formal justice system. This over-reliance on formal justice processes for petty cases has led to widespread and unnecessary incarceration. Additionally, many remandees remain in detention for longer periods due to limited access to legal aid, delayed investigations, and prolonged court processes. This has led to chronic overcrowding, as evidenced by data from Uganda Prisions data, with remandees constituting the majority of the prison population. At the same time, incarcerated individuals are inadequately prepared for reintegration into their communities, increasing the risk of recidivism and perpetuating cycles of incarceration.
The evaluation is anchored in a tightened theory of change that isolates the Local Council prevention pathway as the primary mechanism for reducing formal justice congestion. The core premise is that a meaningful share of disputes and minor offences can be handled at the community level when local justice actors are sufficiently capable, confident, and perceived as legitimate, and when the boundary between “petty” and “serious” cases is applied consistently.
The intervention trains and supports Local Council Court leaders using an official manual, with post-training mentoring and diffusion through a Training-of-Trainers (ToT) model. This package is expected to increase LC leaders’ ability to (i) correctly classify and triage cases according to jurisdiction and severity, (ii) apply more effective mediation and settlement practices that prevent escalation and repeat disputes, and (iii) make appropriate referrals when a case requires formal handling (including sensitive cases such as SGBV and child protection matters).
If these intermediate changes occur, the immediate system-level implication is a reduction in the number (and potentially the share) of petty cases that are routed to policeor formal courts. This reduces demand pressure on the formal justice system, allowing scarce resources (police time, court slots, prosecutorial attention) to be reallocated toward more serious cases and reducing the downstream drivers of congestion. The prevention pathway therefore, targets congestion “upstream,” complementing downstream efforts that focus on accelerating case processing for individuals already inside the system.
A defining feature of the intervention architecture is that learning is not expected to remain confined to directly trained LCs. Both partners embed a ToT diffusion mechanism whereby trained leaders mentor other LCs, potentially expanding the intervention’s reach beyond assigned units. This diffusion is programmatically desirable (for scale and sustainability), but it also implies that impacts may arise through both direct effects (on trained LCs) and indirect/spillover effects (on nearby or network-linked LCs that receive mentoring or knowledge transfers).
Finally, the theory of change recognizes a critical enabling condition: diversion effects depend not only on LC capacity but also on whether community members view LC processes as procedurally fair, unbiased, and capable of producing enforceable outcomes. Where legitimacy is low (or where local institutions are perceived as captured, discriminatory, or unsafe) citizens may continue to route disputes to police/courts even if LCs are trained. For that reason, the evaluation will treat procedural fairness and safeguards as integral to the causal story rather than as peripheral considerations.

## Testable Hypotheses
The LC-centric theory of change motivates a set of testable hypotheses aligned with administrative outcomes, intermediate process measures, and perception-based indicators.
H1 (Caseload diversion): LCCs/villages assigned to receive LC training and structured support will experience a reduction in petty-case inflow to the formal justice system (police and/or courts), relative to a credible counterfactual. This is the primary hypothesis and corresponds directly to the prevention objective highlighted by Legatum.
H2 (Substitution / mechanism consistency): The decline in formal-system inflow will be accompanied by evidence of substitution toward local resolution, reflected in either (or both) (i) higher volumes of cases processed and resolved through LC mechanisms and/or (ii) improved referral quality—meaning that a greater share of cases routed upward are those that legitimately require formal handling, while petty matters are retained locally.
H3 (Procedural justice and legitimacy): Where citizen perceptions are measured, treated areas will exhibit improved perceived legitimacy, trust, and procedural fairness of LC processes, consistent with the view that improved performance and clearer procedures can shift forum-choice behavior toward LCs. This hypothesis is also directly connected to safeguard concerns: improvements must not come at the expense of fairness or protection of vulnerable groups.
H4 (System spillovers via ToT diffusion): Because the intervention includes ToT mentoring, improvements will not be confined to directly trained LCCs. Outcomes may improve in nearby or linked “non-treated” LCCs through knowledge diffusion, producing measurable spillover effects (e.g., reduced formal inflow or improved LC processing in those units as well). This hypothesis is especially relevant given the explicit ToT mentoring targets in both partner models and will inform both the evaluation design and interpretation of estimates.
# METHODOLOGY
## LC intervention design
This Scope of Work proposes an evaluation design that is fully aligned with Legatum’s request to prioritize the Local Council prevention pathway as the causal centerpiece. Because the intervention is delivered to groups of local justice actors operating within a defined community catchment, and because implementation is expected to be phased rather than simultaneous, the preferred approach is a cluster-based randomized design, implemented through a randomized phase-in (stepped rollout).
Under this approach, eligible Local Council Courts (LCCs) (or their village-level catchments) are randomly assigned to receive the training and structured support earlier versus later. This creates a clear counterfactual during the rollout window, while remaining operationally and ethically feasible because all sites ultimately receive the intervention once rollout is completed. The resulting design allows the evaluation to compare trends in key outcomes between LCCs that have already received the intervention and those scheduled to receive it later, while accounting for broad time trends.
This preferred design also matches the reality that both implementing partners already anticipate multi-year implementation and sequential training waves across districts. It therefore minimizes disruption to partner plans while still generating credible impact estimates.
## Key Design Challenges
The evaluation design must explicitly address two challenges that are central to the intervention’s architecture and to the credibility of causal estimates.
First, the Training-of-Trainers (ToT) diffusion component introduces a real risk of contamination between treated and untreated LCCs. This risk is especially salient where mentoring is driven by geographic proximity or informal networks, as appears likely under the CDFU model. If trained leaders provide mentoring or case-tracking support to leaders in LCCs that were assigned to receive training later, the contrast between treatment and control is reduced and simple comparisons will underestimate true program effects.
## Design Options and Feasibility
Option A: Randomized phase-in within each partner (recommended default).
The most straightforward option is to randomize the timing of training among eligible LCCs within each partner’s districts. LCCs would be assigned to early vs. later waves within the planned rollout calendar. This preserves partner autonomy and avoids the need to deny the intervention to any community. To strengthen credibility, assignment would be balanced within relevant administrative groupings (e.g., by district and, where possible, by parish-level clusters) so that early and later groups are comparable at baseline. This option also naturally supports a design in which the evaluation uses repeated administrative outcomes measured monthly or quarterly.
Option B: Cluster randomization with geographic buffers.
If ToT mentoring is expected to occur through proximity-based selection, one way to protect the counterfactual is to create “buffer zones” between treated and comparison LCCs during the evaluation window. Practically, this means avoiding the assignment of “early treatment” to an LCC that is adjacent to an LCC assigned to receive training later, or excluding highly exposed comparison LCCs from primary comparisons. This option can materially reduce contamination risk, but it may also reduce the number of usable comparison units, which can affect statistical precision. Because this is a Scope of Work, the key point is that buffers can be used as a practical safeguard against spillovers when diffusion is likely and difficult to monitor.
Option C: Diffusion-aware design elements (encouragement / saturation) if feasible.
If partners are willing and able to structure some aspects of mentoring—for example, by pre-specifying which trained LCCs are expected to mentor which additional LCCs, or by varying “mentoring intensity” intentionally—then the evaluation can move beyond simply treating spillovers as a threat and instead learn from them directly. This option would allow the study to estimate not only the direct impacts of training but also the added value (and cost-effectiveness) of diffusion mechanisms. This design element is more complex, and would only be recommended if it can be implemented without disrupting program delivery and without excessive tracking burdens.
In all cases, the final design decision will depend on: (i) the finalized LCC sampling frame and training schedule by district, (ii) the practical ability to monitor mentoring and exposure, and (iii) Legatum’s tolerance for design complexity relative to timeline and budget.

# COST-EFFECTIVENESS ANALYSIS (CEA)
The evaluation will include a structured cost-effectiveness analysis to quantify the resources required to achieve measurable reductions in petty cases entering the formal justice system. The CEA will be designed to support two complementary learning goals. First, it will generate partner-specific cost-effectiveness estimates to compare the performance of the two delivery models (CDFU and FCU) under realistic implementation conditions. Second, where intervention effects are sufficiently comparable, it will generate pooled estimates that summarize the overall cost-effectiveness of the LC training approach as a scalable prevention model. The intent is not only to rank partners, but to identify which delivery features drive value for money and what cost structure would look like under scale-up.
## Costing Method
The CEA will rely on an ingredients-based costing approach, which is transparent, auditable, and well-suited for multi-partner programs with heterogeneous delivery structures. For each partner, the evaluation team will compile a standardized cost inventory capturing the full set of resources required to deliver training and post-training support, including staff time (trainers, supervisors, coordination), transport and per diems, training materials, venue-related costs where applicable, communications and monitoring tools (including any ToT-related tracking support), and a clearly documented approach to allocating shared program management and overhead. Costs will be classified into fixed components (e.g., development/adaptation of materials, initial training setup) and variable components (e.g., per-LCC training delivery, travel, mentoring intensity), since this distinction is essential for interpreting cost-effectiveness under different scale scenarios.
## Effect Metrics
The primary effectiveness metric will be the cost per petty case diverted from the formal system, defined as the estimated reduction in petty-case entries into police/court registries attributable to the intervention relative to the counterfactual, standardized to the relevant time window. A closely related secondary metric will be the cost per formal filing avoided, which is useful when registry systems record filings more consistently than case categories or dispositions. Where data permit, additional derived metrics can be reported to support operational decision-making (for example, costs per month of reduced formal inflow, or costs per percentage-point reduction in formal entry rates), but the Scope of Work will keep the headline indicators focused and interpretable.
Because the program includes structured mentoring and diffusion components, the evaluation will also seek to incorporate spillover-adjusted cost-effectiveness estimates if spillovers can be measured credibly. Conceptually, this means that if trained LCCs improve outcomes not only in their own catchments but also in nearby or mentored LCCs, the relevant “effect denominator” expands and the implied cost-effectiveness improves. Whether and how this adjustment is implemented will depend on the feasibility of constructing a defensible exposure measure in the administrative data.
## Sensitivity Analyses
Given uncertainty in both costs and measured effects (especially in administrative-data environments) the CEA will include a set of pre-specified sensitivity analyses. On the costing side, this will test alternative assumptions about overhead allocation, the classification of costs as fixed versus variable, and alternative costing of staff time where opportunity costs differ from financial expenditures. On the effectiveness side, sensitivity checks will examine alternative definitions of the outcome (e.g., narrower vs broader “petty” categorizations where classification is imperfect), alternative aggregation windows (monthly versus quarterly), and alternative time horizons that reflect realistic diffusion and learning dynamics after training. Where the design supports it, cost-effectiveness will be reported both using the primary intention-to-treat effect and using compliance-adjusted estimates, with clear labeling so Legatum and partners can interpret the results appropriately for budgeting and scale decisions.
# DATA AND MEASUREMENT STRATEGY
Quantitative data will be collected through structured beneficiary and institutional surveys using SurveyCTO. Qualitative methods will include Focus Group Discussions, Key Informant Interviews, and in-depth case studies to explore mechanisms, perceptions, and contextual factors.
## Primary and Secondary Outcomes
The evaluation will be anchored in administrative indicators that directly capture whether petty cases are being diverted away from the formal justice system. The primary measurement objective is to track, at regular intervals, the flow of petty matters entering police and/or courts from each LCC catchment area. This will rely on three complementary administrative sources. Police registers and court registry records are the preferred sources for measuring formal system entry, while LC registers provide the corresponding local-system activity needed to interpret substitution patterns and to support triangulation. Because the central theory of change is prevention of formal entry, the evaluation will prioritize constructing a consistent and auditable measure of petty-case inflow across these sources, with operational definitions and coding rules finalized in the technical proposal.
To interpret how and why any reduction in formal entry occurs, the evaluation will also track a small set of secondary outcomes focused on LCC functioning and safeguards. These include measures of LC throughput (e.g., number of cases recorded/handled, resolution markers where available, and time-to-resolution proxies if registers capture dates), indicators of referral quality (e.g., whether serious matters are escalated appropriately and petty matters retained locally), and feasibility-dependent indicators of equity and safeguards. The safeguard focus is not to expand the evaluation beyond scope, but to ensure that diversion does not come at the cost of inappropriate informal handling of sensitive cases—particularly cases involving women, children, or SGBV—where referral to formal institutions or specialized services is required.
## Pre-Period and Supplementary data
A key strength of the proposed evaluation is the ability to construct a historical baseline using pre-intervention register data. Where access permits, the study will extract relevant outcomes from 2024/25 administrative records (and earlier periods if reliable), allowing the evaluation to establish pre-trends, improve statistical precision in a phase-in design, and support early feasibility checks before full rollout. This pre-period compilation will be treated as a core feasibility milestone because the quality and completeness of historical register data will shape both the final design choice and the power of the analysis.
While administrative data will remain the backbone of the evaluation, a limited amount of primary data collection may be included to measure mechanisms that registers cannot capture reliably—especially perceived legitimacy, procedural fairness, and basic markers of institutional practice. This would be deliberately light-touch and targeted, using either short community/user modules or focused qualitative work (KIIs/FGDs) to triangulate administrative trends and identify implementation risks such as elite capture, perceived bias, or inappropriate dispute processing. In addition, short institutional tools with LC leaders (such as knowledge checks and case vignettes) can be used to assess whether the training is changing triage decisions in the intended direction. Whether these components are included, and at what scale, will be finalized in the technical proposal based on budget ceilings and partner logistics.
# DATA QUALITY ASSURANCE, ETHICS AND SAFEGUARDING
## Data Quality Protocols
Because the evaluation is anchored in administrative registers, data quality assurance will focus on (i) standardizing extraction, (ii) auditing completeness and consistency, and (iii) maintaining a clear chain of custody from source registers to cleaned analytical datasets. The evaluation team will develop structured abstraction templates and a uniform coding protocol to ensure that the same fields are captured in the same way across LCCs, police posts/stations, and courts. Field teams (or partner-designated data staff) will be trained on how to locate and interpret registers, how to record uncertain entries, and how to flag missing or inconsistent documentation. Routine audits will include re-abstraction of a random subset of registers, consistency checks across time periods, and basic reconciliation exercises across sources where linkable (e.g., verifying that escalated cases appear in police/court records when such linkage is feasible).
If any supplementary primary data are included (short surveys, vignettes, KIIs/FGDs), the evaluation will apply standard survey QA protocols, including enumerator training, piloting, programmed checks in data capture tools, and a defined plan for back-checks and spot checks. These procedures are not intended to expand field burden, but to ensure that any primary data collected are credible and usable for triangulation and mechanism interpretation.
## Ethics
The ethical approach will be proportional to the evaluation’s data collection modalities and risk profile. Where the evaluation relies exclusively on administrative records that do not include direct identifiers in the analytic dataset, the primary ethical requirements center on confidentiality, data minimization, and secure storage. The evaluation will implement clear procedures for secure transfer and storage of data, restricted access protocols, and de-identification rules prior to analysis. If identifiers are required temporarily for cleaning or linkage, they will be stored separately with controlled access and removed from analysis files as early as feasible.
Where direct interaction with individuals occurs (e.g., surveys, interviews, group discussions, or vignette-based assessments), informed consent procedures will be used, along with clear explanations of voluntary participation, non-retaliation, and the absence of any effect on access to services. The evaluation will also include a structured plan for managing sensitive information, including guidance to enumerators and moderators on minimizing harm and ensuring privacy during data collection.
The specific IRB/ethics review pathway will be confirmed during the technical proposal phase and will reflect Legatum’s requirements, partner institutional processes, and the regulatory environment in Uganda. The Scope of Work anticipates that approvals may be pursued through an appropriate Ugandan ethics body and/or a partner-linked pathway where relevant, with the objective of starting the process early to avoid timeline bottlenecks.
## Safeguarding for Sensitive Cases
Although the causal evaluation focus is on preventing petty cases from entering the formal system, the program operates in a legal pluralism environment where informal or semi-formal institutions sometimes encounter cases that should not be diverted—particularly sexual and gender-based violence (SGBV), child protection matters, and other serious offenses requiring formal referral and specialized services. The evaluation will therefore include safeguarding provisions to ensure that measurement and program learning do not unintentionally incentivize inappropriate diversion.
At minimum, we will require partners to maintain and apply clear referral protocols consistent with Ugandan law and safeguarding standards, including documentation of referral pathways to police, courts, probation/child protection authorities, and relevant service providers. Where feasible, the evaluation will include limited monitoring indicators—drawn from registers or process logs—to verify that safeguarding rules are understood and applied in practice. This safeguarding layer is essential both for ethical integrity and for correct interpretation of results: a measured reduction in formal entry is only meaningful if it reflects appropriate diversion of petty matters rather than misclassification or suppression of serious cases.
