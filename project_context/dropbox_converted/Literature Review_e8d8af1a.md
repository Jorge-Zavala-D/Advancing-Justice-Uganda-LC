Literature Review: Impact Evaluation of a Local Council Justice System Decongestion Program in Uganda
A Research-Informed Synthesis for Scope of Work Development

1. Introduction and Framing
This literature review synthesizes evidence relevant to the design and implementation of a rigorous impact evaluation of a Local Council (LC) training intervention in Uganda, intended to strengthen community-level dispute resolution and divert petty cases from the formal judicial system. The review addresses three overarching goals: (a) identifying the strongest empirical evidence on community-based dispute resolution and diversion interventions; (b) articulating theoretical mechanisms through which such interventions operate; and (c) providing methodological guidance for causal evaluation in settings characterized by spillovers, phased rollout, and administrative data as primary outcomes.
The Ugandan context is particularly instructive. Local Council Courts (LCCs) operate at the village (LC1), parish (LC2), and sub-county (LC3) levels, and have jurisdiction over minor criminal cases, civil disputes related to land held under customary tenure, family disputes, and inheritance disputes (Macdonald et al., 2022; . These courts reflect community cultural norms, inclining toward mediation rather than adjudication, and their understandings of crime, liability, and appropriate outcomes are shaped by local social structures (Macdonald et al., 2022; . Research in post-war northern Uganda has documented the diverse reasons citizens engage with both formal and informal justice institutions, including normative, pragmatic, and tactical motivations that are not well captured by conventional procedural justice frameworks (Macdonald et al., 2022; . This complexity underscores the need for a theoretically grounded and methodologically rigorous evaluation design.

2. Empirical Evidence on Community-Based Dispute Resolution and Diversion
2.1 Community Justice Actors and Formal System Diversion
The evidence base on the causal impact of training local community justice actors—including local councils, customary courts, village courts, and community mediators—on rates of disputes entering the formal justice system remains relatively thin in terms of experimental studies, but a growing body of qualitative and quasi-experimental work provides important insights.
In Uganda specifically, LCCs are a hybridized system of dispute resolution administered by elected local councillors at the village and parish level, drawing on both customary authority and formal law Cooper‐Knock & Macdonald, 2020). Research by Macdonald et al. (Macdonald et al., 2022; documents that citizens in post-war northern Uganda engage with lower state courts for a complex mix of reasons, and that court-administered mediation (ADR) is theoretically mandatory before civil land cases can proceed. However, the same research finds that mediation is often understood as a local process shaped by communal interactions, and that citizens sometimes resist it in favor of formal adjudication when they perceive local authorities as partial or corrupt (Macdonald et al., 2022; . This finding is critical for the proposed evaluation: it suggests that the effectiveness of LC training in diverting cases will depend heavily on perceived legitimacy and procedural fairness of the LC process.
Cooper-Knock and Macdonald Cooper‐Knock & Macdonald, 2020) provide a systematic literature review and multi-sited qualitative study of citizen engagement with lower state courts in South Africa and Uganda, critiquing the procedural justice model underlying much access-to-justice programming. They propose a "trifactor framework" in which citizen engagement occurs as people reconcile how they think courts should act, how they expect them to act, and how they need them to act in any given instance Cooper‐Knock & Macdonald, 2020). This framework has direct implications for the proposed evaluation: it suggests that outcome measures should capture not only case diversion rates but also citizen perceptions of LC legitimacy and procedural fairness.
In East Africa more broadly, Rockowitz et al. Rockowitz et al., 2023) conducted a systematic review of criminal justice initiatives to strengthen investigation and prosecution of sexual violence, identifying seven types of initiatives including community interventions, multisectoral referral networks, and specialized units. Their review found that barriers to success include lack of trained personnel, weak medico-legal partnerships, and stigma and impunity restricting uptake Rockowitz et al., 2023). Critically, they found that "limited systematic evidence on the effectiveness and adaptability of initiatives exists," underscoring the need for the kind of rigorous evaluation proposed here Rockowitz et al., 2023).
2.2 Alternative Dispute Resolution in Low- and Middle-Income Countries
The evidence on ADR programs in LMICs and Sub-Saharan Africa is mixed. On the positive side, community mediation and ADR programs have been associated with reduced formal caseloads, faster dispute resolution, and improved access to justice in some contexts. However, the evidence base is characterized by significant methodological limitations, including selection bias, lack of control groups, and reliance on self-reported outcomes.
In Uganda, research documents that people often avoid state justice, preferring to resolve issues through family meetings or local courts, particularly for land disputes and gender-based violence (Macdonald et al., 2022; . This preference for informal resolution is not simply a matter of access or cost—it reflects complex assessments of legitimacy, social relationships, and expected outcomes (Macdonald et al., 2022; . The implication for the proposed evaluation is that the intervention may work through multiple pathways: improving the quality of LC dispute resolution (so that more cases are resolved satisfactorily at the community level), increasing citizen awareness of LC jurisdiction (so that more cases are brought to LCs rather than police or courts), and improving coordination between LCs and formal institutions (so that appropriate referrals are made).
Albezreh et al. Albezreh et al. (2022) evaluated a multiyear, multisectoral training program in Kenya for health, legal, and law enforcement professionals on survivor-centered sexual and gender-based violence (SGBV) care. Their evaluation found that mean knowledge scores increased significantly (from 75.6% to 84.7%, p < .001), and qualitative interviews revealed improved survivor confidentiality, increased specialized hospital care, more comprehensive forensic care, and greater cross-sector collaboration Albezreh et al. (2022). Participants reported that survivors felt more comfortable pursuing legal action and that perpetrator convictions increased Albezreh et al. (2022). While this study focuses on SGBV rather than general dispute resolution, it provides important evidence that multisectoral training can improve justice outcomes in East African contexts.
2.3 Conditions Under Which Community Justice Interventions Fail
The literature identifies several conditions under which community justice interventions fail or generate adverse effects. These include:
Elite capture and corruption: Research in Uganda and across Africa documents that local justice institutions are vulnerable to capture by local elites, who may use their positions to favor their own interests or those of their social networks (Macdonald et al., 2022; . Cooper-Knock and Macdonald Cooper‐Knock & Macdonald, 2020) note that there is "no romanticized binary between a noncorrupt 'traditional' or 'local' system and a corrupt formal state system," and that the exchange of money is a pervasive feature of both formal and informal justice in Uganda and South Africa.
Bias against vulnerable groups: Research on gender-based violence in Uganda and across Africa documents that informal justice institutions often fail women and other vulnerable groups (Macdonald et al., 2022; . Macdonald et al. (Macdonald et al., 2022; note that women who reported sexual violence viewed the formal system as "corrupt, inaccessible, and often hostile," but also that informal systems often failed to provide accountability or deterrence. The proposed evaluation must therefore include gender-disaggregated outcome measures and qualitative assessment of differential impacts on vulnerable groups.
Reduced due process: Informal justice institutions may lack procedural safeguards that protect the rights of accused persons, including the right to legal representation, the right to appeal, and protection against self-incrimination Cooper‐Knock & Macdonald, 2020). The proposed evaluation should assess whether LC training improves or worsens procedural fairness for both complainants and respondents.
Discriminatory outcomes: Research across Africa documents that customary justice institutions often reproduce and reinforce existing social hierarchies and discriminatory norms, particularly with respect to gender, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status (Macdonald et al., 2022; . The proposed evaluation should include measures of outcome equity across demographic groups.

3. Theoretical Mechanisms
3.1 How LC Training Changes Outcomes
The theoretical literature suggests several mechanisms through which LC training could change dispute resolution outcomes:
Improved legal knowledge and case classification: Training LC leaders on the official training manual should improve their ability to distinguish between petty disputes (within LC jurisdiction) and criminal matters (requiring referral to police or courts). This improved classification should reduce inappropriate referrals to the formal system and improve the quality of LC dispute resolution. Evidence from the Kenya SGBV training program Albezreh et al. (2022) supports this mechanism: knowledge scores increased significantly following training, and participants reported improved case processing.
Changes in mediation skills and bargaining power: Training in mediation techniques should improve LC leaders' ability to facilitate negotiated settlements, reducing the need for formal adjudication. The literature on ADR in LMICs suggests that mediation is most effective when mediators have strong facilitation skills, are perceived as neutral, and have sufficient authority to enforce agreements (Macdonald et al., 2022; .
Norm shifts about reporting and escalation: Training may shift community norms about which disputes are appropriate for LC resolution versus formal reporting. Research in Uganda documents that citizens make complex assessments of the relative merits of different justice forums (Macdonald et al., 2022; , and that these assessments are shaped by social norms as well as individual calculations. Training that improves LC performance may shift norms in favor of LC resolution.
Increased perceived procedural justice and legitimacy: Research by Cooper-Knock and Macdonald Cooper‐Knock & Macdonald, 2020) suggests that citizen engagement with justice institutions is shaped by perceptions of how those institutions should act, how they are expected to act, and how they need to act. Training that improves LC performance and procedural fairness should increase perceived legitimacy, encouraging more citizens to bring disputes to LCs rather than police or courts.
Coordination with police and courts: Training that includes guidance on appropriate referrals should improve coordination between LCs and formal institutions, ensuring that serious cases are referred appropriately and that LC decisions are respected by formal institutions.
3.2 Legal Pluralism and State Capacity
The effectiveness of LC training will be shaped by the broader context of legal pluralism and state capacity in Uganda. Research across Africa documents that citizens navigate multiple justice forums—including family meetings, religious institutions, customary courts, and formal courts—making strategic choices based on their assessment of the likely outcomes in each forum (Macdonald et al., 2022; . The proposed evaluation must account for this forum-shopping behavior, as it means that changes in LC performance may affect not only the rate of cases entering the formal system but also the distribution of cases across informal forums.
State capacity is also a critical contextual factor. Research in post-war northern Uganda documents that the formal justice system is characterized by high levels of corruption and inefficiency (Macdonald et al., 2022; , and that citizens often turn to lower state courts for pragmatic reasons rather than because they believe in their legitimacy. In this context, LC training may be most effective in communities where the formal system is least accessible or most dysfunctional, as citizens in these communities have the strongest incentive to use LC dispute resolution.

4. Risks Related to Procedural Fairness, Gender, and Marginalized Groups
4.1 Gender and Child Protection Risks
The literature documents significant risks of gender bias in informal justice institutions across Africa. Research in Uganda finds that women who report sexual violence face a range of imperfect options, including family meetings, religious forums, and customary courts, and that the criminal justice system is often believed to be most likely to apprehend, punish, and deter offenders (Macdonald et al., 2022; . This suggests that LC training must include specific content on gender-sensitive dispute resolution and appropriate referral of SGBV cases to formal institutions.
Rockowitz et al. Rockowitz et al., 2023) document that barriers to effective SGBV response in East Africa include stigma and impunity restricting uptake and fair distribution of services, and that specialized units have increased reporting of rape but have not necessarily led to increased convictions due to barriers in the legal system. The proposed evaluation should include specific outcome measures for SGBV cases, including rates of appropriate referral to formal institutions and rates of victim satisfaction.
Albezreh et al. Albezreh et al. (2022) found that multisectoral training in Kenya improved survivor confidentiality and increased specialized hospital care, suggesting that training can improve gender-sensitive responses. However, their evaluation did not include a control group, limiting causal inference.
4.2 Design Features to Mitigate Risks
The literature suggests several design features that can mitigate risks of bias and discrimination in informal justice settings:
Gender-balanced LC composition: Ensuring that LC courts include women members can improve gender sensitivity and reduce bias against women complainants.
Clear referral protocols: Training that includes clear protocols for referring SGBV and child protection cases to formal institutions can reduce the risk of inappropriate informal resolution of serious cases.
Monitoring and accountability mechanisms: Regular monitoring of LC case registers and community feedback mechanisms can identify patterns of bias or discrimination and trigger corrective action.
Linkages with civil society organizations: Partnerships with women's rights organizations and other civil society groups can provide additional accountability and support for vulnerable groups.

5. Training-of-Trainers Models and Diffusion Effects
5.1 Evidence on ToT Effectiveness
The Training-of-Trainers (ToT) model is widely used in governance and justice interventions, but the evidence on its effectiveness is mixed. The core concern is that ToT models may dilute training quality as knowledge and skills are transmitted through multiple layers of trainers, with each layer potentially introducing errors or omissions.
Albezreh et al. Albezreh et al. (2022) describe a multiyear training program in Kenya that included a ToT component, with training participants returning to their police precincts and training their colleagues following the workshops. Participants reported accompanying colleagues into the field to collect evidence from crime scenes, modeling how to work with traumatized survivors, and reinforcing chain-of-custody procedures Albezreh et al. (2022). This suggests that ToT can generate effective diffusion of skills in justice settings, at least in the short term.
Takahashi and Otsuka (Takahashi & Otsuka, 2023) review evidence on extension services in Sub-Saharan Africa, including ToT models, and find that "most studies show positive and significant impacts of extension on rice intensification, income, and profits, particularly when training includes management-intensive cultivation practices" (Takahashi & Otsuka, 2023). However, they also note that "studies offering credible estimates of impacts are still scarce and are at best limited to short-term evaluations" (Takahashi & Otsuka, 2023). Critically, they argue that "if spillover effects are not properly measured, we will underestimate the cost-effectiveness of the training program, leading to under-investment in profitable opportunities" (Takahashi & Otsuka, 2023).
The same review documents evidence from Uganda that "the adoption of new technologies by non-participants in cultivation training gradually increased," suggesting that ToT can generate sustained diffusion effects (Takahashi & Otsuka, 2023). However, the review also notes that "identifying the true training impacts and spillover effects is quite complex under the existence of information spillover because it violates the SUTVA" (Takahashi & Otsuka, 2023).
5.2 Diffusion and Peer Effects in Community Institutional Training
The proposed evaluation includes a ToT diffusion component in which trained LC leaders mentor/train leaders in other villages, creating potential spillovers and contamination. This design feature has important implications for both the evaluation design and the interpretation of results.
Benjamin-Chung et al. (Benjamin‐Chung et al., 2017; provide a comprehensive review of spillover effects in epidemiology, noting that "many public health interventions provide benefits that extend beyond their direct recipients and impact people in close physical or social proximity who did not directly receive the intervention themselves" (Benjamin‐Chung et al., 2017; . They classify spillover parameters into several categories, including cluster-level spillovers and spillovers conditional on treatment density, distance, or the number of treated social network links (Benjamin‐Chung et al., 2017; .
Critically, Benjamin-Chung et al. (Benjamin‐Chung et al., 2017; note that "SUTVA does not hold when spillovers are present because an individual's potential outcome depends on their own treatment assignment and the treatment assignment of other individuals connected to them." They recommend the "partial interference" assumption as the most common and theoretically tractable method for making causal inferences about spillover effects, which "states that there are no spillovers between clusters of individuals but allows for spillovers among individuals within the same cluster" (Benjamin‐Chung et al., 2017; .
For the proposed evaluation, the ToT diffusion component means that the partial interference assumption may be violated if trained LC leaders from treatment villages train leaders in control villages. This is a critical design challenge that must be addressed through careful randomization and monitoring strategies.

6. Methodological Guidance for Causal Evaluation
6.1 Cluster Randomized Trials in Community/Governance Settings
Cluster randomized trials (CRTs) are the gold standard for evaluating community-level interventions, and are particularly appropriate for the proposed evaluation given that the intervention is delivered at the village/LC catchment level (Benjamin‐Chung et al., 2017; . The key design considerations for CRTs in community/governance settings include:
Unit of randomization: The proposed evaluation will randomize at the village/LC catchment or parish level. The choice of randomization unit should be guided by the level at which the intervention is delivered and the level at which spillovers are most likely to occur (Benjamin‐Chung et al., 2017; . Randomizing at a higher level (parish) reduces the risk of spillovers between treatment and control units but reduces statistical power.
Sample size and power: CRTs require larger sample sizes than individually randomized trials to achieve the same statistical power, due to the intracluster correlation (ICC) of outcomes within clusters (Ariel et al., 2018). The ICC for justice outcomes in Uganda is unknown and should be estimated from baseline data or prior studies. Ariel et al. (Ariel et al., 2018) note that "the required statistical power calculations resemble analyses in the context of place-based rather than subject-based trials—that is, cluster-randomized trials."
Baseline data collection: Baseline data collection is essential for CRTs, both to verify balance between treatment and control clusters and to improve statistical power through covariate adjustment (Ariel et al., 2018). For the proposed evaluation, baseline data should be collected from LC case registers, police registers, and court registry records.
Stepped-wedge design: The phased rollout of the intervention makes a stepped-wedge or randomized phase-in design particularly appropriate. In a stepped-wedge design, all clusters eventually receive the intervention, but the timing of rollout is randomized (Benjamin‐Chung et al., 2017; . This design has several advantages: it is ethically acceptable (all clusters eventually receive the intervention), it allows for within-cluster comparisons over time, and it can improve statistical power relative to a parallel-arm design.
6.2 Stepped-Wedge and Randomized Phase-In Designs
The stepped-wedge design is increasingly used in health and governance research, and is particularly appropriate for the proposed evaluation given the phased rollout of the intervention. Key considerations include:
Estimands: The stepped-wedge design allows estimation of both the average treatment effect (ATE) and the treatment effect as a function of time since rollout. The latter is important for understanding whether the intervention has sustained effects or whether effects decay over time.
Secular trends: The stepped-wedge design is vulnerable to confounding by secular trends (changes in outcomes over time that are unrelated to the intervention). This can be addressed by including time fixed effects in the analysis model.
Carryover effects: If the intervention has carryover effects (i.e., if the effects of training persist after the training period), the stepped-wedge design may overestimate the treatment effect for later clusters. This can be addressed by including a washout period between the training and measurement periods.
Partial interference: As noted above, the ToT diffusion component of the proposed intervention creates the potential for spillovers between treatment and control clusters. The stepped-wedge design can help address this by ensuring that all clusters eventually receive the intervention, reducing the incentive for trained LC leaders to train leaders in control clusters.
6.3 Causal Inference Under Interference and SUTVA Violations
The ToT diffusion component of the proposed intervention creates a significant challenge for causal inference: the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA) may be violated if trained LC leaders from treatment villages train leaders in control villages. This is a well-recognized challenge in the evaluation of community-level interventions (Benjamin‐Chung et al., 2017; .
Benjamin-Chung et al. (Benjamin‐Chung et al., 2017; provide a comprehensive framework for estimating spillover effects in the presence of SUTVA violations. They recommend the partial interference assumption as the most tractable approach, which "states that there are no spillovers between clusters of individuals but allows for spillovers among individuals within the same cluster" (Benjamin‐Chung et al., 2017; . Under this assumption, two-stage randomized designs can identify both direct effects (on treated clusters) and indirect/spillover effects (on control clusters near treated clusters) (Benjamin‐Chung et al., 2017; .
Baird et al. Baird et al., 2018) formalize the optimal design of experiments in the presence of interference, focusing on "randomized saturation designs" that first randomize treatment saturation of a group, then individual treatment assignment. They show that "the power to detect average treatment effects declines precisely with the ability to identify novel treatment and spillover effects" Baird et al., 2018), highlighting the fundamental trade-off between power and the ability to identify spillover effects.
Aronow and Samii Aronow & Samii, 2017; present a randomization-based framework for estimating causal effects under interference between units, motivated by challenges in analyzing experiments on social networks. Their framework integrates an experimental design, a mapping from treatment assignments to exposures received by units, and estimands that answer questions of substantive interest Aronow & Samii, 2017; . They develop inverse probability weighting (IPW) estimators for average unit-level causal effects under interference of arbitrary but known form Aronow & Samii, 2017; .
For the proposed evaluation, the key challenge is that the form of interference (i.e., the network through which trained LC leaders diffuse knowledge to other villages) is not known in advance. Several approaches can address this challenge:
Geographic buffers: One approach is to exclude clusters within a specified geographic distance of treatment clusters from the control group, creating a buffer zone that reduces the risk of spillovers (Benjamin‐Chung et al., 2017; . This approach is simple and transparent but reduces the effective sample size.
Exposure mapping: Aronow and Samii Aronow & Samii, 2017; propose an "exposure mapping" approach in which the treatment assignment of each unit is mapped to an "exposure" that captures the relevant features of the treatment environment, including the treatment assignments of neighboring units. This approach can accommodate complex interference structures but requires knowledge of the network through which interference operates.
Two-stage randomization: Baird et al. Baird et al., 2018) propose a two-stage randomization design in which clusters are first randomized to treatment saturation levels, and then individuals within clusters are randomized to treatment. This design can identify both direct and spillover effects under the partial interference assumption Baird et al., 2018).
Regression adjustment for interference: Chin Chin (2019) proposes regression adjustment estimators for removing bias due to interference in Bernoulli randomized experiments, using a fitted model to predict counterfactual outcomes of global control and global treatment. This approach "turns the problem of detecting interference into a feature engineering problem" Chin (2019) and can accommodate flexible machine learning estimators.
6.4 Recommended Estimands and Reporting Practices
The literature recommends several estimands for cluster trials with contamination:
Intention-to-treat (ITT) effect: The ITT effect compares outcomes in clusters randomized to treatment versus control, regardless of actual treatment receipt. This is the most conservative estimand and is robust to non-compliance and spillovers Baird et al., 2018).
Treatment-on-the-treated (ToT) effect: The ToT effect compares outcomes for clusters that actually received the intervention versus those that did not. This estimand requires an instrument for treatment receipt (e.g., randomization assignment) and is more sensitive to non-compliance Baird et al., 2018).
Direct effects: The direct effect of the intervention on treated clusters, holding the treatment status of other clusters constant Aronow & Samii, 2017; . This estimand is most relevant for assessing the impact of the intervention on the clusters that receive it.
Indirect/spillover effects: The effect of the intervention on control clusters near treated clusters, due to diffusion of training through the ToT component (Benjamin‐Chung et al., 2017; . This estimand is most relevant for assessing the broader impact of the intervention on the justice system.
Total effects: The sum of direct and indirect effects, capturing the overall impact of the intervention on the justice system Aronow & Samii, 2017; .
Halloran and Hudgens (Halloran & Hudgens, 2016) provide a comprehensive review of methods for estimating direct, indirect, total, and overall effects in the presence of interference, noting that "future studies to evaluate causal effects under interference may continue to rely on cluster-randomization with the accompanying assumption of partial interference" (Halloran & Hudgens, 2016).
6.5 Administrative Data as Primary Outcomes
The proposed evaluation will use administrative registers (LC case registers, police registers, court registry records) as primary outcome measures. This approach has several advantages: administrative data are typically available for all clusters (not just a sample), they are not subject to self-report bias, and they can capture outcomes over long time periods. However, administrative data also have significant limitations:
Measurement error: Administrative registers may be incomplete or inaccurate, particularly in low-resource settings. LC case registers may not be systematically maintained, and police and court registers may not capture all cases that enter the formal system.
Reporting bias: Changes in LC training may affect not only the rate of cases entering the formal system but also the rate of cases recorded in administrative registers. For example, if training improves LC record-keeping, the number of cases recorded in LC registers may increase even if the total number of disputes does not change.
Selection bias: Administrative registers capture only cases that are formally reported or recorded, not all disputes that occur in the community. Changes in LC training may affect the rate of formal reporting without affecting the underlying rate of disputes.
Validation strategies: To address these limitations, the proposed evaluation should include validation studies that compare administrative data with community surveys and qualitative data. This will allow assessment of the completeness and accuracy of administrative registers and identification of systematic biases.
Ariel et al. (Ariel et al., 2018) provide an example of a justice/governance RCT that uses administrative data (official records of assaults) as primary outcomes, finding a 47% significant overall reduction in the odds of assaults against BWC-equipped staff. Their study also found a 26% significant reduction in assaults against all employees in treatment versus control station complexes, suggesting a "spatial diffusion of benefits" effect (Ariel et al., 2018). This study illustrates both the potential of administrative data for justice RCTs and the importance of accounting for spillover effects.

7. Cost-Effectiveness Analysis
7.1 Prior Studies on Cost-Effectiveness in Justice and Governance Interventions
The literature on cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) for justice, policing, ADR, and governance interventions is relatively sparse, particularly in LMIC contexts. However, several studies provide useful methodological guidance.
Ariel et al. (Ariel et al., 2018) estimate that body-worn cameras (BWCs) can reduce at least 3,000 working days per year lost because of physical violence at work, providing a basis for cost-effectiveness calculations. Their study illustrates the importance of capturing both direct effects (on BWC-equipped staff) and indirect effects (on all employees in treatment station complexes) in CEA (Ariel et al., 2018).
Albezreh et al. Albezreh et al. (2022) describe a multiyear training program in Kenya but do not provide a formal CEA. However, their evaluation provides data on training costs (number of trainings, duration, number of participants) that could be used to calculate cost per trained professional and cost per unit of outcome improvement.
7.2 Recommended Costing Methods
The literature recommends several costing methods for multi-partner interventions:
Ingredients-based costing: This approach identifies all inputs required to deliver the intervention (personnel, materials, facilities, etc.) and assigns costs to each input based on market prices or opportunity costs. This approach is transparent and reproducible, and allows for sensitivity analysis on key cost assumptions.
Program cost allocation: For multi-partner interventions, costs must be allocated across partners and activities. This requires clear documentation of each partner's activities and costs, and a principled approach to allocating shared costs (e.g., overhead, management).
Fixed vs. variable costs: Fixed costs (e.g., training materials, curriculum development) are incurred once and do not vary with the scale of the intervention. Variable costs (e.g., trainer salaries, travel) vary with the scale of the intervention. Distinguishing between fixed and variable costs is important for estimating the cost-effectiveness of scaling up the intervention.
Incorporating uncertainty: CEA should incorporate uncertainty in both costs and effects, using sensitivity analysis and probabilistic methods (e.g., Monte Carlo simulation) to characterize the range of plausible cost-effectiveness estimates.
Key cost-effectiveness metrics: For the proposed evaluation, relevant cost-effectiveness metrics include:
Cost per case diverted from the formal system
Cost per court case avoided
Cost per prison-day avoided
Broader social cost reductions (time, money, conflict)
Takahashi and Otsuka (Takahashi & Otsuka, 2023) note that "if spillover effects are not properly measured, we will underestimate the cost-effectiveness of the training program, leading to under-investment in profitable opportunities" (Takahashi & Otsuka, 2023). This is a critical point for the proposed evaluation: if the ToT diffusion component generates significant spillover effects, the cost-effectiveness of the intervention will be substantially higher than estimates based on direct effects alone.

8. Synthesis and Implications for Evaluation Design
8.1 Key Design Recommendations
Drawing on the literature reviewed above, the following design recommendations are proposed for the impact evaluation:
Randomization unit: Randomize at the village/LC catchment level, with parishes as the unit of stratification. This will maximize statistical power while maintaining the plausibility of the partial interference assumption.
Stepped-wedge design: Adopt a stepped-wedge or randomized phase-in design to accommodate the phased rollout of the intervention. This design is ethically acceptable (all clusters eventually receive the intervention) and allows for within-cluster comparisons over time.
Geographic buffers: Exclude clusters within a specified geographic distance of treatment clusters from the control group, creating a buffer zone that reduces the risk of spillovers from the ToT diffusion component.
Two-stage randomization: Consider a two-stage randomization design in which clusters are first randomized to treatment saturation levels, and then LC leaders within clusters are randomized to receive training. This design can identify both direct and spillover effects under the partial interference assumption Baird et al., 2018).
Exposure mapping: Develop an exposure mapping that captures the relevant features of the treatment environment for each cluster, including the treatment assignments of neighboring clusters and the network of ToT diffusion. This will allow estimation of spillover effects using IPW estimators Aronow & Samii, 2017; .
Administrative data validation: Conduct validation studies that compare administrative data with community surveys and qualitative data, to assess the completeness and accuracy of administrative registers.
Gender-disaggregated outcomes: Include gender-disaggregated outcome measures and qualitative assessment of differential impacts on vulnerable groups, including women, children, and ethnic minorities.
Ingredients-based CEA: Conduct an ingredients-based CEA that captures all costs of the intervention (including partner costs, training costs, and overhead) and calculates cost-effectiveness metrics including cost per case diverted, cost per court case avoided, and cost per prison-day avoided.
8.2 Key Measurement Recommendations
Primary outcomes: Rate of cases entering the formal justice system (police and courts) per capita, measured from administrative registers.
Secondary outcomes: LC case resolution rates and speed; rates of escalation to violence or repeat disputes; citizen trust and legitimacy of LC institutions; gender-disaggregated outcomes for SGBV cases.
Mechanism measures: LC leader knowledge and skills (measured through knowledge assessments and vignettes); LC case register quality; rates of appropriate referral to formal institutions; citizen perceptions of LC procedural fairness.
Spillover measures: Outcomes in control clusters near treatment clusters, to assess the indirect effects of the ToT diffusion component.
Cost data: Detailed cost data from both NGO partners, including personnel costs, training costs, materials costs, and overhead, to support the CEA.
8.3 Key Risks and Mitigation Strategies
SUTVA violations from ToT diffusion: The ToT diffusion component creates the risk of spillovers from treatment to control clusters. Mitigation strategies include geographic buffers, two-stage randomization, and exposure mapping (Benjamin‐Chung et al., 2017; , Aronow & Samii, 2017; , Baird et al., 2018).
Administrative data quality: LC case registers may be incomplete or inaccurate. Mitigation strategies include baseline data quality assessment, training on register maintenance, and validation studies (Ariel et al., 2018).
Elite capture and gender bias: LC training may be captured by local elites or may fail to improve outcomes for women and other vulnerable groups. Mitigation strategies include gender-balanced LC composition, clear referral protocols for SGBV cases, and monitoring and accountability mechanisms (Macdonald et al., 2022; , Rockowitz et al., 2023).
Secular trends: Changes in outcomes over time that are unrelated to the intervention may confound the stepped-wedge design. Mitigation strategies include time fixed effects in the analysis model and careful documentation of contextual changes during the evaluation period.
Non-compliance: LC leaders may not implement the training as intended. Mitigation strategies include fidelity monitoring, process evaluation, and ToT analysis to assess the relationship between training fidelity and outcomes.

9. Conclusion
This literature review has synthesized evidence from a range of disciplines—including law and society, criminology, epidemiology, economics, and statistics—to inform the design of a rigorous impact evaluation of a Local Council training intervention in Uganda. The review has identified several key findings:
The evidence base on community-based dispute resolution and diversion in Sub-Saharan Africa is characterized by significant methodological limitations, underscoring the need for the kind of rigorous evaluation proposed here (Macdonald et al., 2022; , Rockowitz et al., 2023).
The effectiveness of LC training will depend on multiple mechanisms, including improved legal knowledge, enhanced mediation skills, norm shifts about reporting and escalation, increased perceived legitimacy, and improved coordination with formal institutions (Macdonald et al., 2022; , Cooper‐Knock & Macdonald, 2020).
The ToT diffusion component creates significant challenges for causal inference, requiring careful attention to spillover effects and SUTVA violations (Benjamin‐Chung et al., 2017; , Aronow & Samii, 2017; , Baird et al., 2018).
The stepped-wedge design is well-suited to the phased rollout of the intervention, but requires careful attention to secular trends and carryover effects.
Administrative data are a valuable source of outcome data but require validation studies to assess completeness and accuracy (Ariel et al., 2018).
CEA should capture both direct and indirect (spillover) effects of the intervention, and should use ingredients-based costing to ensure transparency and reproducibility (Takahashi & Otsuka, 2023).
The proposed evaluation has the potential to make a significant contribution to the evidence base on community-based dispute resolution in Sub-Saharan Africa, and to provide actionable guidance for the scaling up of the LC training intervention in Uganda and beyond.
