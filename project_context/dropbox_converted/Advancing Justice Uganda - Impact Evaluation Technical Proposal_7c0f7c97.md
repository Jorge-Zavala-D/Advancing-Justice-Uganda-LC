# Introduction and Proposal Overview
## Purpose of the Technical Proposal
This Technical Proposal presents the research design for evaluating the Local Council training model under the Advancing Justice Uganda program. It frames the evaluation as serving both accountability and learning objectives, with baseline, monitoring, and endline components intended to estimate whether the intervention reduces the inflow of petty cases into the formal justice system while generating operational evidence for program improvement.
This proposal sets out the full evaluation architecture in a rigorous and complete form, specifying the intervention logic, the identification strategy, the randomization and rollout structure, the data and measurement system, the implementation monitoring requirements, and the analytical framework that would allow Legatum and CDFU to test whether the model works in practice.
The proposal is organized around a staggered randomized rollout. The phased rollout of the program creates an opportunity to compare villages that receive the intervention earlier with villages scheduled to receive it later, thereby generating a credible counterfactual within the program itself. The proposal, therefore, treats the phased rollout as the core design feature that makes a rigorous impact evaluation feasible. At the same time, it recognizes that the intervention includes a mentoring and Training-of-Trainers component that may create spillovers across villages, making it necessary to incorporate exposure, diffusion, and implementation fidelity directly into the evaluation design rather than treating them as secondary concerns.
## Proposal Scope and Current Evaluation Focus
This proposal is intentionally focused on a single intervention stream within the broader Advancing Justice Uganda program: the Local Council training model implemented by Community for Development Foundation Uganda in Bushenyi, Sheema, and Rubirizi. Earlier versions of the project concept considered a broader evaluation architecture that included multiple partners and multiple intervention pillars across the justice chain. However, as the scope of the study became more clearly defined through successive discussions with Legatum and the implementing teams, it became evident that a rigorous and feasible evaluation required a narrower and more coherent focus. For that reason, the present proposal no longer treats FCU as part of the causal evaluation design and does not attempt to estimate effects across the full portfolio of justice interventions. Instead, it concentrates exclusively on the CDFU-delivered Local Council pathway as the central object of analysis.
This narrowed focus is not simply a budgetary adjustment; it is also a methodological strengthening of the proposal. By centering the evaluation on one intervention model, one implementing partner, and one clearly defined operational theory of change, the study is better positioned to generate credible evidence on whether the model itself works. The causal centerpiece of the proposal is the prevention pathway: the idea that strengthening Local Council leaders’ ability to attract, classify, resolve (using ADR mechanisms) and appropriately refer petty cases can reduce the inflow of such cases into the formal justice system. The proposal treats the Local Council intervention not as a peripheral community sensitization exercise, but as an institutional reform mechanism that operates at the key upstream point where disputes are either diverted locally or pushed into police and court systems.
The evaluation strategy is correspondingly designed around that institutional focus. Rather than relying primarily on large-scale survey measurement, the proposal adopts an administrative-data-centered approach in which police, court, and Local Council records form the backbone of outcome measurement. This reflects both the substantive logic of the intervention and the practical constraints of implementation. Because the main policy question concerns whether petty cases are being diverted away from the formal justice system, administrative records are the most direct and cost-effective source for tracking these flows over time. They also provide the basis for implementing a staggered impact evaluation design under realistic budget conditions, provided that rollout timing is disciplined and that data extraction and quality assurance are handled carefully.
At the same time, the proposal does not rule out all primary data collection. Rather, it treats primary data as a selective and supporting component of the design, to be included only where it adds clear value beyond the administrative systems. If resources permit, light-touch primary data may be used to measure mechanisms that registers cannot capture well, such as community trust, legitimacy, procedural fairness, leadership knowledge, or implementation fidelity. In that sense, primary data are positioned as strategically complementary rather than foundational. The central logic of the proposal remains that a rigorous impact evaluation can be built primarily around administrative outcomes, with targeted primary data collection used only to strengthen interpretation, validation, and operational learning.
## Structure of the Proposal
The proposal is organized to move from the broader program and institutional context to the specific methodological and operational details of the evaluation design. Following this introductory section, the document first presents the background and justice-sector rationale for the study, with particular attention to the role of Local Council Courts within Uganda’s justice architecture and the policy relevance of preventing petty cases from entering the formal system. It then describes the intervention in detail, including the CDFU implementation model, the geographic scope of the study, the phased rollout structure, and the role of Training-of-Trainers and mentoring in shaping both implementation and potential spillovers.
The next sections develop the conceptual and research foundations of the proposal. These include the literature review, the refined theory of change, the study hypotheses, and the main evaluation questions. Together, these sections establish why the intervention matters, how it is expected to work, and what the evaluation is specifically designed to test. Building on that foundation, the proposal then turns to the core technical sections: the identification strategy, the staggered randomized rollout design, the sampling frame and randomization operations, the data and measurement strategy, and the proposed analytical framework.
Subsequent sections address the operational dimensions that are essential to the credibility of the study but are often secondary in more general concept notes. These include field operations, implementation monitoring, data quality assurance, ethics and safeguarding, and the cost-effectiveness analysis. The document then concludes with the practical components required for implementation and contracting, including deliverables, workplan and timeline, team roles and governance, and the budget structure. In this way, the proposal is designed not only to justify the evaluation scientifically but also to function as a practical blueprint for executing it under the institutional, logistical, and financial conditions currently facing the project.


















# Background, Context, and Rationale
## Justice System Bottlenecks in Uganda
The intervention proposed in this technical evaluation responds to a structural problem that sits at the heart of Uganda’s justice system: the combination of excessive case inflow, slow case processing, and weak early-stage sorting of disputes has produced a system in which congestion and delay are not isolated failures, but routine features of how justice is experienced. The current program framing already identifies these problems in clear terms, describing a justice chain marked by excessive pre-trial detention, severe case backlogs, and the systematic denial of fair and timely hearings across policing, the judiciary, and prisons.
A central driver of this pressure is the continued movement of petty cases and low-level disputes into formal justice institutions even when many of them could, in principle, be resolved through local or community-based mechanisms. The revised program documents explicitly describe this as an upstream bottleneck: minor disputes and low-level offences that might otherwise be handled through alternative local processes often end up entering police and court systems, where they contribute to unnecessary arrests, longer remand periods, and additional burdens on already stretched institutions. The problem is therefore not only that the justice system processes cases too slowly once they are inside it, but also that too many cases enter the formal pipeline in the first place without adequate filtering, triage, or diversion.
This has important implications for both system efficiency and fairness. From an efficiency perspective, when petty matters enter the formal system at scale, they consume police time, judicial attention, prosecutorial resources, and detention capacity that could otherwise be directed toward more serious matters. The effect is cumulative: the larger the volume of low-level cases entering the system, the harder it becomes for formal institutions to process cases quickly and proportionately. The current project materials link this directly to chronic overcrowding, prolonged remand, and the persistence of avoidable incarceration, especially where delays in investigation, limited access to legal assistance, and slow court processes reinforce one another.
But the consequences are not merely administrative. The overload of the formal justice system also raises questions of equity and access. When formal institutions are congested, the resulting delays and procedural burdens fall most heavily on people with fewer resources, weaker legal representation, and less ability to navigate repeated follow-up with police, courts, and detention facilities. In that sense, the problem this intervention addresses is not simply a matter of improving throughput or reducing caseloads; it is also a matter of making justice more proportionate, accessible, and socially grounded. The project’s own problem framing recognizes this point by emphasizing that the pipeline of petty cases into formal institutions is not only inefficient, but also contributes to a pattern in which avoidable detention and delayed justice are disproportionately experienced by vulnerable populations.
This is precisely why an upstream prevention and diversion strategy matters. A recent learning session with the Justice Defenders paralegals in Luzira prisons affirmed that downstream efforts such as legal aid, bail support, or faster court processing remain important, but they do not fully solve the congestion problem if the entry point into the system remains overloaded. Even well-designed legal assistance interventions will struggle to produce lasting decongestion if petty cases continue to enter police and court channels at high volume. The logic of the present intervention is therefore to address the problem closer to its source by improving the capacity of local institutions to resolve eligible disputes earlier, more appropriately, and closer to the community. In practical terms, this means reducing the unnecessary formalization of minor matters while preserving referral pathways for serious cases. The upstream focus is central to the policy rationale of the proposal: if petty disputes can be handled more effectively before they become formal justice cases, then improvements in efficiency and fairness can occur simultaneously rather than being treated as competing goals.

## Local Council Courts within Uganda’s Justice Architecture
Local Council Courts occupy a strategically important position within Uganda’s justice architecture because they sit at the point where community dispute resolution, local governance, and the formal justice system intersect. At the most decentralized level, Uganda’s Local Council structure is organized across successive administrative tiers, beginning with LC1 at the village level, followed by LC2 at the parish level and LC3 at the sub-county level. In practical terms, this means that Local Council institutions are not external to community life; they are embedded within the everyday administrative and social organization of villages and parishes, which is precisely what makes them such a plausible entry point for reform. The current program materials already define LC1, LC2, and LC3 as the relevant administrative levels and identify the Local Council Court or village catchment as the core operational unit for implementation and measurement.
Their institutional relevance for this study lies in the kind of matters they are expected to handle. The intervention is explicitly designed around petty disputes and low-level offences that fall within the scope of local resolution rather than formal criminal processing. Local Council Courts are not being treated here as a generic community platform but rather as specific jurisdictional and procedural lever through which the justice system can redirect appropriate cases away from formal institutions and toward timely local handling.
At the same time, Local Council Courts do not operate in isolation. Their role is meaningful precisely because they sit in relationship to both police and courts on one side and to informal community dispute resolution practices on the other. For some matters, the LC is the first and most accessible point of contact. For others, it functions as an initial sorting or mediation venue before a case is referred upward. This relationship is central to the logic of the intervention: the training is intended not only to improve local case handling, but also to strengthen classification and referral decisions so that petty matters are retained locally while serious cases are escalated appropriately. The evaluation therefore treats LCCs as part of a broader justice pathway rather than as a self-contained institution. That is also why the core outcomes are defined in terms of flows across institutional boundaries, especially whether cases that originate in village catchments ultimately enter police and court registries.
This institutional position also has an important conceptual implication: the effectiveness of Local Council Courts depends not only on formal jurisdiction, but on the fact that people make choices across multiple justice forums. In practice, citizens may take a dispute to family elders, religious leaders, Local Councils, police, or formal courts depending on cost, perceived fairness, speed, social pressure, expected outcomes, and the seriousness of the matter. This is the essence of legal pluralism, and it is especially important in the Ugandan context because the study is not merely asking whether LCCs can process cases, but whether strengthening them changes where cases go in the first place. The intervention is therefore plausible as a reform lever because it aims to shift both institutional performance and forum choice: it seeks to make LCCs more capable of handling eligible matters while also making them a more viable and legitimate first resort for community members.
## Logic and Contribution of the Evaluation
The case for evaluating the Local Council training model at this stage is both strategic and substantive. Strategically, Legatum and CDFU are no longer asking only whether the intervention can be delivered, but whether it can be shown to work in a way that is credible enough to justify future scale-up, adaptation, and sustained investment. The current program framing already makes clear that the evaluation is expected to serve both accountability and learning objectives: on the one hand, it should determine whether the intervention reduces the burden placed on the formal justice system; on the other, it should identify the mechanisms through which the intervention succeeds or fails and the conditions under which impacts are stronger or weaker. The intervention is therefore at exactly the point where a rigorous evaluation is most valuable: the model is sufficiently developed to be implemented at scale, but still early enough that evidence can shape rollout decisions rather than merely document them after the fact.
This timing is particularly important because the intervention is not designed as a generic awareness campaign or broad governance strengthening exercise. It is a specific institutional model that seeks to change how petty cases are classified, managed, and routed within Uganda’s justice system. The intervention is a structured package of training and post-training support for Local Council leaders, intended to strengthen their ability to classify, mediate, and resolve petty cases locally while improving referral of serious matters. Its core target is therefore operational and measurable: reducing the entry of eligible petty matters into police and court systems. Because the program is being implemented in phases rather than all at once, and because the current implementation model includes a built-in mentoring and diffusion component, the present moment offers an unusual opportunity to test the model under real program conditions while still preserving the possibility of credible comparison.
The evaluation also has immediate value for scale decisions. If the intervention proves effective, Legatum and CDFU will be in a far stronger position to argue not only that Local Councils are important in principle, but that this particular training model produces measurable system benefits. That matters for program expansion, for future resource mobilization, and for decisions about whether the model should be adapted to other districts or integrated into broader justice-sector programming. Just as importantly, if the intervention produces mixed or uneven results, the evaluation can identify why. It can distinguish between the effect of training itself, the role of implementation fidelity, the importance of baseline context such as hotspot status or administrative capacity, and the extent to which diffusion through mentoring strengthens or complicates the effects. In that sense, the study is not simply a summative exercise; it is also a tool for structured learning about program design.
The need for credible attribution is central to this logic. Legatum has made clear that descriptive benchmarking alone is not sufficient: the evaluation must be able to say whether observed changes in case flows and local justice processing can reasonably be attributed to the intervention rather than to unrelated trends or contextual change. This is what makes a rigorous design especially valuable. The intervention’s phased rollout and its village-level implementation structure make it possible to generate that attribution without requiring a design that is detached from implementation realities. Rather than treating causal inference as an academic add-on, this proposal treats it as a practical requirement for deciding whether the model should be retained, expanded, or redesigned.
Beyond its programmatic value, the evaluation also fills an important evidence gap. The broader literature on community justice, alternative dispute resolution, and local legal empowerment in low- and middle-income countries suggests that these mechanisms are widely used and often normatively central, yet the causal evidence on whether they actually reduce pressure on formal justice systems remains surprisingly thin. Much of the existing literature documents how people navigate multiple justice forums, how legitimacy and forum choice operate in legally pluralistic settings, and how local institutions may either improve access or reproduce inequality. But there are still relatively few rigorous evaluations that test whether strengthening local dispute-resolution institutions changes actual system flows, especially in ways that can be tied to concrete policy decisions about congestion, delay, and case management. The present study is therefore positioned to contribute not only to program management, but also to the broader evidence base on how local justice institutions can function as a mechanism of prevention rather than merely as a parallel forum.
Its contribution is also interdisciplinary. At the policy level, the study speaks directly to debates on justice decongestion, legal empowerment, and state capacity. At the governance level, it provides evidence on whether local institutions can perform a meaningful gatekeeping role without undermining procedural fairness or referral integrity. At the academic level, it offers a rare opportunity to study a community-level justice intervention using a design that is attentive to both causal identification and the institutional realities of legal pluralism. This is especially important because the intervention does not occur in a vacuum: its effects depend on how citizens choose among competing forums, how Local Councils interact with police and courts, and how implementation structures such as Training-of-Trainers generate both intended diffusion and potential spillovers. The study’s contribution, therefore, is not simply to estimate whether the model “works,” but to explain how it operates within a layered justice system and what that implies for future reform.




# Intervention Description and Implementation Architecture
## Overview of the CDFU Intervention
The intervention evaluated in this proposal is a Local Council training and support model implemented by Community for Development Foundation Uganda with the explicit objective of reducing the unnecessary entry of petty cases into the formal justice system. At its core, the model treats Local Council Courts not simply as community institutions that already exist, but as frontline justice actors whose capacity can be strengthened so that low-level disputes are handled earlier, closer to the community, and in a manner that reduces avoidable pressure on police and courts. In that sense, the intervention is best understood as a prevention and diversion model: it seeks to change the point at which petty matters are processed, so that cases that can be resolved locally do not automatically escalate into formal justice channels.
The intervention itself has three closely connected components. The first is the formal training of Local Council leaders using the official Local Council training manual, which is expected to be aligned with the Ministry of Local Government handbook. The second is the strengthening of day-to-day justice practices at the local level, especially around case identification, mediation, referral, and record-keeping. The third is a structured post-training support system, delivered through mentoring and Training-of-Trainers mechanisms, intended to reinforce application of the training over time and extend knowledge beyond the first set of directly trained leaders. Together, these elements make the intervention more than a one-off workshop model; it is designed as an operational package that combines initial capacity-building with continued support and diffusion.
The substantive logic of the intervention is as follows: If Local Council leaders are better able to distinguish petty matters from cases requiring formal escalation, and if they have stronger skills and confidence to mediate disputes locally, then the number of petty cases flowing into police and court systems should decline. The intended outcome is to reduce those flows by strengthening classification, mediation, and appropriate referral at the Local Council level. The intervention therefore operates at the key upstream decision point where community disputes are either resolved locally or passed into formal institutions.
A further defining feature of the CDFU model is that support does not end with the initial training event. The intervention includes a mentoring and diffusion structure through which trained leaders are expected to support additional Local Councils, and in the CDFU model this process is reinforced by phone-enabled tracking and reporting. This matters for both implementation and evaluation. Programmatically, it means that knowledge and practice change are expected to continue after the initial training and potentially extend beyond directly treated villages. Analytically, it means that the intervention should not be conceptualized as a static “treated versus untreated” distinction, but as a rollout model in which direct exposure, mentoring, and possible spillovers are all part of the intervention architecture itself.
For the purposes of this proposal, then, the CDFU intervention is defined precisely as a structured Local Council capacity-building model that combines training, procedural strengthening, and post-training mentoring in order to divert petty cases away from the formal justice system while preserving appropriate referral for more serious matters. That definition is important because it anchors the rest of the evaluation design: the study is not testing a vague notion of local awareness or justice reform in general, but a concrete institutional model with a specific operational logic and a measurable policy target.
## Geographic Scope and Target Units
The intervention evaluated in this proposal is geographically concentrated in the three districts where CDFU is implementing the Local Council training model: Bushenyi, Sheema, and Rubirizi. Within this footprint, the relevant operational unit is the village-level Local Council Court catchment. This is the level at which training is delivered, where petty disputes are expected to be classified and managed, and where the main prevention mechanism is expected to operate. It is also the most appropriate level for measurement, since the core outcomes of interest concern whether disputes originating in a given village catchment are resolved locally or instead enter police and court systems.
The available planning figures also indicate that the intervention has sufficient scale within CDFU’s geography to support a phased evaluation design. The current implementation notes state that CDFU targets 966 of 1,484 Local Council Courts across its three districts, with an initial phase covering a subset of courts distributed across Bushenyi, Sheema, and Rubirizi. This matters because the design proposed in this technical proposal depends not only on where the intervention takes place, but on the fact that coverage is expected to expand over time rather than all at once.
This phased structure is central to the evaluation design. Rather than conceptualizing the study as a simple comparison between permanently treated and permanently untreated places, the proposal treats the CDFU geography as a common implementation space in which villages can be assigned to different rollout stages. Under that logic, villages scheduled to receive training in later waves function as comparison units during earlier phases of implementation, allowing the study to estimate the effects of the intervention while remaining consistent with CDFU’s operational model.
For the purposes of the technical proposal, then, the geographic scope of the study is not simply a list of districts. It is a structured implementation territory made up of village-level Local Council Court catchments within Bushenyi, Sheema, and Rubirizi, organized in a way that allows both program delivery and rigorous evaluation. ## Training Content and Delivery Model
The CDFU intervention is built around a structured Local Council training model that is intended to strengthen the practical capacity of village-level justice actors to manage petty disputes more effectively and to reduce unnecessary escalation into the formal justice system. The starting point for the training is the official Local Council training manual, which provides the institutional and procedural foundation for the intervention and is expected to be aligned with the relevant Ministry of Local Government guidance.
At the center of the training is the issue of legal classification and triage. A core weakness in many low-capacity justice environments is not only that cases are handled slowly, but that they are routed incorrectly from the beginning. The training is therefore designed to strengthen Local Council leaders’ ability to distinguish matters that fall within their local mandate from those that require referral to police, courts, or specialized protection systems. In practical terms, this means helping participants identify what constitutes a petty dispute or low-level matter appropriate for local handling, what should be escalated immediately, and what kinds of warning signs require caution or consultation.
A second major component of the training concerns mediation and alternative dispute resolution. The intervention assumes that Local Council leaders require not only formal knowledge of their jurisdiction, but also the practical ability to convene parties, facilitate dialogue, manage conflict, and support workable settlements. For that reason, the delivery model is expected to include applied instruction on mediation techniques, conflict de-escalation, listening and facilitation skills, and the procedural handling of common petty disputes.
Closely linked to this is the training’s focus on referral protocols. A well-functioning Local Council system does not retain every case; it acts as an early institutional filter that resolves what can appropriately be handled locally and channels onward what must be managed elsewhere. The training model, therefore, includes explicit attention to when and how cases should be referred to police, courts, probation authorities, or other service providers. This is particularly important for sensitive or serious matters, including cases involving sexual and gender-based violence, child protection concerns, or other forms of harm for which local informal handling may be inappropriate or unsafe. The proposal, therefore, treats referral not as an administrative afterthought, but as a core part of the intervention logic and a critical safeguard within the delivery model.
The final substantive pillar of the training concerns record-keeping and case tracking. Because the evaluation relies primarily on administrative records, the quality and consistency of Local Council documentation are essential both to the functioning of the intervention and to the credibility of the study. The training is therefore expected to reinforce routine documentation practices, including how cases are logged, how outcomes and referrals are recorded, and how follow-up information is maintained over time. In the CDFU model, this is further supported by post-training tracking mechanisms that are intended to improve the visibility of case handling within treated areas. In this sense, record-keeping is not only an administrative skill but part of the intervention itself: stronger local justice processes require stronger information practices.
In terms of delivery, the model is designed to be practical and implementation-oriented rather than purely didactic. While the final training schedule, session length, and facilitation structure will be specified in the operational annexes, the technical logic of the model suggests that training will need to combine legal and procedural content with applied exercises, case examples, and locally relevant scenarios. The expectation is that participants are not only told what their role is, but are guided through how to make difficult decisions in practice: how to classify cases, when to mediate, when to refer, and how to document those decisions. In the CDFU context, the immediate trainees are expected to be the key Local Council leaders designated for direct training, who then form the first layer of implementation and later become part of the mentoring and diffusion architecture described in the next subsection.
## Training-of-Trainers and Mentoring Structure
A defining feature of the CDFU intervention is that it is not designed to stop at the first layer of direct training. Instead, it incorporates an explicit Training-of-Trainers and mentoring structure intended to expand the reach of the model beyond the initially trained Local Council leaders and to reinforce the practical application of the training over time. This diffusion mechanism is central to how the intervention is expected to function in the field and therefore needs to be treated as part of the intervention itself rather than as a secondary implementation detail.
Under the current CDFU model, the first directly trained participant in each village is the Local Council Chairperson. The expectation is that this individual will not only absorb the initial training but will also serve as the first conduit for expanding its practical use within the village-level justice structure. The current implementation notes indicate that the Chairperson is expected to train other members of the village LC committee and to help cascade knowledge beyond the first direct recipient. In subsequent phases, a subset of those initially trained are expected to take on a more formal Training-of-Trainers function.
This structure implies that the intervention includes two distinct forms of exposure. The first is direct exposure through initial formal training. The second is indirect exposure through mentoring, follow-on support, and knowledge transfer from trained leaders to nearby Local Council structures. In implementation terms, this is attractive because it allows the model to extend beyond the first wave of direct training without requiring the same level of formal delivery resources in every village. In evaluation terms, however, it means that the study cannot rely on a simple binary distinction between treated and untreated villages. Some villages may receive direct training, some may receive mentoring or spillover exposure before their scheduled rollout, and others may be indirectly influenced simply because they are adjacent to trained villages.
The Training-of-Trainers structure therefore has two major implications for the design of the study. The first concerns exposure and spillovers. Because mentoring is expected to occur across neighboring villages, the evaluation must be designed in a way that can detect and account for these patterns rather than ignore them. This is one of the reasons the proposal favors a staggered rollout logic and anticipates explicit tracking of treatment timing, mentoring links, and potential cross-village influence. The second implication concerns implementation fidelity. For the intervention to be evaluated meaningfully, it is not enough to know whether direct training sessions took place. The study must also track whether the intended mentoring activities actually occurred, whether they happened in the villages and phases expected, and whether their intensity varied across places.
## Operational Rollout and Sequencing
The operational rollout of the CDFU intervention is designed as a phased process rather than a single simultaneous launch across all eligible villages. This feature is not incidental to the proposal; it is one of the main reasons a rigorous evaluation is feasible under real implementation conditions.
In practical terms, this means that eligible village-level Local Council Court catchments will not all begin receiving the intervention at once. Instead, rollout will occur in multiple waves, with some villages receiving the training and post-training support earlier and others receiving it later according to an agreed implementation calendar. This sequencing creates a natural evaluation structure: villages scheduled to begin treatment in later waves can serve as comparison units during the period in which earlier-wave villages have already begun receiving the intervention. Eligible LCCs can be assigned to earlier versus later rollout so that trends in outcomes can be compared over time between already treated and not-yet-treated units.
This approach has several advantages for both program management and evaluation. Operationally, it is consistent with the reality that training teams, supervision capacity, and mentoring structures cannot cover all sites simultaneously. It allows implementation to remain manageable while preserving the ability to document and monitor each wave. From an evaluation perspective, the phase-in structure avoids the need to define a permanently untreated group outside the intervention space and instead generates a counterfactual within the implementation plan itself. In other words, comparison is created through timing rather than through exclusion. That makes the design easier to align with partner incentives, while still supporting credible estimation of program effects as long as rollout timing is assigned in a disciplined and documented way.
At the same time, the success of this approach depends on respecting the sequencing that the design requires. If villages assigned to later waves begin receiving training earlier than planned, or if mentoring spills over into not-yet-treated villages before their scheduled start, the contrast between early- and later-wave villages is weakened. The current SoW explicitly identifies this as a major challenge and highlights that the mentoring component may introduce contamination where treated leaders provide support to villages scheduled for later rollout. For this reason, the phased rollout must be understood not just as a timetable for delivery, but as a core part of the evaluation architecture. It needs to be coordinated carefully with CDFU so that implementation remains flexible enough to respond to field realities, but disciplined enough to preserve the temporal structure on which the design depends.
The proposal therefore treats sequencing as both an implementation and a research function. Villages assigned to earlier waves are expected to receive training and structured support first, while villages scheduled for subsequent waves remain not-yet-treated during the corresponding observation period. Those later-wave villages are not treated as “control” in a permanent or static sense; rather, they serve as comparison units temporarily, until their own rollout begins. This is an important distinction because it keeps the design aligned with a program that intends eventual coverage, while still allowing the evaluation to generate clear contrasts at each stage of implementation.
## Implementation Risks and Operational Dependencies
The credibility of this evaluation will depend not only on the logic of the design, but on whether the intervention is implemented with enough discipline and transparency for the design to operate as intended. For that reason, several practical risks and dependencies need to be treated as central to the proposal from the outset rather than as secondary operational concerns.
The first and most important of these is adherence to the randomized rollout. The core evaluation strategy assumes that eligible Local Council Court catchments will be assigned to earlier and later waves of implementation and that this sequencing will be respected closely enough for not-yet-treated villages to provide a valid comparison during each stage of rollout. The evaluation design loses credibility if implementation drifts away from the agreed sequence, for example because villages are brought forward informally, because treatment is delivered selectively outside the assigned wave, or because local operational pressures lead to unrecorded deviations from the original assignment.
A closely related risk concerns mentoring and diffusion outside assigned waves. The intervention is explicitly designed to spread beyond initially trained villages through Training-of-Trainers activity, a major threat to clean comparison if treated leaders begin supporting villages scheduled for later rollout. In the CDFU model, where mentoring is expected to occur partly through geographic proximity and informal linkages, the risk is not simply that villages will “contaminate” one another in a technical sense. The deeper issue is that exposure to the intervention may begin before formal training is delivered, making it harder to distinguish between direct treatment effects and early mentoring spillovers. This means that mentoring logs, timing records, and any available documentation of follow-on support will need to be treated as essential components of implementation monitoring rather than optional program documentation.
A third operational dependency is the quality and accessibility of administrative data. The proposal is built around an administrative-data-centered strategy because the key outcomes of interest concern whether petty cases are entering police and court systems and whether local institutions are processing and referring cases differently over time. Police registers, court registries, and Local Council records are the core measurement backbone. The feasibility of the evaluation therefore, depends heavily on whether these records are available at the relevant level, whether they can be linked to village catchments with reasonable consistency, and whether their quality is sufficient to support longitudinal analysis. If register completeness varies sharply across places or over time, or if data access depends on ad hoc permissions that delay extraction, the evaluation could face serious constraints even if the rollout itself proceeds as planned.
Finally, all of these issues depend on close coordination with CDFU field teams. The evaluation cannot be implemented as an external research exercise running parallel to program delivery; it has to be embedded in the operational life of the intervention. This means that the evaluation team will need timely access to rollout schedules, village rosters, training attendance records, mentoring plans, and any case-tracking tools used by the program. It also means that CDFU field teams will need clear guidance on what aspects of implementation are fixed for evaluation purposes, what kinds of adaptations require documentation, and what kinds of deviations could compromise the design. # Literature Review and Conceptual Framing
## Evidence on Community-Based Dispute Resolution and Diversion
The empirical literature provides a strong substantive rationale for evaluating the Local Council training model, even though the existing evidence base remains methodologically uneven. Across low- and middle-income countries, and especially in Sub-Saharan Africa, community-based dispute resolution mechanisms frequently play an important role in how citizens actually access justice. In many settings, local forums are more accessible, less costly, more socially embedded, and often faster than formal institutions. At the same time, the literature shows that these local mechanisms do not automatically reduce pressure on the formal justice system simply by existing. Their ability to divert cases depends on how they are perceived, how they operate in practice, and whether they are capable of handling disputes in ways that are seen as legitimate, fair, and enforceable. For that reason, the policy relevance of the present intervention lies not only in the existence of Local Council Courts, but in whether targeted support can improve their functioning enough to alter the actual flow of petty cases into police and courts.
In Uganda, this question is especially important because Local Council Courts occupy a hybrid institutional position between state law and community-based authority. Research on lower state courts in northern Uganda shows that citizens engage with these institutions for a wide range of reasons that are normative, tactical, and pragmatic rather than purely procedural or legalistic (Macdonald et al., 2022). Local courts are often expected to mediate rather than adjudicate, and the meaning of mediation itself is shaped by local social norms, community relationships, and perceptions of authority. This makes them potentially powerful institutions for diversion, but it also means that their effectiveness cannot be assumed from formal jurisdiction alone. Macdonald et al. (2022) show that people may resist mediation or avoid local institutions when they perceive them as partial, corrupt, or unable to protect their interests. Similarly, Cooper-Knock and Macdonald (2020), drawing on evidence from both Uganda and South Africa, argue that citizen engagement with lower courts is best understood through a broader “trifactor” logic in which people weigh how institutions should act, how they are expected to act, and how they need them to act in a given situation.
The broader literature on alternative dispute resolution in LMICs reinforces this point. Community mediation and local dispute-resolution mechanisms are often associated with lower costs, faster case handling, and improved accessibility, but much of this evidence is based on descriptive or qualitative work rather than rigorous causal designs. In Uganda, people often prefer to resolve disputes through family meetings, customary processes, or local courts rather than immediately turning to the formal system, especially for land disputes, family conflicts, and other everyday grievances (Macdonald et al., 2022). This suggests that there is already a behavioral and institutional basis for diversion. What remains uncertain is whether strengthening Local Council leaders through training and structured support can change these patterns at scale and in a measurable way. The present intervention is therefore well grounded in institutional reality, but the empirical literature still leaves open the central causal question that this proposal is designed to answer.
There is also a relevant regional literature showing that training justice actors can improve justice-related outcomes, even when the evidence stops short of a rigorous causal test. Albezreh et al. (2022), for example, evaluate a multiyear multisectoral training program in Kenya focused on sexual and gender-based violence response across health, legal, and law-enforcement sectors. They find significant improvements in knowledge and report qualitative evidence of improved confidentiality, better forensic and legal processes, and stronger coordination across institutions. Although this study does not focus on petty-case diversion and does not use a randomized design, it is important for the present proposal because it demonstrates that structured training can change the behavior and practices of justice actors in East African institutional settings. Similarly, Rockowitz et al. (2023), in a systematic review of criminal justice initiatives related to sexual violence in East Africa, conclude that the evidence base remains limited and fragmented, but that training, multisectoral coordination, and institutional strengthening are recurring features of interventions intended to improve justice outcomes. Their review highlights exactly the gap that this proposal seeks to address: there is clear programmatic interest in justice-sector interventions, but far less rigorous evidence on whether they actually change the relevant system outcomes.
Taken together, this literature supports the intervention in two important ways. First, it establishes that community justice institutions and ADR-type mechanisms are already central to how justice is experienced and navigated in Uganda and similar settings. Second, it shows that while local institutions may plausibly reduce formal-system pressure, the empirical evidence on whether targeted reforms actually produce diversion remains limited, especially under rigorous causal designs. In this sense, the intervention is not speculative; it is grounded in an existing institutional pathway that citizens already use. But neither is it redundant. The key unresolved question is whether strengthening Local Council capacity changes justice flows in a measurable and policy-relevant way.
This is precisely where the present evaluation makes its contribution. The intervention responds to a real and documented problem—unnecessary formalization of petty matters in a congested justice system—but it also addresses a clear gap in the evidence base. Existing studies explain why local justice institutions matter and how citizens navigate among them, but they do not yet provide sufficiently robust evidence on whether strengthening those institutions reduces formal-system inflow. By focusing on petty-case diversion in Uganda, this study is positioned to bridge the worlds of access-to-justice programming, legal pluralism, and rigorous impact evaluation. It is therefore justified both by the practical importance of the intervention and by the empirical need for stronger evidence on community-based dispute resolution in LMIC contexts (Cooper-Knock & Macdonald, 2020; Macdonald et al., 2022; Rockowitz et al., 2023).
## Theoretical Mechanisms
The logic of the intervention rests on the premise that strengthening Local Council leaders can change how disputes are processed at the point where community-level grievance handling meets the formal justice system. The expected effects of the training model do not depend on a single pathway. Rather, they depend on a set of interrelated mechanisms through which improved local institutional capacity can alter the classification, handling, routing, and social perception of petty cases. The literature suggests that at least five mechanisms are particularly central to understanding how the intervention may produce measurable reductions in the entry of petty cases into police and courts: improved classification and triage, stronger mediation capacity, greater legitimacy and procedural fairness, clearer coordination and referral practices, and the diffusion of knowledge through mentoring and Training-of-Trainers structures.
The first mechanism is improved classification and triage. At the center of the intervention is the expectation that training Local Council leaders on the official manual and associated legal guidance will improve their ability to distinguish between matters that fall within local jurisdiction and matters that require escalation to formal institutions. This is theoretically important because many justice bottlenecks emerge not only from slow case processing, but from weak front-end sorting. If local leaders are unable to identify which disputes are appropriately handled at the Local Council level and which must be referred onward, petty matters are more likely to enter the formal system unnecessarily, while serious matters may be mishandled locally. The literature reviewed for this project strongly supports the plausibility of this mechanism. In particular, Albezreh et al. (2022) show that structured multisectoral training in Kenya significantly improved justice-related knowledge and case handling practices, suggesting that targeted training can affect how frontline actors classify and process cases. Applied to the present context, the hypothesis is that improved legal knowledge and triage capacity should reduce inappropriate referrals into police and courts and improve the quality of local case management.
The second mechanism is mediation capacity. The intervention is not designed only to change legal knowledge in the abstract; it is also intended to improve the practical ability of Local Council leaders to facilitate negotiated dispute resolution. This matters because petty-case diversion will only be sustainable if disputes handled locally are not merely intercepted, but resolved in ways that parties regard as workable, timely, and socially acceptable. Macdonald et al. (2022) suggest that lower state courts in Uganda often function through local mediation processes shaped by community norms and relationships, while the broader literature on ADR indicates that the effectiveness of mediation depends heavily on whether mediators are perceived as sufficiently capable, neutral, and authoritative. The intervention therefore assumes that better mediation skills—such as structuring dialogue, de-escalating conflict, managing local bargaining, and documenting agreements—will increase the probability that disputes are resolved before they become formal cases. In this sense, mediation capacity is the mechanism that links improved local handling to lower formal-system inflow.
A third mechanism concerns legitimacy and procedural fairness. The literature makes clear that citizens’ use of justice institutions is shaped not only by jurisdiction or convenience, but by whether those institutions are perceived as fair, trustworthy, and capable of acting in line with social expectations. Cooper-Knock and Macdonald (2020) argue that people engage with lower courts through a complex reconciliation of how institutions should act, how they expect them to act, and how they need them to act. This suggests that even if Local Council leaders are technically more capable after training, diversion will not occur at scale unless citizens also believe that Local Council processes are credible and worth using. Similarly, Macdonald et al. (2022) show that people in Uganda may avoid or resist mediation when they perceive local authorities as partial or corrupt. For the present intervention, this means that legitimacy is not a secondary normative issue; it is one of the central mechanisms through which training may or may not affect forum choice. If the training improves not only procedural handling but also the fairness and consistency with which Local Councils operate, then citizens may become more willing to bring eligible disputes to them rather than escalating directly to police or courts.
The fourth mechanism is coordination and referral. The intervention does not assume that successful Local Councils are those that retain all disputes locally. On the contrary, one of the clearest theoretical pathways to improved system performance is the strengthening of referral behavior: petty matters are handled locally when appropriate, while serious, sensitive, or legally unsuitable matters are referred upward in a more timely and consistent way. This makes the Local Council less of a substitute for the formal system in general and more of a gatekeeping institution that improves the match between dispute type and dispute forum. The literature on justice-sector strengthening suggests that training can improve inter-institutional coordination, especially when it clarifies roles, procedures, and thresholds for referral. Albezreh et al. (2022) find that training in Kenya improved cross-sector collaboration and institutional response, which supports the broader proposition that structured training can improve the way justice actors relate to one another across a system. In the Ugandan case, the key expectation is that better referral practices will both reduce unnecessary formal entry of petty cases and improve the handling of cases that truly require police or court involvement.
The fifth mechanism is diffusion. Unlike interventions that are delivered once and contained within directly treated units, the present model includes a Training-of-Trainers and mentoring structure that is intended to spread knowledge and practice beyond the first wave of trained leaders. This means that the intervention may produce effects not only through direct exposure but also through social and institutional diffusion. The literature on diffusion and training spillovers suggests that such effects can be meaningful, but also analytically challenging. Takahashi and Otsuka (2023), reviewing extension and training models in Sub-Saharan Africa, note that training effects may spread gradually to non-participants and that ignoring such spillovers can lead to underestimation of cost-effectiveness. At the same time, methodological work on spillovers emphasizes that diffusion complicates standard causal assumptions because outcomes in one unit may depend on treatment status in nearby or connected units (Aronow & Samii, 2017; Benjamin-Chung et al., 2017; Halloran & Hudgens, 2016). In this proposal, diffusion is therefore treated as both a substantive mechanism and an evaluation challenge. Programmatically, it may increase the reach and value of the intervention. Analytically, it requires explicit attention to exposure, spillovers, and possible displacement when interpreting effects.
Taken together, these mechanisms suggest that the intervention should not be understood as a simple training-to-outcome pipeline. Its effects depend on whether Local Council leaders learn to classify cases more accurately, mediate them more effectively, act in ways that increase perceived fairness and legitimacy, coordinate more appropriately with formal justice actors, and diffuse those practices beyond the first set of treated villages. The significance of the intervention lies precisely in the interaction among these mechanisms. Better triage without mediation capacity may still result in unresolved disputes. Better mediation without legitimacy may not change citizen behavior. Better local handling without clear referral rules may generate harmful informalization of serious cases. And successful direct training without attention to diffusion may miss a substantial part of the intervention’s system effect. The conceptual framing of this evaluation therefore treats these mechanisms as mutually reinforcing pathways through which Local Council training may affect both local justice practices and broader formal-system outcomes.
## Risks and Boundary Conditions
The intervention is grounded in a plausible and policy-relevant reform logic, but the literature also makes clear that strengthening local justice institutions does not automatically generate better justice outcomes. Community-based dispute resolution mechanisms can fail, produce weak effects, or even generate harm when they operate under conditions of weak accountability, unequal local power, or unclear boundaries between what should be handled locally and what should be escalated to formal institutions. For that reason, any serious evaluation of the Local Council training model must be explicit not only about the mechanisms through which the intervention may work, but also about the risks and boundary conditions under which it may not.
One of the most important risks is elite capture. Local justice institutions are often embedded in existing community hierarchies, which means that improving their operational capacity does not necessarily make them more impartial. In Uganda and comparable African settings, research shows that lower-level justice institutions can be shaped by local patronage, social influence, and informal exchanges that favor particular families, groups, or individuals (Cooper-Knock & Macdonald, 2020; Macdonald et al., 2022). This is particularly important for the present intervention because it seeks to increase the use of Local Councils as a first point of dispute resolution. If trained leaders use their enhanced authority in ways that reinforce local power asymmetries rather than reduce them, the intervention could improve throughput without improving justice. In that sense, elite capture is not simply a corruption problem; it is a structural boundary condition on whether stronger Local Councils become more effective public institutions or merely more efficient gatekeepers for unequal local power.
A second major risk concerns gender bias and the treatment of vulnerable groups. The literature consistently shows that informal and semi-formal justice institutions can reproduce social hierarchies and discriminatory norms, particularly with respect to women, children, and socially marginalized individuals (Macdonald et al., 2022; Rockowitz et al., 2023). In Uganda, women facing sexual violence or family-related harms often navigate a justice environment in which both formal and informal institutions may be experienced as inaccessible, biased, or unsafe (Macdonald et al., 2022). This creates a difficult tension for the intervention. On one hand, stronger local institutions may improve access to justice for some groups by reducing costs and bringing resolution closer to the community. On the other hand, if the Local Council process reflects pre-existing community biases, greater use of those institutions may expose vulnerable groups to pressure, under-protection, or unequal outcomes. This is why gender and inclusion are not ancillary concerns in the evaluation; they are central to understanding whether the intervention improves access to justice in an equitable way.
Closely related to this is the risk of inappropriate informalization of serious cases. The intervention is specifically justified as a mechanism for diverting petty matters away from police and courts, but that logic can become problematic if it blurs the distinction between disputes that are eligible for local handling and those that require formal investigation, adjudication, or protective services. The literature strongly cautions against assuming that all local handling is beneficial. Macdonald et al. (2022) show that the local resolution of some sensitive disputes may fail to provide accountability, deterrence, or protection, while Rockowitz et al. (2023) emphasize that weak referral pathways, stigma, and institutional fragmentation often undermine justice responses in cases of sexual violence. This makes referral integrity one of the most important boundary conditions of the intervention. The model can only be considered successful if it diverts petty matters while preserving or improving the escalation of serious, harmful, or legally unsuitable cases to the appropriate formal authorities. If reductions in formal-system entry are achieved partly by retaining serious cases locally, then the intervention could appear effective on paper while producing serious substantive harms.
Weak legitimacy is another important boundary condition. The theoretical logic of the intervention assumes that stronger local institutions will become more credible and more widely used, but the literature suggests that improved technical capacity does not automatically translate into public trust. Cooper-Knock and Macdonald (2020) show that people engage with lower courts not simply because those institutions exist, but because they make strategic judgments about fairness, likely outcomes, and practical necessity. Similarly, Macdonald et al. (2022) show that citizens may bypass mediation or local forums when they believe these institutions are partial, compromised, or unlikely to protect them. This means that the intervention may fail even if training is delivered correctly and local leaders retain the content. If citizens do not perceive Local Councils as legitimate, they may continue taking disputes directly to police or courts, or may rely on other informal forums instead. In this way, legitimacy functions as both a mechanism and a boundary condition: stronger technical performance can increase use only if it also translates into more credible local justice.
Finally, the intervention faces risks related to spillovers and displacement. Because the CDFU model includes a Training-of-Trainers and mentoring structure, knowledge and practices may diffuse beyond directly treated villages, which can be beneficial from a programmatic perspective but challenging from an evaluation perspective. Methodological work on interference emphasizes that when one unit’s outcomes depend on treatment in nearby or connected units, standard evaluation assumptions become more difficult to sustain (Aronow & Samii, 2017; Benjamin-Chung et al., 2017; Halloran & Hudgens, 2016). In the present study, spillovers may occur if trained leaders mentor nearby Local Councils before those councils are formally scheduled for rollout. In addition, there is the possibility of displacement: rather than reducing petty disputes overall, treatment may shift where they are reported, how they are classified, or which neighboring villages or institutions receive them. This means that changes observed in untreated villages may not necessarily indicate absence of effect; they may reflect indirect exposure or redistribution of case flows. As a result, spillovers and displacement are not merely technical nuisances. They are substantive features of how the intervention may operate in real institutional settings and therefore must be incorporated into both design and interpretation.
## Methodological Implications
The literature reviewed above does not only justify the intervention substantively; it also points quite directly to the kind of evaluation design that is most appropriate for studying it. Because the intervention is delivered at the level of Local Council Courts and village justice structures rather than to isolated individuals, the most relevant methodological lesson is that the unit of evaluation should match the level at which treatment is assigned and where institutional behavior is expected to change. This is why a cluster-based design is more appropriate than an individual-level design for the present study. As Benjamin-Chung et al. (2017) note in their review of spillover-sensitive interventions, when treatment is delivered in socially and geographically connected environments, cluster-level designs are often the most plausible way to preserve interpretability while acknowledging that outcomes are shaped by institutional context. In the case of the Local Council intervention, the relevant cluster is the village or Local Council Court catchment, since that is the level at which leaders are trained, disputes are handled, and case flows are likely to be affected.
The same literature also strongly supports the use of a randomized rollout or phased implementation design. The practical reason is clear: the intervention cannot realistically be delivered to all eligible villages at the same time. But the methodological reason is just as important. A randomized phase-in design allows all clusters to receive the intervention eventually while still creating valid comparison periods in which some villages are treated and others are not yet treated. This makes the design both ethically and operationally attractive in settings where implementers intend eventual coverage. Benjamin-Chung et al. (2017) highlight the advantages of stepped-wedge and related phased-rollout designs for interventions that are expected to expand over time, while the broader discussion in the literature emphasizes that these designs can improve feasibility and acceptability without abandoning causal identification. In this proposal, that logic is especially compelling because the intervention is already organized in waves and because future implementation is expected to unfold across a finite universe of eligible villages rather than through a one-off universal launch.
The literature also clarifies why administrative data should be placed at the center of the evaluation. The main policy question is whether petty cases are diverted away from police and court systems and handled differently at the Local Council level. Those are institutional flows, and administrative records are the most direct source for observing them over time. Ariel et al. (2018), in their study of body-worn cameras in railway stations, illustrate the value of using official records as primary outcomes in justice-related randomized evaluations, particularly when the objective is to measure behavioral and institutional changes rather than self-reported perceptions alone. More generally, the literature reviewed for this project suggests that administrative data have several advantages in this context: they are potentially available for all treatment units, can be collected repeatedly over time, and map more closely onto the justice-system outcomes that matter most to Legatum and CDFU. At the same time, the literature also warns against treating administrative data as automatically clean or complete. Ariel et al. (2018) and the broader methodological discussion make clear that such records may reflect changes in recording quality as much as changes in underlying behavior. This is why the proposal treats validation, auditing, and targeted supplemental primary data as important complements rather than as substitutes for the administrative backbone.
A further methodological implication is that spillovers and displacement are not peripheral concerns but central features of the design problem. The inclusion of a Training-of-Trainers and mentoring component means that knowledge and practice may spread beyond directly trained villages, and this violates the assumption that outcomes in one unit are unaffected by treatment in neighboring units. Benjamin-Chung et al. (2017) argue that this kind of interference is common in socially embedded interventions and should be anticipated rather than ignored. Aronow and Samii (2017) similarly show that when exposure can extend beyond direct treatment assignment, the relevant causal question is no longer simply whether a unit was treated, but what kind of treatment environment it experienced. Baird et al. (2018) further demonstrate that there is a fundamental trade-off between the power to detect average treatment effects and the ability to learn about spillovers. In the present study, these insights imply that the evaluation cannot rely on a naïve treated-versus-untreated distinction. It must instead be designed so that rollout timing, village adjacency, hotspot status, and mentoring exposure can all be tracked and incorporated into interpretation. This is also where displacement becomes important: if petty cases are rerouted toward neighboring untreated villages or neighboring institutional channels, then apparent “effects” may reflect redistribution rather than pure reduction. The literature therefore supports the proposal’s emphasis on tracking neighboring exposure and on distinguishing direct treatment effects from broader system effects.
Finally, the literature has direct implications for the cost-effectiveness analysis. If the intervention generates meaningful diffusion through mentoring, then the value of the model cannot be assessed only by looking at directly treated villages. Takahashi and Otsuka (2023), in their discussion of training and extension models in Sub-Saharan Africa, make this point explicitly: if spillover effects are ignored, cost-effectiveness will be underestimated because the denominator of impact is artificially restricted to direct participants. For the present evaluation, this means that cost-effectiveness should not be framed only as the cost per outcome achieved in directly treated villages, but also, where possible, as the cost relative to the broader pattern of effects created through diffusion and indirect exposure. This is particularly relevant because the CDFU model is not a pure one-shot training intervention; it is a layered capacity-building and mentoring system whose value may lie partly in its ability to extend beyond the first wave of direct treatment.








# Theory of Change and Hypotheses
## LC-Centric Theory of Change
The theory of change for this proposal is built around a simple but institutionally important proposition: if Local Council leaders are better equipped to identify, mediate, and appropriately refer petty cases, then a meaningful share of disputes that would otherwise enter the formal justice system can be handled earlier and more effectively at the community level. The intervention is therefore conceived as an upstream reform that acts on the point where disputes are first processed, rather than as a downstream effort to accelerate cases once they are already inside police stations, courts, or detention facilities.
The first step in this causal chain is the training itself. The intervention provides Local Council leaders with structured instruction, based on the official Local Council manual and related operational guidance, that is intended to improve how they distinguish between matters that fall within local jurisdiction and matters that require escalation to formal institutions. In practice, this means strengthening legal classification and triage, improving the procedural handling of petty disputes, and clarifying when referral is necessary. The underlying logic is that many justice bottlenecks are created not only by slow case processing, but by weak front-end sorting. When petty matters are incorrectly routed into the formal justice system, they consume scarce institutional resources and contribute to avoidable congestion. Conversely, when serious or sensitive matters are inappropriately retained locally, harms can be intensified rather than reduced. The intervention is therefore designed to improve both sides of the sorting problem: more accurate local retention of petty matters and more appropriate referral of serious cases.
The second step in the theory of change concerns mediation and local dispute-resolution capacity. Training alone is not expected to reduce formal-system inflow unless Local Council leaders are also able to resolve disputes in ways that parties find workable and legitimate. The intervention therefore assumes that stronger mediation capacity will increase the likelihood that petty disputes are settled locally rather than escalating into police and court channels. This is consistent with the broader literature on community justice and alternative dispute resolution, which suggests that local forums are most likely to reduce formal-system demand when they combine accessibility with credible, socially acceptable handling of disputes (Macdonald et al., 2022). In the context of Uganda’s legally pluralistic justice environment, the model does not seek to eliminate local forum choice; rather, it seeks to make Local Councils a more effective and appropriate forum for disputes that can legitimately be handled there.
If these first two steps function as intended, the immediate behavioral result should be a larger share of petty matters being managed locally and a smaller share entering the formal justice system. This is the central policy mechanism of the evaluation. The expected effect is not necessarily that the underlying number of disputes in communities declines, but that the distribution of where those disputes are handled changes in a more efficient and proportionate direction. In this sense, the proposal does not treat reduced police or court inflow as a stand-alone indicator of success. It treats it as meaningful only when accompanied by evidence that Local Councils are actually resolving eligible matters more effectively and referring inappropriate cases more consistently.
The theory of change also includes a relational and perceptual dimension. Training may affect outcomes not only because it changes what Local Council leaders know and do, but because it changes how citizens view Local Council processes. The literature on lower state courts in Uganda suggests that people choose among justice forums based not just on legal rules or convenience, but on whether institutions are perceived as fair, responsive, and likely to produce outcomes that are socially and practically acceptable (Cooper-Knock & Macdonald, 2020; Macdonald et al., 2022). For that reason, improved procedural handling at the Local Council level may produce effects partly by increasing the legitimacy of local institutions. If citizens come to see Local Councils as more reliable or more appropriate for petty matters, this may reinforce the diversion effect by shifting reporting behavior and forum choice toward the local level.
A final and distinctive feature of the theory of change is that the intervention may generate effects beyond directly treated villages. Because the model includes Training-of-Trainers and mentoring components, the proposal assumes that knowledge and practice may diffuse from directly trained Local Council leaders to nearby or linked leaders who have not yet received formal training. This has two implications. Programmatically, it means the intervention may create broader system benefits than would be expected from direct training alone. Analytically, it means that impacts may not be confined to directly treated villages and that spillovers are part of the substantive theory of change rather than merely a nuisance to be controlled away. The literature on diffusion and interference suggests that such indirect effects are especially likely in socially connected institutional environments and that they can materially affect both estimated impacts and the eventual cost-effectiveness of the intervention (Aronow & Samii, 2017; Benjamin-Chung et al., 2017; Takahashi & Otsuka, 2023).
## Causal Pathways and Assumptions
The intervention’s theory of change depends on a set of assumptions that connect the training model to the outcomes the evaluation is designed to measure.
A first assumption is that citizens will actually use Local Councils for eligible petty matters when those institutions are perceived as trustworthy, capable, and appropriate. The intervention is premised on the idea that stronger Local Council performance can alter forum choice, but this only happens if people view the Local Council process as a credible alternative to police, courts, or other informal mechanisms. This assumption is especially important in a context of legal pluralism, where disputes can be routed through multiple channels and where institutional choice depends heavily on social expectations, prior experiences, and perceived fairness. If local leaders are trained but community members continue to see Local Councils as partial, corrupt, politically captured, or ineffective, then improvements in technical capacity may not translate into measurable changes in where cases are reported and resolved. The intervention therefore assumes that at least some of the gains in classification and mediation capacity will also be visible enough to affect trust and use.
A second assumption is that the intervention improves diversion of petty matters without undermining the referral of serious or sensitive cases. This is one of the most important substantive conditions for success. The proposal is not based on the idea that all disputes should be kept away from the formal system. Rather, it assumes that better local justice means more accurate routing: petty disputes handled closer to the community, and serious cases moved upward more consistently and appropriately. If Local Council leaders begin retaining cases that should have been referred to police, courts, or specialized protection systems, then a reduction in formal-system inflow would not represent a positive reform effect. It would reflect inappropriate informalization. The intervention therefore assumes that training strengthens the boundary between what should be resolved locally and what must be escalated, rather than blurring it.
A third assumption is that administrative records can capture meaningful changes in justice flows over time. Because the evaluation is built primarily around Local Council, police, and court registers, it assumes that those administrative systems are sufficiently stable, accessible, and interpretable to reflect real changes in case handling and referral patterns. This does not mean the data are assumed to be perfect. On the contrary, the proposal explicitly recognizes that administrative data may be incomplete, inconsistently maintained, or affected by improvements in recording practices induced by the intervention itself. But for the evaluation to function, it must still be the case that changes in the relevant indicators—especially petty cases entering police and courts, and cases processed at the Local Council level—capture meaningful movement in institutional behavior rather than pure artifact. The evaluation therefore assumes not only that administrative data exist, but that they can be cleaned, harmonized, and interpreted in ways that preserve their analytical value.
A fourth assumption is that rollout fidelity can be maintained well enough for the staggered design to generate valid comparison periods. The core identification strategy depends on villages receiving training according to the assigned rollout sequence, such that not-yet-treated villages can serve as comparison units during earlier implementation phases. This requires discipline in implementation. If villages assigned to later waves are brought forward informally, if mentoring reaches them extensively before their official start, or if training timing shifts without clear documentation, the distinction between earlier and later treatment becomes blurred. The intervention therefore assumes that CDFU and the evaluation team can coordinate closely enough to preserve the sequence on which the design depends, while also recording deviations where they occur. In this proposal, rollout fidelity is not treated as a minor operational issue; it is one of the central assumptions underlying causal interpretation.
A final cross-cutting assumption is that the intervention’s effects are not fully offset by spillovers or displacement that make the treatment-comparison contrast unintelligible. The proposal already recognizes that mentoring and diffusion are part of the intervention’s logic and may produce desirable spillovers. However, this also means that untreated or not-yet-treated villages may be indirectly affected by neighboring treated villages, either through positive knowledge diffusion or through shifts in case reporting and routing. The design can accommodate some degree of this complexity, but it still assumes that exposure can be tracked and that the pattern of spillovers will not eliminate all useful contrast between villages at different rollout stages. In other words, the evaluation assumes that indirect effects will be important, but not so pervasive and invisible that the study can no longer distinguish between direct treatment, indirect influence, and no exposure.
## Testable Hypotheses
The theory of change outlined above can be translated into a set of testable hypotheses that link the intervention directly to observable changes in dispute handling, justice-system flows, and the broader institutional environment in which Local Councils operate. These hypotheses are structured so that the evaluation can distinguish between the intervention’s primary intended effect, its expected intermediate mechanisms, and the wider indirect effects that may emerge through mentoring and phased rollout.
The primary hypothesis, H1, is that villages assigned to receive the Local Council training intervention earlier will experience a reduction in the inflow of petty cases into police and court systems relative to villages assigned to receive the intervention later. This is the central impact hypothesis of the study and corresponds directly to the intervention’s prevention objective. The underlying expectation is that stronger local classification, mediation, and case handling will reduce the unnecessary formalization of eligible petty matters. In practical terms, this means that administrative records from treated villages should show a lower volume, or lower rate, of petty disputes entering the formal justice system during the period in which those villages have received the intervention and comparison villages have not yet done so.
The second hypothesis, H2, concerns the intermediate pathway through which that reduction is expected to occur. The proposal anticipates that lower formal-system inflow will not arise because disputes disappear, but because a greater share of them are handled locally and more appropriately. The expectation is therefore that treatment will increase the local resolution of petty disputes and/or improve referral quality. These are conceptually distinct but closely related mechanisms. One possibility is that trained Local Councils resolve more disputes directly, leading to a higher volume or share of petty matters handled within the village. Another possibility is that the intervention improves the sorting of cases even when local throughput does not rise dramatically, such that serious or sensitive matters are referred more appropriately while petty matters are less likely to be escalated unnecessarily. The evaluation will therefore treat increased local resolution and improved referral behavior as the key intermediate outcomes that should accompany any decline in formal-system inflow.
The third hypothesis, H3, concerns the perceptual and relational dimension of the intervention. Where measurement permits, the proposal expects the intervention to improve the legitimacy of Local Council processes and strengthen perceptions of procedural fairness. This hypothesis follows from the broader literature on legal pluralism and lower state courts, which suggests that citizens’ forum choices are shaped not only by formal jurisdiction but also by whether institutions are perceived as fair, responsive, and socially appropriate. The intervention may therefore change behavior partly by altering how people evaluate Local Councils as dispute-resolution forums. If trained Local Councils are seen as more capable, more impartial, or more consistent in handling disputes, then citizens may become more willing to bring eligible matters to them rather than bypassing them in favor of police, courts, or other forums. This hypothesis is secondary in the sense that it depends on the inclusion of primary data modules or qualitative instruments, but it remains substantively important because it helps explain whether changes in local justice flows are likely to be durable.
The fourth hypothesis, H4, addresses the possibility of spillovers through the Training-of-Trainers and mentoring structure. Because the intervention is designed to diffuse beyond directly trained villages, the proposal expects that some villages not yet scheduled for formal rollout may nonetheless experience changes in justice practices or outcomes through exposure to nearby trained leaders or mentoring relationships. In this sense, the intervention’s influence may extend beyond the villages directly assigned to early treatment. The hypothesis is therefore that some non-treated or later-treated villages may show improvements in petty-case handling, formal-system inflow, or local dispute resolution before their official rollout begins, to the extent that they are exposed to ToT mentoring or related diffusion processes. This hypothesis is important both substantively and methodologically, because it recognizes that spillovers are not simply a threat to identification but also part of the intervention’s intended mechanism of spread.
The fifth hypothesis, H5, introduces the possibility of displacement. While the intervention may reduce formal-system inflow in directly treated villages, it is also possible that some case flows are rerouted rather than reduced in absolute terms. For example, if neighboring untreated villages become a residual outlet for disputes that are no longer entering the formal system from treated villages, or if local changes in case classification alter where disputes are reported, then untreated villages near treated ones may experience temporary increases in certain types of recorded petty-case activity. The proposal therefore treats displacement as an empirical possibility rather than as an anomaly. This hypothesis matters because it allows the evaluation to distinguish between a genuine net reduction in unnecessary formal-system entry and a redistribution of justice-system demand across neighboring communities. In doing so, it strengthens the proposal’s ability to interpret both intended and unintended system effects of the intervention.

# Evaluation Objectives, Questions, and Learning Agenda
## Primary Evaluation Objective
The primary objective of this evaluation is to generate credible causal evidence on whether strengthening Local Council Court capacity through structured training and post-training support reduces the unnecessary entry of petty cases into Uganda’s formal justice system. More specifically, the study is designed to test whether villages exposed to the CDFU Local Council intervention experience a measurable decline in petty-case inflow into police and court channels relative to comparable villages that have not yet received the intervention at a given point in the rollout sequence. This objective follows directly from the current program framing, which identifies the Local Council prevention pathway as the central intervention area for impact evaluation and defines the key policy outcome as reducing pressure on formal institutions by improving upstream local dispute resolution and referral practices.
The proposal therefore treats the evaluation not as a broad assessment of justice-sector programming in general, but as a focused test of one institutional model: whether better-equipped Local Councils can act as effective gatekeepers for petty disputes. The study’s causal purpose is to determine whether the intervention changes the actual routing of cases through the justice system, rather than only improving perceptions, awareness, or program delivery processes. In that sense, the primary evaluation objective is tightly aligned with Legatum’s interest in attribution: the study is intended to estimate whether the Local Council model itself produces observable changes in formal-system case flows under real implementation conditions.
## Secondary Learning Objectives
Although the primary objective is causal attribution, the evaluation is also designed to generate a broader learning agenda that can inform implementation, interpretation, and future scale-up. The first of these secondary objectives is to understand the mechanisms through which the intervention operates. The proposal does not assume that reduced formal-system inflow, if observed, is self-explanatory. It seeks to determine whether such changes are associated with better case classification, stronger local mediation, clearer referral behavior, and more consistent local case handling. This is important because the policy value of the intervention depends not only on whether outcomes change, but on how they change and whether those changes reflect the intended prevention pathway rather than unintended suppression or rerouting of cases.
A second learning objective is to identify heterogeneity in the intervention’s effects. The proposal is built on the expectation that impacts may vary across types of villages and implementation contexts. For example, the model may function differently in hotspot and non-hotspot areas, in places with different baseline levels of formal-system reliance, or in areas where the Local Council already has stronger administrative and social legitimacy. By documenting such differences, the evaluation can move beyond an average treatment effect and generate more useful guidance on where the model works best, where it struggles, and what kinds of local conditions shape implementation success.
A third objective concerns safeguards and the quality of justice outcomes. The proposal is not simply interested in whether Local Councils handle more cases; it is also concerned with whether they do so appropriately. This includes understanding whether serious or sensitive matters continue to be referred upward when required, whether the intervention risks inappropriate informalization of cases involving violence or vulnerability, and whether the model appears to function in ways that are procedurally fair and socially legitimate. These questions are especially important in a legally pluralistic setting where local justice institutions can expand access while also reproducing local inequalities or harmful norms if safeguards are weak.
A fourth objective is to document delivery and implementation fidelity. Because the intervention includes phased rollout, mentoring, and Training-of-Trainers activity, the study must learn not only what effects occur, but also whether the intervention was delivered according to plan. This includes understanding whether villages were treated in the intended sequence, whether mentoring occurred as expected, and whether diffusion beyond directly treated villages was substantial enough to shape outcomes in non-treated or not-yet-treated areas. In this respect, implementation learning is not separate from the impact evaluation; it is essential for interpreting the results of the design.
Finally, the evaluation is intended to inform scale-up decisions. Legatum and CDFU are interested not only in whether the model works in the current implementation space, but in whether it represents a scalable and replicable approach to justice decongestion and community-level dispute resolution. For that reason, the learning agenda includes questions of operational feasibility, diffusion through mentoring, and the relationship between implementation intensity and measurable outcomes. These issues are directly relevant to future programming and also connect to the proposal’s cost-effectiveness component, which seeks to understand not just whether the intervention has effects, but whether those effects justify broader investment under realistic implementation conditions.
## Evaluation Questions
The evaluation is organized around a small set of questions that are designed to remain tightly aligned with the intervention’s theory of change while also responding to Legatum’s need for clear attribution and actionable learning. The primary question is straightforward and policy-facing: does the Local Council training model reduce the unnecessary entry of petty cases into the formal justice system? More precisely, the evaluation asks whether villages exposed to the intervention experience lower inflows of petty cases into police and court channels than comparable villages that have not yet received the intervention during the same period. This question corresponds directly to the central objective of the intervention as defined in the current program materials, namely to strengthen local case handling in ways that reduce pressure on formal institutions.
A second question focuses on the operational pathway through which any such reduction occurs. If petty-case inflow into the formal system declines, does this reflect more effective local handling and better referral decisions at the Local Council level? This question matters because reduced formal-system entry is only meaningful if it is accompanied by stronger local dispute resolution or improved sorting of cases into the correct justice forum. The evaluation therefore asks whether trained villages show evidence of increased local processing of petty cases, improved local resolution, and more appropriate escalation of cases that should be referred to formal authorities.
A third question concerns legitimacy and procedural fairness, where measurement permits. The intervention assumes that strengthening Local Councils may alter community behavior not only by improving internal case handling, but also by making Local Councils more credible and more attractive as dispute-resolution forums. The evaluation therefore asks whether the intervention appears to improve perceptions of fairness, trust, and institutional appropriateness among relevant users or community members. Because this dimension may require supplementary primary data, it is treated as a secondary question rather than a universal requirement of the design, but it remains substantively important because it helps explain whether changes in forum choice are likely to be sustained over time.
A fourth question addresses spillovers and diffusion. Since the intervention includes a Training-of-Trainers and mentoring model, the evaluation asks whether villages that have not yet received formal training nonetheless exhibit changes in case handling or formal-system inflow because of indirect exposure to treated villages. This question is important both for substantive learning and for interpretation of the causal estimates. If the intervention produces indirect effects through mentoring and proximity, those effects are part of the program’s broader value, but they also complicate the distinction between treatment and comparison villages.
A final question concerns unintended system effects, especially displacement. The evaluation asks whether changes in treated villages lead to shifts in case reporting or handling in nearby not-yet-treated villages. This possibility matters because a decline in petty-case inflow from one area could, in principle, reflect a redistribution of disputes rather than a net reduction in unnecessary formalization. Treating displacement as an explicit evaluation question strengthens the study’s ability to distinguish between beneficial diffusion, redistribution, and genuine net impact.

# Evaluation Design and Identification Strategy
## Design Overview
The preferred evaluation design for this proposal is a staggered village-level randomized rollout within the CDFU implementation areas. Under this approach, eligible village-level Local Council Court catchments are randomly assigned to different phases of implementation rather than being divided into permanently treated and permanently untreated groups. Villages scheduled to receive the intervention earlier constitute the treated group in the initial phases, while villages assigned to later phases serve as the comparison group during the period before their own training begins. As rollout proceeds, those later-wave villages also receive the intervention, and the evaluation continues to compare villages according to when they become exposed to treatment. In practical terms, this means that the study generates its counterfactual from the program’s own rollout sequence rather than from external non-program areas.
This is the most appropriate design for the current intervention for three reasons. First, it matches the actual structure of implementation. The CDFU model cannot be delivered simultaneously to all eligible villages, and the intervention is already expected to unfold in phases. A randomized rollout therefore does not impose an artificial research structure on the program; it organizes a real operational constraint in a way that creates credible comparison periods. Instead of treating phased implementation as a limitation, the design uses it as the basis for causal inference.
Second, the design is ethically and operationally more feasible than a conventional parallel-arm experiment with permanent controls. In this setting, there is a clear preference from the implementing side to expand the intervention across the eligible village universe rather than deny treatment indefinitely to a subset of communities. A staggered rollout addresses that concern because all selected villages are expected to receive the intervention eventually. The evaluation does not depend on withholding the program permanently, but only on sequencing access in a transparent and randomized way. This makes the design easier to align with implementation incentives while still preserving the logic of experimental comparison.
Third, the design is the strongest rigorous option that can realistically be implemented under the project’s financial constraints. A large-scale survey-based baseline and endline design across many communities would be difficult to sustain within the available budget and would risk shifting resources away from the elements that matter most for identification, namely rollout discipline, administrative data extraction, and implementation monitoring. By contrast, a staggered rollout design built around repeated administrative outcomes allows the study to estimate causal effects using data that are already generated by the justice system itself. The central outcomes of interest, particularly petty-case inflow into police and courts and changes in Local Council case handling, are institutional flows that are naturally captured through registers. This makes the design not only more affordable, but also more tightly aligned with the policy question at hand.
The logic of identification is straightforward. If villages are randomly assigned to earlier and later waves, and if rollout is implemented with reasonable fidelity, then differences in outcomes observed between already treated villages and not-yet-treated villages during the same period can be interpreted as the causal effect of the intervention, rather than as the result of baseline differences in crime, reporting, or administrative intensity. Randomization is what makes the groups comparable in expectation, and the phased structure is what creates repeated opportunities to observe those comparisons over time. Because the evaluation relies on administrative outcomes measured repeatedly across villages, it can detect not only whether the intervention changes formal-system inflow, but also whether those changes appear gradually, persist, or vary across stages of rollout.
## Unit of Assignment and Unit of Analysis
The core unit of assignment in this evaluation will be the village-level Local Council Court catchment. This is the most appropriate level for randomization because it matches the level at which the intervention is substantively delivered, institutionally organized, and expected to affect the handling of petty cases. The Local Council training model is not directed at isolated individuals acting independently of one another; it is designed to strengthen the functioning of a local justice forum embedded in a specific village administrative and social context. For that reason, assigning treatment at the village or Local Council Court catchment level ensures that the randomized unit corresponds to the actual locus of institutional change.
This choice also reflects the practical structure of the outcomes the study aims to measure. The intervention is expected to influence how disputes originating in a given village are classified, handled locally, referred, or escalated into the formal justice system. That means the natural observational unit for most substantive outcomes is also the village catchment. In the main analysis, the study will therefore be organized around a panel in which village-level units are observed repeatedly over time, with outcomes measured at regular intervals using administrative records. Depending on the frequency and quality of the registers, the preferred structure will likely be village-by-month or village-by-quarter observations, since this allows the evaluation to capture changes as villages move from not-yet-treated to treated status across rollout waves.
At the same time, the proposal recognizes that not all questions will be best answered at exactly the same level of aggregation. Some outcomes may need to be summarized at a slightly higher administrative level, particularly where police or court data are more consistently available or interpretable at the parish or sub-county level than at the individual village level. In such cases, the village/LCC catchment remains the unit of assignment, but outcomes may be aggregated upward for selected descriptive analyses, robustness checks, or system-level summaries. This is especially relevant where spillovers or displacement across nearby villages are likely, since some broader effects may be easier to observe when outcomes are examined at parish or sub-county level rather than only within a single village boundary.
Time will also be an explicit dimension of the unit of analysis. Because the evaluation relies on a staggered rollout, the contrast between treated and not-yet-treated villages exists within specific time periods rather than only across places. The analysis will therefore treat time not simply as background context, but as part of the design itself. Repeated village-level outcome measures over months or quarters will make it possible to compare changes associated with treatment timing, distinguish short-run from later effects, and track whether any observed changes persist, diffuse, or are displaced as rollout proceeds.
## Randomization Strategy
Villages will be randomized into implementation waves using a transparent and reproducible protocol designed to preserve comparability across phases of rollout. The basic principle is straightforward: within the eligible CDFU village roster, villages will not be selected into treatment based on need, convenience, or implementer discretion. Instead, they will be assigned at random to earlier and later waves of implementation, so that villages scheduled for later waves serve as valid comparison units during earlier stages of the rollout.
The randomization will be stratified at minimum by district and hotspot status. This is important because baseline levels of petty-case incidence and justice-system interaction are likely to differ meaningfully across Bushenyi, Sheema, and Rubirizi, and also between villages classified as hotspot and non-hotspot areas. Stratifying on these variables helps ensure that each rollout wave contains a reasonable mix of villages facing different baseline levels of risk and caseload intensity, rather than allowing one wave to become disproportionately concentrated in higher- or lower-incidence areas. This improves balance at baseline and strengthens the credibility of comparisons across treatment phases.
The assignment process will be implemented through a documented randomization script using the finalized eligible village frame. Each village will be uniquely identified in the assignment file, classified by district and hotspot status, and then allocated to a rollout wave within its stratum. The full protocol will specify the random seed, the final analytic roster used for assignment, the number of implementation waves, and the exact output tables delivered to CDFU for operational use. Once completed, the assignment file will be locked and archived to ensure that the rollout sequence can be audited and reproduced later if needed.
Because the integrity of the evaluation depends on the assignment being respected, the protocol will also include clear procedures for handling implementation changes. If a village cannot be treated in its assigned wave for operational reasons, that deviation will need to be documented formally rather than handled informally in the field.
## Role of Hotspot and Non-Hotspot Villages
Hotspot classification will play an explicit role in both the sampling frame and the randomization strategy because baseline dispute incidence is likely to shape both the intervention’s relevance and the interpretation of its effects. Villages with consistently higher levels of petty-case reporting or justice-system interaction may respond differently to the training model than villages with lower baseline case volumes. If these differences are ignored at the assignment stage, the evaluation could end up comparing earlier- and later-wave villages that face systematically different starting conditions, which would weaken both balance and interpretability. For that reason, hotspot status is not treated as a descriptive label only; it is incorporated directly into the design.
Operationally, this means that hotspot status will be determined before random assignment and used as a stratification variable alongside district. Once villages are classified, randomization will occur within hotspot and non-hotspot strata so that each implementation wave contains a mixture of both. This prevents a situation in which, for example, one wave is dominated by villages with the highest baseline case burden while another is composed mostly of lower-incidence villages. Such imbalance would make it difficult to determine whether observed differences across waves reflect treatment timing or simply underlying differences in justice demand.
Finally, hotspot status is substantively useful beyond assignment itself. It will also provide a natural dimension for heterogeneity analysis later in the study. If the intervention has larger effects in hotspot areas, that would suggest that the model is especially effective where baseline formal-system overload is greatest. If effects are stronger in non-hotspot areas, that may indicate that Local Councils are more able to absorb and resolve disputes where baseline pressure is more manageable. In either case, incorporating hotspot status directly into the design improves both the rigor of the evaluation and the policy value of the findings.
## Internal Validity and Main Threats
The proposed design is intended to maximize internal validity under real implementation conditions, but its credibility depends on how several foreseeable risks are managed. The first is non-compliance with the rollout sequence. Because the evaluation relies on comparing villages that are treated earlier with villages that are treated later, the design assumes that villages receive the intervention broadly according to their assigned implementation wave. If villages scheduled for later rollout are brought forward informally, or if implementation timing shifts without documentation, the contrast between treated and not-yet-treated villages is weakened. The proposal addresses this risk by treating rollout fidelity as a core part of the evaluation rather than as a purely operational matter. Training dates, wave assignment, mentoring activity, and any deviations from the original rollout schedule will need to be documented systematically so that treatment timing is observable and interpretable in the analysis.
A second threat, present in earlier versions of the project, was the risk of cherry-picking villages based on pre-existing levels of crime, caseload, or convenience. That risk is substantially reduced under the proposed design because eligible villages are assigned to rollout waves through a randomization protocol rather than through implementer discretion. Random assignment does not eliminate all practical differences across places, but it does remove the most important source of selection bias by ensuring that earlier and later rollout villages are comparable in expectation. Stratification by district and hotspot status further strengthens that comparability by reducing the likelihood that one phase is disproportionately composed of villages with systematically higher or lower baseline justice-system pressure.
A third set of threats concerns spillovers and measurement. Because the intervention includes Training-of-Trainers and mentoring, some villages may be indirectly exposed before their formal rollout begins. This creates a risk that not-yet-treated villages are not “pure” controls in the simple sense, which could attenuate estimated effects if exposure is ignored. The proposal responds to this by explicitly recognizing spillovers as part of the intervention environment. Rather than assuming perfect separation, the design will track mentoring links, rollout timing, and village adjacency where feasible, so that indirect exposure can be distinguished from direct treatment as much as possible. Related to this is the possibility of displacement, whereby changes in treated villages alter reporting or case flows in nearby untreated villages. This is not only a substantive question but also a validity concern, since observed effects may otherwise be misread as net reductions when they partly reflect redistribution across space. For that reason, neighboring exposure and possible displacement are built into the evaluation framework from the outset.
A fourth threat is differential record quality. Because the study is administrative-data-centered, its outcomes depend on the quality, completeness, and consistency of Local Council, police, and court records. Villages or institutions with better record-keeping may appear to have higher caseloads simply because they document more fully, while treatment itself may improve recording practices and therefore change what is visible in the data even before it changes underlying behavior. The proposal addresses this risk through standardized abstraction protocols, baseline data-quality checks, and regular auditing of registers across sites and time periods. Where feasible, changes in record quality will be monitored directly so that improvements in documentation are not confused with substantive increases or decreases in dispute volume.
## Estimands
The evaluation is designed to estimate a small set of policy-relevant quantities that correspond to the way the intervention is actually implemented. The primary estimand is the intention-to-treat effect. In this proposal, that means the difference in outcomes between villages assigned to receive the intervention earlier and villages assigned to receive it later, regardless of whether implementation in every village unfolds perfectly or with the same intensity. This is the most appropriate primary estimand for the study because it aligns with the randomization protocol, preserves the integrity of the staggered design, and answers the practical policy question Legatum and CDFU care most about: what is the effect of assigning villages to the Local Council training model under real implementation conditions.
The intention-to-treat framework is especially important in this setting because implementation is unlikely to be perfectly uniform. Some villages may receive stronger mentoring than others, some may experience minor delays in rollout, and some not-yet-treated villages may be indirectly exposed through Training-of-Trainers activity. By anchoring the main impact estimate to assignment rather than idealized treatment receipt, the evaluation avoids overstating precision and remains faithful to how the intervention is actually delivered. In other words, the primary estimate will reflect the effect of the program as implemented in the field, not the effect of a perfectly controlled version of the intervention.
A secondary estimand, if feasible, is the treatment-on-the-treated effect. This would capture the effect of actual exposure to the intervention rather than assignment alone. In this study, that requires a credible way to distinguish villages that truly received the intended treatment package—direct training plus the relevant support intensity—from those assigned to treatment but only partially reached, delayed, or weakly exposed. Because the intervention includes mentoring and indirect diffusion, defining “treatment received” will require careful operational criteria. For that reason, treatment-on-the-treated will only be estimated if implementation records are sufficiently reliable to distinguish actual receipt from assignment in a defensible way. If those conditions hold, this estimand can provide a useful complement to the primary analysis by showing the effect of realized program exposure rather than rollout assignment alone.
The proposal also treats spillover effects as substantively important, provided they can be measured with enough credibility. Spillover estimands would capture whether villages that have not yet been formally treated nonetheless experience changes in case flows or Local Council practices because they are exposed to nearby treated villages or to mentoring through the Training-of-Trainers structure. In this setting, such effects are not merely a nuisance; they are part of the intervention’s broader logic of diffusion. If measurable, these estimands will help distinguish the direct effect of formal treatment assignment from the broader indirect effects generated by proximity, mentoring, or networked exposure.
Relatedly, the study will explore displacement effects if the data permit. These estimands are intended to capture whether the intervention causes case flows to shift toward neighboring not-yet-treated villages rather than reducing unnecessary formalization overall. This matters because a decline in petty-case inflow from treated villages could reflect genuine diversion and resolution, but it could also partly reflect redistribution of reporting or case handling across space. A displacement estimand therefore helps guard against over-interpreting localized reductions as net system gains when some burden may simply have moved elsewhere.

The eligible study universe for this evaluation consists of the village-level Local Council Court catchments within the three CDFU implementation districts: Bushenyi, Sheema, and Rubirizi. The proposal treats the village or LCC catchment as the relevant operational unit because that is the level at which training is assigned, mentoring is expected to diffuse, and administrative outcomes are expected to be traced. If the frame is incomplete, inconsistent, or poorly linked to administrative outcomes, then the randomization may still be technically possible but the evaluation will be substantially weakened. Conversely, a well-constructed village-level frame creates the foundation for a disciplined rollout, credible comparison across waves, and interpretable administrative measurement over time.

## Assignment to Treatment Waves
The randomization protocol will be designed to ensure that assignment to rollout waves is both reproducible and fully auditable. Once the final analytic sample has been defined, the evaluation team will generate the village-to-wave assignment using a documented randomization script based on the cleaned village-level frame. That script will record the final list of villages included in the randomization, the stratification variables used for assignment, the number of rollout waves, and the random seed used to govern assignment. Recording the random seed and preserving the exact code used to generate the allocation are essential because they enable ex post reproduction of the assignment, verification that the protocol was followed correctly, and demonstration that rollout sequencing was not manipulated after the fact.
The documentation package will include more than the code alone. The evaluation team will produce an assignment log that records, for each village in the final analytic sample, its unique identifier, district, hotspot classification, assigned rollout wave, and any relevant balancing variables used in the randomization. This assignment log will become the master reference document for both implementation and evaluation. It will allow CDFU to know when each village is scheduled to receive the intervention and will allow the evaluation team to compare intended treatment timing with actual rollout as implementation proceeds.
The protocol will also specify how any post-randomization updates are handled. If a village must be removed, delayed, or replaced for unavoidable operational reasons, the change will not be made informally. Instead, it will be documented through a formal revision log that records the reason for the change, the date on which it occurred, who authorized it, and whether the village remains in the analytic sample. This ensures that the randomization remains a living operational tool without losing its value as the foundation of the study’s identification strategy.
## Monitoring Adherence to Assignment
Monitoring adherence to the assignment is critical because the credibility of the staggered rollout design depends not only on how villages are randomized, but on whether implementation follows that sequence closely enough for not-yet-treated villages to serve as valid comparison units. The evaluation will therefore include a structured rollout monitoring system that tracks when each village receives the intervention, which components were delivered, and whether any mentoring or follow-on support occurred outside the assigned wave.
At a minimum, implementation fidelity monitoring will compare planned and actual treatment timing. For each village, the evaluation team will record whether the training was delivered in the assigned phase, whether there were delays or accelerations, and whether the full intended treatment package was implemented. This includes not only the direct training event, but also post-training support and mentoring where relevant. Because the intervention includes a Training-of-Trainers component, fidelity monitoring must extend beyond direct delivery to capture whether villages assigned to later phases were indirectly exposed earlier than planned through mentoring or neighboring support. In this design, implementation fidelity is therefore not limited to “did the training happen?” It also asks whether treatment status remained meaningfully distinct across rollout waves.
The monitoring system will rely on several complementary sources. These are expected to include training attendance records, rollout calendars, mentoring logs, phone-based tracking used by ToTs where available, and regular coordination updates with CDFU field teams. The purpose of combining these sources is to reduce the risk that the evaluation relies solely on one implementation record that may be incomplete or overly program-facing. Where feasible, the evaluation team will also conduct periodic cross-checks between administrative rollout records and field-level reporting to confirm that villages classified as treated or not-yet-treated are being categorized correctly at each point in time.
This monitoring function serves two purposes simultaneously. First, it protects the internal validity of the evaluation by identifying cases in which the intended treatment contrast is being weakened by early exposure, delayed delivery, or informal expansion into later-wave villages. Second, it generates essential analytic information for interpreting results. If a village assigned to a later wave begins to show changes in outcomes before formal treatment, fidelity and mentoring records may help determine whether those changes reflect spillover exposure, misclassification of treatment timing, or some unrelated contextual shift. In this way, monitoring adherence to assignments is not a purely managerial exercise; it is part of the evaluation's evidence structure.













# Data and Measurement Strategy
## Primary Outcomes
The primary outcomes of the evaluation are designed to capture the central policy question of the study: whether the Local Council training model reduces the unnecessary entry of petty cases into the formal justice system. Because the intervention is intended to act at the upstream point where cases are first classified and routed, the main outcomes must reflect movement into formal institutions rather than only local institutional activity. For this reason, the primary outcome family focuses on petty-case inflow into police and courts, measured at the village or Local Council Court catchment level over repeated periods of time.
The first primary outcome is the volume or rate of petty cases entering the police system from each village catchment. Conceptually, this outcome captures the most immediate form of unnecessary formalization: disputes that move from the community level into police channels even though they may have been eligible for local handling. Depending on the structure of the police records and the feasibility of village-level linkage, this measure may be operationalized either as a count of petty cases associated with a given village over a defined period or as a rate standardized by village population or baseline case burden. Its substantive importance lies in the fact that police entry is often the earliest formal point at which petty matters begin to place pressure on the justice chain.
The second primary outcome is the volume or rate of petty cases entering courts from each village catchment. This measure captures a later stage of formal-system involvement and reflects whether disputes have moved beyond local institutions into adjudicative channels. In some settings, police and court entry may be tightly linked, while in others there may be attrition, informal resolution after police contact, or direct movement into courts through other routes. For that reason, police inflow and court inflow are treated as related but distinct indicators. Together, they allow the evaluation to detect whether the intervention changes formal-system entry at multiple points rather than only at one administrative stage.
The third primary outcome is a composite measure of formal-system entry that combines the relevant police and court indicators into a unified outcome concept. The purpose of this composite is not to replace the separate outcomes, but to provide a high-level measure of whether petty matters originating in treated villages are less likely to enter the formal justice system overall. This is particularly useful for policy communication and for cost-effectiveness analysis, where the most important question is often whether the intervention reduces the overall burden on formal institutions rather than whether effects are concentrated in one part of the chain. The exact construction of this composite will be specified in the technical annex once the structure of the administrative records is fully confirmed, but the logic is already clear: the central impact of the intervention should be observed as a reduction in the flow of petty cases from village catchments into the formal justice apparatus.

## Secondary Outcomes
The secondary outcomes are intended to capture the mechanisms through which the intervention may affect the primary outcomes, as well as the safeguards needed to interpret any apparent reduction in formal-system entry responsibly. While the primary outcomes tell the evaluation whether petty-case flows into police and courts change, the secondary outcomes help explain how those changes occur and whether they reflect the intended functioning of the intervention.
The first group of secondary outcomes concerns Local Council throughput. These indicators capture the extent to which Local Councils are actually receiving, recording, and handling cases in the period after training. At a minimum, this includes the number or rate of cases logged in Local Council registers over time. Where the records permit, it may also include the number or share of cases closed or marked as resolved. These measures are important because the intervention’s core logic is not that petty disputes disappear, but that a greater share of them is managed locally. In that sense, increases in Local Council throughput can be interpreted as evidence of substitution away from formal institutions, provided they occur alongside reductions in formal-system inflow and do not involve inappropriate retention of serious matters.
A second set of secondary outcomes concerns resolution quality and time to resolution. If Local Councils are handling more petty matters locally, the evaluation must also consider whether those disputes are being resolved in a timely and workable manner. Where the records include dates or other temporal markers, the proposal will construct time-to-resolution indicators or related proxies that capture the speed with which disputes move from registration to closure or referral. Even if full duration data are not consistently available, the presence or absence of closure markers, repeated entries, or unresolved cases over time can still provide useful information about whether the Local Council system is functioning more effectively after the intervention.
A third mechanism-focused outcome domain is referral appropriateness. The intervention is not intended to reduce formal-system inflow indiscriminately. It is intended to improve the matching between dispute type and dispute forum. Secondary outcomes in this domain therefore capture whether Local Councils appear to be referring serious or ineligible cases more appropriately while retaining petty matters that can legitimately be handled locally. Depending on the structure of the records, this may include counts or shares of cases marked as referred, the composition of referred cases by category, or indicators derived from register audits that assess whether referral patterns are more consistent with the intended legal and procedural boundaries after training.
A fourth secondary domain concerns gender- and child-sensitive handling. Because one of the major risks identified in the literature is the possibility that local institutions may absorb cases that should be escalated, especially where women, children, or vulnerable individuals are involved, the proposal includes safeguard-oriented outcomes that speak to whether sensitive cases are handled appropriately. These outcomes will depend on what categories can be observed reliably in the available records, but the underlying principle is clear: the evaluation should be able to detect whether changes in Local Council activity or declines in formal-system entry are accompanied by warning signs that serious or sensitive cases are being retained locally inappropriately. This does not require a separate large-scale gender study, but it does require the inclusion of a safeguard lens within the outcome framework.

## Spillover and Displacement Measures
Because the intervention includes a Training-of-Trainers and mentoring component, the study will not assume that villages are affected only when they receive formal training themselves. Instead, the data strategy will explicitly distinguish between direct treatment, indirect exposure, and possible displacement. Direct treatment will be defined by assignment to a rollout wave and confirmed through implementation records. Indirect exposure will be approximated using information on mentoring links, rollout timing, and village adjacency, so that villages not yet formally treated but plausibly influenced by nearby treated villages can be identified analytically. This is important because some not-yet-treated villages may begin to experience changes in Local Council practice or case flows before their official rollout date if trained leaders provide support or if practices diffuse informally across neighboring areas.
Displacement will be tracked separately from positive spillovers. The concern here is not that untreated villages may improve because of diffusion, but that changes in treated villages may shift petty-case reporting or routing into nearby untreated villages or into different institutional channels. For that reason, neighboring not-yet-treated villages will be flagged in the data architecture so that changes in their police, court, and Local Council case patterns can be monitored alongside outcomes in directly treated villages. Operationally, the study will classify villages into treatment-exposure categories using the rollout roster, hotspot status, village identifiers, and available adjacency information. These categories will later support descriptive and causal analyses of direct effects, indirect exposure, and potential redistribution of cases. This emphasis is fully consistent with the current proposal logic, which already identifies mentoring, neighboring exposure, and displacement as core features of the intervention environment rather than marginal technical issues.
## Pre-Period and Time-Series Data
A major strength of the proposed design is the ability to construct a historical baseline using administrative records that pre-date implementation. The study will rely, wherever access and quality permit, on 2024/25 administrative records from Local Council registers, police registers, and court registries to establish pre-intervention levels and trends in the main outcome domains. These pre-period data are important for several reasons. First, they allow the evaluation to assess whether villages assigned to earlier and later rollout waves look similar before treatment. Second, they improve the precision of the eventual impact estimates by providing repeated observations before and after rollout. Third, they help determine whether the available administrative systems are strong enough to support the full staggered design before large-scale implementation begins. The revised proposal materials already treat this pre-period compilation as a core feasibility milestone for both design calibration and statistical power.
The evaluation will therefore be structured as a repeated village-by-time dataset, ideally at the monthly level and, if necessary, at the quarterly level where register quality or extraction burden makes monthly aggregation less reliable. This time-series structure is essential because the intervention is phased rather than simultaneous. Villages will move from not-yet-treated to treated status over time, and the analysis depends on observing how key outcomes evolve around those transitions. The use of repeated administrative periods also makes it possible to examine whether effects emerge quickly, build gradually, or persist after rollout, while at the same time allowing the evaluation to monitor shifts in case recording quality or unusual temporal shocks that may affect interpretation.
## Supplementary Primary Data
Although administrative data will remain the backbone of the evaluation, the proposal allows for a limited amount of targeted primary data collection where it adds clear value. The first potential use of supplementary primary data is to capture legitimacy and procedural fairness, since these are important parts of the intervention’s theory of change but are not directly observable in registers. Short community or user-facing modules, if retained within budget, would focus narrowly on perceptions of fairness, trust, and willingness to use Local Councils for eligible disputes. The purpose would not be to build a large survey component, but to generate a compact mechanism-oriented lens that helps interpret whether changes in forum choice are likely to be sustainable.
A second use of supplementary primary data is to assess whether the training is changing Local Council leaders’ decision-making in the intended direction. For this purpose, the proposal anticipates short institutional tools for LC leaders, such as knowledge checks or case vignettes, that can test how participants classify disputes, decide when to mediate, and determine when referral is required. These tools are especially useful because they connect directly to the intervention’s mechanism of improved triage and referral behavior.
A third use of supplementary primary data is validation. Because the evaluation relies heavily on administrative registers, some light-touch qualitative or survey-based verification may be included to help assess whether changes in recorded outcomes reflect real changes in dispute handling rather than only improvements in documentation. In the current proposal logic, these validation-oriented components would remain deliberately small and targeted (for example, short KIIs, FGDs, or user checks) rather than developing into a parallel survey-heavy design.







# Benchmarking, Process Evaluation, and Non-Experimental Learning
While the impact evaluation component is designed to estimate whether the Local Council intervention causes a reduction in petty-case inflow into the formal justice system, the broader research agenda for this project extends beyond causal attribution alone. The program also requires a systematic evidence base that can document baseline conditions, track implementation quality, generate benchmarking data for key indicators, and support adaptive learning throughout the life of the intervention. For that reason, this proposal includes a complementary non-experimental component centered on descriptive benchmarking, qualitative inquiry, and process evaluation.
This part of the research design serves a different purpose from the experimental component. It is not intended to answer the causal question of whether the intervention worked relative to a counterfactual. Instead, it is designed to generate the baseline information and implementation evidence needed to assess program performance over time, interpret impact findings more accurately, and identify barriers, facilitators, and operational adjustments that matter for delivery. In practice, this means combining descriptive analysis of administrative and program data with selective qualitative and institutional inquiry. It also means aligning data collection with the indicator framework Legatum intends to use for program monitoring and results reporting, so that the baseline is analytically useful both for future impact estimation and for ongoing management.
## Purpose of the Benchmarking and Learning Component
The first function of this component is to establish a rigorous baseline for key outcome and output indicators linked to the Advancing Justice Uganda program. This includes indicators directly related to the Local Council intervention, such as petty-case diversion and local ADR use, but also broader indicators that Legatum is interested in tracking across the justice chain. The value of this baseline is twofold. It creates a reference point against which future changes can be measured, and it helps clarify the starting conditions under which the intervention is being introduced.
The second function is to generate structured learning on implementation. A program can fail to produce measurable effects for many different reasons: weak rollout discipline, low uptake by Local Councils, poor mentoring performance, lack of trust among community members, weak data systems, or inconsistent referral practices. A purely outcome-based impact design does not always reveal which of these factors explain the results. The process evaluation component addresses that gap by documenting how implementation unfolds in practice and by identifying the operational conditions that enable or constrain the theory of change.
The third function is interpretive. The benchmarking and process evaluation component provides the contextual and institutional evidence needed to interpret both experimental and non-experimental findings responsibly. For example, if police and court inflow appears to fall in treated areas, complementary evidence on Local Council throughput, legitimacy, referral quality, and record-keeping helps clarify whether this reflects genuine local resolution, improved triage, or potentially problematic under-referral. In this sense, the non-experimental component is not an optional add-on; it is part of the architecture that makes the overall evaluation meaningful.
## Learning and Benchmarking Agenda
The benchmarking and learning component will focus on a limited set of domains that are central to the intervention logic and to Legatum’s reporting framework. These domains include access to justice, perceived accountability and legitimacy, civic awareness of Local Council jurisdiction, gender and inclusion, institutional responsiveness, formal-system congestion trends, and cost and resource use.
In relation to access to justice, the study will examine whether dispute-resolution services and information appear to become more accessible in target areas over time. This will be assessed descriptively using partner monitoring records, selected survey-based evidence where available, and justice-sector administrative data. The aim here is not to estimate causal effects, but to document whether the implementation environment is shifting in ways that are consistent with the program’s intended direction.
A second domain concerns perceived accountability and legitimacy. Because the broader literature makes clear that local justice institutions only function as effective alternatives when they are seen as fair and credible, this domain will examine whether citizens and relevant users perceive Local Councils and formal institutions as more responsive, fair, and accountable over time. This may be measured through short perception modules, grievance records, or targeted qualitative inquiry depending on the final data plan.
A third domain concerns civic engagement and legal awareness. The intervention assumes that eligible petty disputes should be more likely to enter Local Council channels if people understand what Local Councils can and cannot do. Benchmarking in this area will therefore assess whether awareness of Local Council jurisdiction and the appropriate forum for petty disputes increases during implementation.
A fourth domain concerns gender and inclusion. This is not only a normative priority but an analytical necessity, given the risks identified in the literature around local bias, unequal treatment, and inappropriate informalization of sensitive cases. Benchmarking will therefore pay attention to whether the intervention appears to function equitably across women, youth, children, and persons with disabilities, and whether safeguards are operating as intended.
A fifth domain concerns institutional capacity and responsiveness. This part of the learning agenda focuses on whether Local Council leaders appear to improve concrete practices such as record-keeping, triage, mediation, and referral over time. It is closely linked to the process evaluation because it speaks directly to whether the training model is being operationalized as intended.
A sixth domain concerns formal-system congestion trends. Although the impact evaluation will focus on causal estimation for specific outcomes, the broader benchmarking agenda can also document descriptive trends in police and court caseload composition over time, while recognizing that many factors beyond the intervention may influence those trends.
The final domain concerns cost and resource use. Since Legatum is interested in the value-for-money implications of the intervention, the non-experimental component will also compile cost inputs needed for the later cost-effectiveness analysis, including personnel time, transport, training inputs, materials, and overhead allocation.
## Process Evaluation
The process evaluation component is designed to document how implementation unfolds in practice and how that affects both the plausibility and the interpretation of eventual results. It is especially important in this project because the intervention includes multiple operational layers: direct training, phased rollout, Training-of-Trainers diffusion, mentoring, and administrative case tracking. Even if the impact evaluation is well designed statistically, the study will remain difficult to interpret unless implementation processes are observed and understood.
A first objective of the process evaluation is to identify barriers and facilitators to implementation. This includes operational constraints such as rollout delays, staffing capacity, logistical bottlenecks, and the practical challenges Local Council leaders face in applying what they have been taught. It also includes social and institutional factors, such as community acceptance, local power dynamics, or the willingness of police and courts to recognize and support Local Council referral patterns.
A second objective is to assess the performance of the Training-of-Trainers and mentoring model. Because mentoring is one of the main pathways through which the intervention is expected to diffuse, the process evaluation will document whether it occurs as planned, whether it reaches the intended villages, and whether its intensity and quality vary across space. This is crucial both for substantive learning and for later interpretation of spillovers.
A third objective is to understand local institutional response. This includes how Local Council leaders, police actors, court officials, and community members react to the intervention, whether referral practices shift, whether Local Councils are perceived as handling disputes differently, and whether implementation creates unintended tensions or adaptations in the local justice ecosystem.
A fourth objective concerns data quality and record use. Since the broader evaluation relies heavily on administrative records, the process evaluation will also examine how registers are maintained, whether case documentation practices change after training, and whether record improvements may themselves be part of the intervention effect. This is analytically important because the same intervention that changes case handling may also improve what becomes visible in the data.

## Legatum Indicator Framework and Baseline Strategy
In addition to the broader learning agenda, the baseline will be structured so that it generates benchmarking data for the indicators Legatum intends to monitor under Advancing Justice Uganda Phase 3. Not all of these indicators are attributable within the baseline phase alone, and some extend beyond the direct scope of the Local Council intervention. However, the baseline can still establish their starting values, define the necessary data structure, and identify which indicators are best measured administratively, which require qualitative evidence, and which will only become meaningful once later evaluation phases are activated.
At the highest level, Legatum is interested in indicators such as the percentage reduction in petty cases entering the formal judicial system, reduction in remandees in the prison population, evidence of favorable conditions for preventing reincarceration, cost per petty case diverted, and evidence of increased community confidence in Local Councils. Among these, the most direct fit with the current intervention is the petty-case diversion outcome, which aligns exactly with the causal centerpiece of the proposed impact evaluation. Other higher-level indicators, especially those related to remand and reintegration, are important programmatically but extend beyond what can be causally attributed to the Local Council intervention alone in the current design. For those indicators, the baseline should be framed as descriptive and preparatory rather than as a promise of direct causal attribution.
At the outcome and output level, the baseline will be especially important for indicators related to increases in petty cases resolved at local level through ADR, increases in petty cases referred to Local Council Courts for ADR, and evidence on formal-system use and local dispute routing. These indicators are well aligned with the Local Council pathway and can be supported by the administrative and qualitative systems proposed in this technical design. By contrast, indicators tied to prison legal aid, remand duration, ex-prisoner income generation, and advocacy impacts should be treated as part of the broader program results framework rather than the direct core of this intervention-specific evaluation. Where feasible, baseline values can still be assembled descriptively for those indicators, but the proposal should remain clear about which of them are directly tied to the current intervention and which are part of a wider monitoring architecture.
The role of the baseline, then, is not simply to populate a results matrix mechanically. It is to build a coherent evidence foundation that distinguishes between what this intervention can credibly affect, what the broader program may influence through other pathways, and what kinds of data are needed to interpret both over time. In that sense, the benchmarking function of the study supports both management and rigor: it helps Legatum monitor the wider program while also preserving analytical clarity about the specific claims this evaluation is designed to test.











# Cost-Effectiveness Analysis (CEA)
The evaluation will include a structured cost-effectiveness analysis to quantify the resources required to achieve measurable reductions in petty cases entering the formal justice system. The CEA will be designed to support two complementary learning goals. First, it will generate partner-specific cost-effectiveness estimates to compare the performance of the two delivery models (CDFU and FCU) under realistic implementation conditions. Second, where intervention effects are sufficiently comparable, it will generate pooled estimates that summarize the overall cost-effectiveness of the LC training approach as a scalable prevention model. The intent is not only to rank partners, but to identify which delivery features drive value for money and what cost structure would look like under scale-up.
## Costing Method
The CEA will rely on an ingredients-based costing approach, which is transparent, auditable, and well-suited for multi-partner programs with heterogeneous delivery structures. For each partner, the evaluation team will compile a standardized cost inventory capturing the full set of resources required to deliver training and post-training support, including staff time (trainers, supervisors, coordination), transport and per diems, training materials, venue-related costs where applicable, communications and monitoring tools (including any ToT-related tracking support), and a clearly documented approach to allocating shared program management and overhead. Costs will be classified into fixed components (e.g., development/adaptation of materials, initial training setup) and variable components (e.g., per-LCC training delivery, travel, mentoring intensity), since this distinction is essential for interpreting cost-effectiveness under different scale scenarios.
## Effect Metrics
The primary effectiveness metric will be the cost per petty case diverted from the formal system, defined as the estimated reduction in petty-case entries into police/court registries attributable to the intervention relative to the counterfactual, standardized to the relevant time window. A closely related secondary metric will be the cost per formal filing avoided, which is useful when registry systems record filings more consistently than case categories or dispositions. Where data permit, additional derived metrics can be reported to support operational decision-making (for example, costs per month of reduced formal inflow, or costs per percentage-point reduction in formal entry rates), but the Scope of Work will keep the headline indicators focused and interpretable.
Because the program includes structured mentoring and diffusion components, the evaluation will also seek to incorporate spillover-adjusted cost-effectiveness estimates if spillovers can be measured credibly. Conceptually, this means that if trained LCCs improve outcomes not only in their own catchments but also in nearby or mentored LCCs, the relevant “effect denominator” expands and the implied cost-effectiveness improves. Whether and how this adjustment is implemented will depend on the feasibility of constructing a defensible exposure measure in the administrative data.
## Sensitivity Analyses
Given uncertainty in both costs and measured effects (especially in administrative-data environments) the CEA will include a set of pre-specified sensitivity analyses. On the costing side, this will test alternative assumptions about overhead allocation, the classification of costs as fixed versus variable, and the alternative costing of staff time where opportunity costs differ from financial expenditures. On the effectiveness side, sensitivity checks will examine alternative definitions of the outcome (e.g., narrower vs broader “petty” categorizations where classification is imperfect), alternative aggregation windows (monthly versus quarterly), and alternative time horizons that reflect realistic diffusion and learning dynamics after training. Where the design supports it, cost-effectiveness will be reported both using the primary intention-to-treat effect and using compliance-adjusted estimates, with clear labeling so Legatum and partners can interpret the results appropriately for budgeting and scale decisions.


















# Data Quality Assurance, Ethics, and Safeguarding
## Data Quality Protocols
Because the evaluation is anchored in administrative registers, data quality assurance will focus on (i) standardizing extraction, (ii) auditing completeness and consistency, and (iii) maintaining a clear chain of custody from source registers to cleaned analytical datasets. The evaluation team will develop structured abstraction templates and a uniform coding protocol to ensure that the same fields are captured in the same way across LCCs, police posts/stations, and courts. Field teams (or partner-designated data staff) will be trained on how to locate and interpret registers, how to record uncertain entries, and how to flag missing or inconsistent documentation. Routine audits will include re-abstraction of a random subset of registers, consistency checks across time periods, and basic reconciliation exercises across sources where linkable (e.g., verifying that escalated cases appear in police/court records when such linkage is feasible).
If any supplementary primary data are included (short surveys, vignettes, KIIs/FGDs), the evaluation will apply standard survey QA protocols, including enumerator training, piloting, programmed checks in data capture tools, and a defined plan for back-checks and spot checks. These procedures are not intended to expand field burden, but to ensure that any primary data collected are credible and usable for triangulation and mechanism interpretation.
## Ethics
The ethical approach will be proportional to the evaluation’s data collection modalities and risk profile. Where the evaluation relies exclusively on administrative records that do not include direct identifiers in the analytic dataset, the primary ethical requirements center on confidentiality, data minimization, and secure storage. The evaluation will implement clear procedures for secure transfer and storage of data, restricted access protocols, and de-identification rules prior to analysis. If identifiers are required temporarily for cleaning or linkage, they will be stored separately with controlled access and removed from analysis files as early as feasible.
Where direct interaction with individuals occurs (e.g., surveys, interviews, group discussions, or vignette-based assessments), informed consent procedures will be used, along with clear explanations of voluntary participation, non-retaliation, and the absence of any effect on access to services. The evaluation will also include a structured plan for managing sensitive information, including guidance to enumerators and moderators on minimizing harm and ensuring privacy during data collection.
The specific IRB/ethics review pathway will be confirmed during the technical proposal phase and will reflect Legatum’s requirements, partner institutional processes, and the regulatory environment in Uganda. The Scope of Work anticipates that approvals may be pursued through an appropriate Ugandan ethics body and/or a partner-linked pathway where relevant, with the objective of starting the process early to avoid timeline bottlenecks.
## Safeguarding for Sensitive Cases
Although the causal evaluation focus is on preventing petty cases from entering the formal system, the program operates in a legal pluralism environment where informal or semi-formal institutions sometimes encounter cases that should not be diverted—particularly sexual and gender-based violence (SGBV), child protection matters, and other serious offenses requiring formal referral and specialized services. The evaluation will therefore include safeguarding provisions to ensure that measurement and program learning do not unintentionally incentivize inappropriate diversion.
At minimum, we will require partners to maintain and apply clear referral protocols consistent with Ugandan law and safeguarding standards, including documentation of referral pathways to police, courts, probation/child protection authorities, and relevant service providers. Where feasible, the evaluation will include limited monitoring indicators to verify that safeguarding rules are understood and applied in practice. This safeguarding layer is essential both for ethical integrity and for correct interpretation of results: a measured reduction in formal entry is only meaningful if it reflects appropriate diversion of petty matters rather than misclassification or suppression of serious cases.

# Workplan and Timeline
## Overall Evaluation Timeline
The evaluation is structured as a multi-phase architecture that aligns the research design with the operational realities of the intervention. At the broadest level, the proposal distinguishes between three linked phases: an inception and baseline phase, a monitoring and implementation-tracking phase, and a later endline and impact-estimation phase.
The baseline phase is intended to establish the analytical and operational foundations of the study. This includes finalizing agreements, constructing and cleaning the village-level sampling frame, confirming data access arrangements, compiling pre-period administrative data, finalizing the rollout design, and generating the baseline evidence required both for benchmarking and for future causal analysis. The monitoring phase then follows the intervention as it unfolds, tracking whether the randomized rollout is implemented as assigned, whether mentoring and indirect exposure occur outside intended waves, and whether the administrative data system remains strong enough to support longitudinal analysis. The final phase of the architecture is the endline and impact-analysis stage, in which later treatment comparisons, spillover patterns, and cost-effectiveness estimates are brought together into the final impact-evaluation outputs.
This multi-phase structure is important because it makes clear that the evaluation is not a single one-off field exercise. It is a sequenced research program in which each phase creates conditions for the next. The baseline is not only descriptive; it enables randomization, validates the data systems, and defines the study universe. Monitoring is not only managerial; it protects internal validity by preserving the treatment-control contrast over time. Endline analysis is not an isolated reporting activity; it depends directly on whether the earlier phases were executed with sufficient discipline and documentation. In this sense, the workplan is part of the methodological design itself.
## Baseline / Inception Phase Timeline
This phase covers all the activities required to move the study from concept to implementation-ready evaluation design while also generating the first round of benchmark evidence. The first set of activities in this phase concerns start-up and design finalization. This includes establishing formal agreements with Legatum, CDFU, and relevant data-holding institutions; confirming the final evaluation scope; cleaning the village-level administrative frame; and aligning the rollout logic with the evaluation protocol. During this stage, the evaluation team will also finalize the randomization protocol, the measurement architecture, and the operational rules for handling deviations, spillovers, and data linkage issues.
The second set of activities concerns baseline data construction. This includes extraction and harmonization of pre-period administrative data from Local Council, police, and court records, along with any light-touch primary data modules that may be retained for mechanism measurement, validation, or benchmarking. The purpose of this work is to produce both a clean baseline dataset and a baseline report that not only describes conditions at the start of implementation but also demonstrates that the evaluation can be supported by the available data systems.
The final part of the baseline phase concerns reporting and validation. This includes baseline analysis, preparation of the draft report, discussion of findings with Legatum and CDFU, a validation and learning workshop, and finalization of the baseline deliverables. Under the current proposal logic, this phase should culminate in a baseline dataset and codebook, a baseline report, a finalized randomization protocol, and a partner-facing set of operational recommendations to support rollout discipline and data quality.
## Monitoring and Endline Phase Timeline
The next stage is a structured monitoring and implementation-tracking phase that runs alongside the staggered rollout of the intervention. The purpose of this phase is to preserve the integrity of the design by documenting actual treatment timing, mentoring activity, deviations from the assigned rollout sequence, and changes in the administrative data environment over time. This phase would also generate periodic learning outputs, such as short implementation memos or midline briefs, particularly if the rollout extends across multiple waves and years.
The endline phase would occur only after a sufficient share of the rollout has been completed and enough post-treatment time has accumulated to estimate impacts credibly. This phase would involve the final extraction and cleaning of follow-up administrative outcomes, any closing primary-data collection if retained in the design, the final impact and spillover analysis, and the cost-effectiveness analysis. It would culminate in the endline impact report, cost-effectiveness report, and dissemination outputs.
The timing of this later phase will depend on how quickly rollout progresses and how much exposure time is needed to detect meaningful changes in petty-case inflow, Local Council throughput, and referral patterns. For that reason, the proposal does not treat the endline phase as fixed to a mechanical calendar date. Instead, it is tied to implementation and exposure milestones, ensuring that the final analysis is timed to the actual dynamics of the intervention rather than to an arbitrary reporting deadline.
## Decision Gates and Dependencies
The proposed workplan depends on several decision gates that must be crossed in sequence for the later phases to remain feasible and credible. The first is data access. The evaluation cannot proceed in its intended form unless the team secures timely and sustained access to Local Council registers, police records, and court records in a format that can be linked to the village-level frame. This is the foundational dependency for both the baseline and later impact analysis.
The second is the final sampling frame. Randomization, stratification, and later outcome measurement all depend on a cleaned and validated village/LCC roster. Until that roster is confirmed, including hotspot classification and administrative identifiers, the study cannot finalize its analytic sample or assignment structure.
The third is formal agreement with CDFU on the rollout logic. Because the evaluation uses a staggered randomized rollout design, the study depends on CDFU accepting and implementing the agreed sequencing of villages into treatment waves. This is not simply an operational detail; it is the central requirement that makes the counterfactual credible.
The fourth is ethical and administrative approval. Even in an administrative-data-centered design, the project still requires data governance agreements, confidentiality protections, and, where relevant, ethics clearance for any primary-data activities. Delays in these approvals could postpone both baseline data collection and the start of implementation-linked monitoring. | Macdonald, Cooper-Knock, & Hopwood (2022) | Citizen engagement with lower state courts in post-war northern Uganda | Citizens use lower courts and mediation for a mix of normative, pragmatic, and tactical reasons. Mediation and local justice are shaped by social relationships, trust, and expectations, not just formal legal rules. People may avoid local mediation when local authorities are perceived as partial or corrupt. | The effectiveness of LC training depends not only on technical knowledge, but also on whether Local Councils are perceived as legitimate, fair, and capable of producing acceptable outcomes. | The evaluation should not only measure diversion of petty cases to local forums, but also include mechanisms related to legitimacy, procedural fairness, and forum choice. |
| Cooper-Knock & Macdonald (2020) | Citizen engagement with lower state courts in Uganda and South Africa | The procedural justice model is too narrow for understanding how people choose justice forums. Citizens weigh how institutions should act, how they expect them to act, and how they need them to act in specific circumstances. | Strengthening Local Councils may affect case flows only if the intervention changes not just capacity, but also public perceptions of fairness, usefulness, and appropriateness. | The proposal should include a conceptual focus on legal pluralism and, if feasible, light-touch primary data on legitimacy and procedural fairness. |
| Rockowitz et al. (2023) | Systematic review of criminal justice initiatives on sexual violence in East Africa | Evidence on justice-sector interventions remains limited and fragmented. Barriers include weak capacity, stigma, impunity, and poor institutional coordination. | Community justice interventions can only be beneficial if they are accompanied by clear safeguards and referral pathways, especially for sensitive or serious cases. | The evaluation must include safeguards and indicators related to referral quality, especially for SGBV and child-related cases, and avoid defining “success” as diversion at any cost. |
| Albezreh et al. (2022) | Multiyear multisectoral justice and SGBV training in Kenya | Training improved participant knowledge and was associated with better institutional coordination, confidentiality, and justice practices, although without a rigorous causal design. | Structured training can plausibly improve frontline justice actors’ ability to classify cases, coordinate across institutions, and handle disputes more appropriately. | Supports the plausibility of the intervention’s training model and justifies measuring intermediate mechanisms such as legal knowledge, referral practices, and institutional coordination. |
| Benjamin-Chung et al. (2017) | Review of spillover effects and study designs under interference | Spillovers are common in socially embedded interventions. Standard assumptions fail when untreated units are affected by treated units. Partial interference and cluster-based designs are often more realistic. | The Training-of-Trainers and mentoring model is likely to generate indirect effects beyond directly treated villages. | Supports the use of a cluster-based design and the need to explicitly anticipate spillovers, contamination, and exposure mapping in the evaluation strategy. |
| Aronow & Samii (2017) | Causal inference under interference and social-network exposure | When treatment affects neighboring or connected units, outcomes depend on exposure environments rather than simple treatment status. Exposure mapping is essential. | The CDFU mentoring structure may generate multiple forms of exposure: direct training, indirect mentoring, neighboring treatment, and broader diffusion. | The design should go beyond a simple treated-versus-untreated distinction and document treatment timing, adjacency, and mentoring links where possible. |
| Baird, Bohren, McIntosh, & Özler (2018) | Optimal design of experiments in the presence of interference | There is a trade-off between maximizing power and learning about spillovers. Designs with interference require careful structuring of treatment saturation and exposure. | The intervention may produce both direct effects and indirect spillover effects, but identifying both requires intentional design choices. | Supports a staggered rollout / phased design and reinforces the need to think carefully about what can realistically be identified under budget and operational constraints. |
| Halloran & Hudgens (2016) | Methodological review of causal effects under interference | Cluster-randomized designs remain the most common practical approach when spillovers are present, but interpretation must distinguish direct, indirect, and total effects. | The intervention’s effects may extend beyond directly trained villages, so the total policy effect may be larger than the direct treatment effect alone. | Reinforces the proposal’s emphasis on cluster-level rollout, spillover-aware interpretation, and caution in reading average treatment effects in isolation. |
| Chin (2019) | Regression adjustment for experiments with interference | Interference can be handled analytically if treatment environments are measured carefully, though this requires strong data structure and design discipline. | The intervention may require more nuanced analysis if mentoring and neighboring exposure shape outcomes. | Supports including implementation tracking and treatment-exposure documentation from the beginning, even if the final analysis remains operationally simple. |
| Ariel et al. (2018) | Justice-sector RCT using administrative outcomes (body-worn cameras in railway stations) | Administrative records can serve as credible primary outcomes in justice evaluations and can also reveal diffusion effects beyond directly treated units. | Justice-related behavioral and institutional outcomes can be measured credibly through administrative systems rather than relying only on surveys. | Supports the proposal’s administrative-data-centered design and the use of repeated official records as the backbone of outcome measurement. |
| Takahashi & Otsuka (2023) | Review of extension and training models in Sub-Saharan Africa, including Training-of-Trainers diffusion | Training often has positive effects, but credible evidence remains limited. Spillovers can be substantial, and ignoring them can underestimate cost-effectiveness. | The mentoring/ToT structure may be an important part of the intervention’s value, not just a contamination risk. | Supports explicit treatment of diffusion in both the impact evaluation and the cost-effectiveness analysis, including spillover-adjusted interpretations where feasible. |
