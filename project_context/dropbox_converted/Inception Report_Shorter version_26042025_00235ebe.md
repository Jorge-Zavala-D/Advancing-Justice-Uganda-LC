# EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This Inception Report presents the finalized framework for the Advancing Justice Uganda baseline study, including (i) an impact evaluation of the Local Council (LC) justice pathway implemented by CDFU in Bushenyi, Sheema, and Rubirizi; and (ii) the establishment of benchmark baselines for higher-level indicators. The impact evaluation employs a Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT) to generate causal evidence by comparing changes between treatment and control villages. The benchmarking baseline will aim to establish the status quo prior to program implementation, providing benchmark data for comparison with endline indicator data, to assess program performance. This report translates the earlier technical proposal into an implementation-ready design that aligns the intervention model, the evaluation strategy, the sampling and randomization framework, and the data architecture. Its central purpose is to clarify how the study will generate both rigorous causal evidence and practical implementation learning on whether strengthening Local Council Courts can reduce the unnecessary entry of petty disputes into the formal justice system and generate benchmarking data related to outcome indicators.
In Uganda, many petty disputes and low-level cases enter police and court channels even when they could potentially be resolved earlier and more appropriately at community level. This contributes to avoidable congestion, delays, and pressure across the justice chain. Within the broader Legatum program, the LC pathway is therefore treated as an upstream prevention mechanism: by improving classification, mediation, referral, and local case handling, stronger Local Council institutions may reduce unnecessary formal-system inflow while improving access to justice at the community, closer to where disputes arise.
The RCT approach centres on a structured Local Council strengthening model, comprising of three core components: (i) legal and procedural capacity; (ii) ADR and case-handling capacity; and (iii) norms and reintegration. The first stage of implementation involves direct CDFU-led training of LC leaders in randomly selected core villages. However, under the current implementation model, this direct-training stage is not the randomized controlled trial itself. Because the directly trained villages are reached broadly rather than through a clean treatment-control contrast. Therefore, stage 1 is treated as a benchmarking and mechanism-validation phase. Its function is to assess whether direct training improves knowledge, institutional functioning, referral practice, record keeping, and overall mentor readiness.
After direct training, CDFU applies a maturity and verification tool to identify the subset of trained LC leaders who are sufficiently functional and credible to act as mentors. These mentor-ready leaders then support neighboring villages, which become the experimental sample for the RCT. Eligible neighboring villages are mapped through administrative and geospatial information, organized into mentor-linked catchments, and randomly assigned to earlier versus later mentorship waves. This design allows the study to estimate the effect of the LC-led diffusion model on petty-case inflow into police and courts, local dispute handling, referral quality, and related institutional outcomes. While the CDFU proposal targets 35% of trained Local Council members to mentor others, this proportion may be insufficient to generate statistically credible inferences on spillover and diffusion effects; increasing the threshold to at least 50% is recommended to strengthen analytical power and detect meaningful effects.
Beyond the RCT, the report details approaches for a broader benchmarking, process-evaluation, and learning framework. This component will document implementation quality, mentor performance, institutional response, and trends relevant to Legatum’s wider results framework. The report additionally outlines the high-level cost-effectiveness analysis, data quality and ethics protocols, a phased workplan and timeline linking inception, baseline, randomized rollout, monitoring, and endline analysis.



# INTRODUCTION
This Inception Report is the first deliverable under the consultancy to design and implement a baseline study for the Access to Justice Program in Uganda. It draws on the inception desk review and consultations with the Legatum Foundation and CDFU held on April 9 and 29. It translates the evaluation proposal into an implementation-ready framework and serves as a reference for aligning stakeholders to the study’s scope, methods, and expected evidence.
The Advancing Justice Program Uganda is a three-years justice-sector program supported by the Legatum Foundation in partnership with six local civil society organizations working across different points of Uganda’s justice chain. The broader program is designed around a set of linked objectives: reducing unnecessary entry into prison, shortening time spent on remand through better legal assistance and alternatives to custodial processing, improving the reintegration prospects of incarcerated and formerly incarcerated individuals, and strengthening the wider Justice, Law and Order Sector through advocacy, network building, and institutional reform. Across these pillars, the program’s overarching ambition is not only to improve access to justice and reduce systemic bottlenecks, but to identify approaches that are cost-effective, scalable, and replicable over time.
Therefore, the baseline serves two primary purposes: (i) to underpin a rigorous impact evaluation of Local Council courts as a diversion mechanism using an RCT; and (ii) to establish benchmark data for assessing program performance over time through benchmarking, process evaluation, and non-experimental approaches.
## IMPACT EVALUATION
### 2.1 The intervention
Methodologically, the RCT-based impact evaluation is embedded within the intervention design and implementation. Therefore, it’s critical for the evaluation team to understand the intervention, and CDFU adhere to the intervention design and implementation. The intervention will be implemented in selected villages across Bushenyi, Sheema, and Rubirizi districts under the CDFU Local Council (LC) strengthening model. The intervention is a structured LC strengthening model, not a standalone training. It has three components: (1) legal-procedural capacity to strengthen understanding of LC mandates and legal frameworks; (2) ADR and case handling to improve mediation, triage, referral, and record-keeping; and (3) norms change to address stigma and support reintegration. The village/LC Court is the unit of analysis, as it is where training, dispute resolution, and potential mentoring spillovers occur. It has two intervention arms: (i) direct-training villages, where LC I leaders receive initial CDFU training; and (ii) neighbouring villages within the catchment of trained, mentor-eligible leaders, which form the RCT sample. Their proximity makes them the most likely to receive mentorship and allows structured exposure assignment for causal identification.
The process begins by training LC I Chairpersons in hotspot villages, who cascade knowledge to committee members and mentor neighbouring leaders. The initial training phase acts as a benchmarking stage to assess early changes and validate the theory of change, while the mentorship phase applies the RCT design to estimate the effects of mentorship.

### 2.2 Literature review and conceptual framing
Evidence on Community-Based Dispute Resolution and Diversion
Community justice in low- and middle-income settings begins from the premise that disputes are rarely processed within a single, monopolistic legal order. Instead, they are handled within legally plural environments in which state law coexists with customary, religious, kin-based, and community-level normative systems (Merry, 1988; Woodman, 1996). In African contexts, this means that access to justice cannot be understood only through the lens of formal courts. Local dispute-resolution institutions are often the first, and sometimes the only, realistic forum available to ordinary citizens. At the same time, African scholarship on ADR cautions against describing these institutions as either simple survivals of tradition or straightforward transplants of Western mediation models. Rather, local dispute resolution reflects historically rooted practices of reconciliation, consensus building, dispute avoidance, and matching disputes to the most appropriate process, while remaining deeply shaped by local authority structures and broader state formation dynamics (Grande, 1999; Maraire, 2025).
Local justice institutions matter not only because they are nearby, but because they are embedded in the social logic through which people actually choose forums for dispute resolution. Research on lower state courts in Uganda and South Africa shows that citizens engage justice institutions for normative, pragmatic, and tactical reasons, rather than simply because those institutions enjoy formal authority or abstract legitimacy (Cooper-Knock & Macdonald, 2020; Macdonald et al., 2022). Related evidence from Kosovo suggests that informal justice is strongly associated with perceptions that state justice is slow or inefficient and with local social conventions about where disputes should go (Krakowski & Kursani, 2024). Comparative work also shows that village mediation can remain durable even amid broader social and economic change when it is tied to local authority and everyday governance (Read & Michelson, 2018). In the Ugandan context, scholarship on traditional and clan-based courts further suggests that local justice institutions can possess recognizable structures of authority, internal procedure, and culturally grounded notions of fairness, even when they do not mirror the design of formal courts (Owor, 2012).
Access to justice and legal empowerment shifts attention from institutional form alone to the question of whether ordinary people can actually use legal and quasi-legal mechanisms to protect rights, resolve disputes, and make claims on authority. This work argues that justice-sector reform in LMICs cannot rely exclusively on building or reforming formal courts; it must also engage with the intermediaries, local institutions, and information channels through which poor and marginalized populations navigate disputes in practice (Maru, 2010; Banik, 2009). At the same time, this literature is appropriately cautious. Evaluations of legal empowerment and paralegal aid show that such interventions can expand knowledge, improve access, and widen the menu of dispute-resolution options, but that gains are often incremental and constrained by local power asymmetries, weak incentives, and institutional limits (Khair, 2009; Stephens, 2009; Swenson, 2018).
Community-based dispute resolution, mediation, and diversion provides cautious but important support for the proposition that local and non-adversarial justice mechanisms can alter real behavior. Experimental evidence from Liberia shows that training local leaders in ADR practices increased dispute resolution and reduced violence, while also revealing unintended effects that underscore the need for safeguards and careful case boundaries (Blattman et al., 2014). Evidence from court-connected mediation and restorative or diversionary justice programs in other settings suggests that alternative processes can improve settlement, accelerate case disposition, and in some cases reduce repeat offending, although effects are heterogeneous and strongly shaped by implementation quality and case type (Grajzl et al., 2026; Sherman et al., 2015; Beckman et al., 2024; McCart et al., 2022).
Theoretical Mechanisms
The first mechanism is classification and triage. At the most basic level, the intervention assumes that better-trained LC leaders are more capable of distinguishing among disputes that can be handled locally, disputes that require mediation but not formal escalation, and disputes that must be referred onward because they fall outside local jurisdiction or involve greater seriousness. This mechanism is central in legally plural settings because poor forum choice is often driven by uncertainty about which institution is appropriate, by weak local capacity to sort cases, and by the absence of clear boundaries between community handling and formal justice. Evidence from community dispute-resolution interventions and multisectoral justice training suggests that training can improve knowledge, case processing, and the practical ability of frontline actors to handle and route cases more effectively, although the quality of that triage remains highly consequential for both efficiency and rights protection (Blattman et al., 2014; Albezreh et al., 2022; Mennen, 2010).
The second mechanism is mediation quality. The relevant question is not only whether LC leaders attempt to mediate disputes, but whether they do so in ways that improve bargaining, reduce escalation, and generate outcomes that disputants perceive as workable and legitimate. The broader ADR and restorative justice literature suggests that community-based dispute resolution is most likely to matter when mediators can facilitate voice, structure negotiation, and move parties toward repair or agreement rather than simply impose informal authority. Experimental and review-based evidence shows that such approaches can improve dispute resolution, case disposition, victim experience, and in some settings reduce repeat offending, but also that these gains depend heavily on the quality and credibility of the process itself (Menkel-Meadow, 2007; Braithwaite, 2021; Sherman et al., 2015; Grajzl et al., 2026).
A third mechanism runs through legitimacy and procedural fairness. People’s willingness to use, accept, and comply with justice institutions depends not only on outcomes, and LC leader’s capacity but also on whether they perceive those institutions as fair, respectful, impartial, and trustworthy. Procedural justice theory emphasizes voice, neutrality, dignity, and trustworthy motives as core dimensions through which authorities build legitimacy, while more context-sensitive work in Uganda and comparable settings shows that these dynamics are filtered through practical concerns about effectiveness, corruption, and social necessity (Tyler, 1988; Tyler, 2017; Jackson, 2018; Cooper-Knock & Macdonald, 2020; Macdonald et al., 2022). In the context of this program, the implication is that stronger LC leaders may shift dispute-routing behavior not only because they know more law or procedure, but because communities may become more willing to bring petty matters to local forums if those forums are perceived as more competent, fair, and usable.
A fourth mechanism is coordination and referral, which links local justice to the wider system. The intervention is not designed to replace police or courts, but to improve the interface between community-level handling and formal institutions by making referral more timely, documented, and appropriate. This matters because one of the core bottlenecks identified in the regional justice literature is weak cross-institutional coordination: even where local actors or frontline providers are trained, gains can be undermined by poor referral pathways, fragmented institutional roles, and breakdowns between community institutions and formal justice actors (Albezreh et al., 2022; Rockowitz et al., 2024).
Finally, the  program also depends on a mechanism of diffusion through mentors. The second-stage intervention assumes that practices acquired by directly trained and higher-performing LC leaders can be transmitted outward to neighboring villages through mentorship, and repeated support. The logic is consistent with evidence from extension and intermediary-actor literatures showing that local lead actors can generate meaningful spillovers when they are credible and capable, but that diffusion is neither automatic nor uniform and depends on both the quality of the intermediary and the structure of local networks (Swenson, 2018; Takahashi & Otsuka, 2023).
Risks and Boundary Conditions
Community-based justice institutions should not be treated as uniformly empowering or benign. One major risk is elite capture: LCs may use their authority, training, or external legitimacy to reinforce existing power structures rather than challenge them. This concern appears both in critical work on community mediation, which warns that informal justice can operate as a mechanism of regulation and discipline rather than simple empowerment (Pavlich, 1996), and in more applied studies of intermediary justice actors, which show that paralegals and locally embedded justice brokers often face principal-agent problems and may end up bolstering rather than transforming local hierarchies (Swenson, 2018). In the context of this program, stronger LC leaders may not automatically produce fairer justice unless training is accompanied by clear procedural expectations, monitoring, and safeguards around how authority is exercised.
A second set of boundary conditions concerns gender bias and the inappropriate informalization of serious cases. Local institutions can be more accessible than formal courts while still failing women, children, and other vulnerable groups, especially in cases involving gender-based violence or severe abuse (Mennen, 2010; Rockowitz et al., 2024). In such contexts, top-down removal of local jurisdiction may also fail if it simply drives cases underground rather than into functioning formal channels (Mennen, 2010). The implication for the present intervention is that improved local handling of petty disputes can only be considered successful if it is paired with more appropriate referral of serious or legally ineligible matters. In other words, the study must remain attentive to the possibility that better functioning local justice could still generate harm if it expands into informal handling of cases that should not be resolved locally.
A final set of risks relates to weak local legitimacy, spillovers, and displacement. Local and lower-level courts in Uganda and other LMICs are used for normative, pragmatic, and tactical reasons, meaning that communities may engage them even when they are not widely trusted or when they are valued mainly for strategic reasons rather than perceived fairness (Cooper-Knock & Macdonald, 2020; Macdonald et al., 2022). This means that improving technical capacity alone may not be sufficient to change the community choices unless people also experience local institutions as usable, fair, and effective. At the same time, the intervention’s mentorship model creates the possibility of spillovers beyond assigned villages, which is substantively meaningful but methodologically challenging. Evidence from Liberia suggests that ADR-type interventions can spread beyond those directly trained, but also generate unintended effects such as more extrajudicial punishment or the activation of disputes that had previously remained dormant (Blattman et al., 2014). For this reason, the study must explicitly consider both positive diffusion and negative displacement: mentoring may improve neighboring practice beyond treated villages, but it may also contaminate comparison areas or shift disputes across village boundaries in ways that complicate attribution and raise important substantive concerns.
### 2.3 STUDY DESIGN AND IDENTIFICATION STRATEGY
The evaluation team has approached this assignment as part of a broader impact evaluation, rather than as a standalone baseline study. While the primary deliverable is the design and implementation of a baseline assessment, the approach is intentionally framed to align with a rigorous impact evaluation strategy and future causal analysis.
Design Overview
The study follows a two-part evaluation design that reflects the final implementation architecture of the intervention. The first part focuses on the direct training of core LC leaders in the selected CDFU villages. This stage is used for benchmarking and mechanism validation: it documents whether direct training improves legal-procedural knowledge, institutional functioning, record keeping, case handling, and overall mentor readiness among the first group of trained LC leaders. Its purpose is therefore to establish whether the intervention is generating the expected early changes in the core villages and whether a sufficiently strong pool of mentor-ready LC leaders emerges from that process.
The second part of the design is the randomized rollout of mentorship to neighboring villages, which constitutes the main causal component of the study. Once mentor-ready LC leaders have been identified, neighboring eligible villages become the experimental sample, and mentorship exposure is assigned in a structured and randomized way. Villages receiving mentorship earlier are compared with otherwise eligible villages that have not yet been mentored during the same period. This design allows the study to estimate the effect of the LC-led diffusion model on local dispute handling, referral quality, and the inflow of petty cases into police and courts, while preserving a practical and policy-relevant pathway for later scale-up.
A central implication of this two-part design is that the direct-training stage is not treated as the randomized controlled trial itself. Under the current implementation model, the directly trained LC leaders in the core villages are reached broadly as part of the programme’s first operational layer rather than through a design that creates a clean treatment-control contrast. Because these villages are not being assigned to immediate versus delayed training under comparable randomized conditions, the study cannot use that stage alone to generate a credible causal estimate of the effect of direct training. For that reason, the direct-training component is interpreted as a benchmarking and mechanism-validation stage, not as the main source of causal attribution.
The first stage remains analytically essential because it provides the evidence needed to determine whether the intervention is functioning as intended before the mentorship rollout begins. Data from the directly trained villages are used to assess whether LC leaders improve in legal-procedural knowledge, committee functioning, record keeping, case handling, and referral practice, and whether those improvements are strong enough to produce a pool of mentor-ready LC leaders.
The preferred experimental design is therefore located in the second-stage mentorship rollout to neighboring villages. Under this design, the neighboring villages linked to mentor-ready LC leaders constitute the experimental sample. These villages are the units through which the study can create a credible treatment-control contrast, because mentorship exposure can still be assigned in a structured and randomized way. Depending on the final operational arrangement, this randomization may take the form of assignment to earlier versus later mentorship, randomized rollout order within a mentor catchment, or phased assignment across eligible neighboring villages. In all cases, the key design principle is the same: the study exploits randomized variation in when or whether neighboring villages receive mentor support during a given observation window.
This means that villages assigned to later mentorship serve as not-yet-treated controls for villages assigned to earlier mentorship. The resulting comparison identifies the effect of the LC-led diffusion model rather than the effect of centralized direct training alone. In substantive terms, the study is therefore designed to test whether mentor-ready LC leaders can transmit improved practices to neighboring communities in ways that strengthen local dispute handling, improve referral quality, and reduce unnecessary petty-case inflow into police and courts.
Evaluation objectives
The study has one primary causal objective, alongside a set of secondary learning objectives and a broader set of implementation and scale-up learning objectives. Distinguishing these objectives clearly is important because the study is designed to generate different types of evidence at different stages of the intervention.
Primary causal objective. The primary objective of the study is to estimate whether the mentor-led rollout to neighboring villages changes how petty disputes are handled and routed through the justice system. More specifically, the evaluation seeks to identify whether assignment to mentorship support reduces the unnecessary inflow of petty cases into police and courts by improving the capacity of neighboring Local Council Courts to classify, mediate, document, and appropriately refer disputes. This is the central causal question of the study and the core focus of the randomized evaluation design.
Secondary learning objectives. In addition to the main causal objective, the study aims to generate learning on the mechanisms through which the intervention may operate. This includes understanding whether direct training improves the readiness and institutional quality of core LC leaders, whether mentored villages improve in case classification, ADR practice, referral quality, and record keeping, and whether changes in procedural fairness, trust, or local legitimacy accompany changes in dispute-routing behavior where these dimensions are measured. These objectives are important because they help explain not only whether the intervention works, but also how and under what conditions it produces change.
Implementation and scale-up learning. A third objective of the study is to generate practical evidence for programme refinement and future expansion. It examines whether the mentor-selection process yields a strong cadre of local mentors, whether the mentorship model can be delivered with fidelity across villages, what operational constraints arise (e.g., geography, transport, timing, record quality), and whether mentor-led expansion is a realistic and scalable pathway for strengthening community justice beyond initially trained villages. Overall, the study aims not only to estimate impact but also to inform implementation quality, delivery design, and long-term scalability.
Evaluation Questions and Hypotheses
The evaluation design gives rise to a focused set of research questions that reflect the two-stage architecture of the study while keeping the main analytical emphasis on the mentorship RCT. At the broadest level, the study asks;
Does the Local Council training reduce the unnecessary entry of petty cases into the formal justice system?
Does a reduction in petty-case inflow into the formal system declines, reflect a more effective local handling and better referral decisions at the Local Council level? (operational pathway)
Does strengthening Local Councils lead to increased community confidence in their ability to deliver fair, impartial, and appropriate dispute resolution?
Within these overarching questions, the evaluation is organized around the following more specific questions:
Does assignment to the mentorship intervention in neighboring villages reduce the inflow of petty cases into police and courts?
Does mentorship improve the quality of local dispute handling, including case classification, mediation, referral decisions, and record keeping?
Does mentorship improve community-facing dimensions of local justice, such as perceived procedural fairness, trust, or local legitimacy, where these outcomes are measured?
Do the effects of mentorship extend beyond directly assigned villages through spillovers, informal diffusion, or broader changes in local practice?
Does the intervention generate displacement effects, such that disputes shift into nearby untreated villages or other forums rather than being resolved more appropriately overall?
To what extent do changes in perceived legitimacy and fairness of local council leaders influence community preferences for resolving petty cases through Local Councils rather than formal justice institutions?

Theory of Change
LC-Centric Theory of Change. The theory of change reflects the two-stage architecture of the study and the practical reality of how the intervention will unfold. Rather than assuming that direct training alone can provide the main causal contrast, the revised framework treats the first stage as the mechanism-building and readiness stage, and the second stage as the diffusion and causal-testing stage. The core logic is that direct training by CDFU strengthens the legal, procedural, and organizational capacity of selected Local Council leaders in the core villages; that a subset of these leaders, once assessed as mentor-ready, becomes capable of transmitting improved practices to neighboring villages; and that this second-stage mentorship leads to better local classification, mediation, referral, and record keeping in those neighboring communities. Unit of Assignment and Unit of Analysis
A clear distinction between the unit of assignment and the unit of analysis is essential for the credibility and interpretability of the study. Because the intervention is delivered through Local Council leaders and is expected to influence dispute handling at the level where cases are first received, discussed, recorded, and referred, the design should be anchored at the village-level Local Council Court catchment rather than at the level of individual respondents or higher administrative units. This is the level at which mentorship can be assigned meaningfully, and it is also the level at which the main outcomes of interest are most naturally expressed.
Unit of assignment: the neighboring village / LCC catchment is the most appropriate unit of assignment for the mentorship-stage RCT. This is the unit that can be assigned to earlier or later mentorship exposure and the level at which the treatment is expected to alter local case handling, ADR practice, referral decisions, and record-keeping routines.
Unit of analysis: the main unit of analysis should be the village / LCC-by-period, where each village catchment is observed repeatedly over time using administrative records and, where relevant, supplementary measurement. This allows the study to track changes in local justice outcomes before and after mentorship exposure while preserving the village as the core analytical unit.
In practice, this implies that the dataset should be constructed as a panel organized around village-level LCC catchments observed across multiple periods, with outcomes such as petty-case inflow, local case volume, referrals, or record quality attached to each village-period observation. Where administrative realities require it, outcomes may also be aggregated to the parish, sub-county, or mentor-cluster level for selected robustness checks or descriptive summaries, but such aggregation should remain secondary to the village-level design. Keeping the unit of assignment and the primary unit of analysis aligned in this way strengthens the internal logic of the evaluation, reduces ambiguity in interpretation, and makes it easier to connect treatment exposure directly to the operational setting in which the intervention is expected to work.
Internal Validity and Main Threats
The proposed design is intended to maximize internal validity under real implementation conditions, but several foreseeable threats must be managed explicitly. The first is noncompliance with mentorship assignment. Because the study relies on comparing villages assigned to earlier mentorship with villages assigned to later mentorship, the design assumes that rollout broadly follows the randomized sequence. If villages scheduled for later exposure are mentored earlier than planned, or if rollout timing shifts informally without documentation, the contrast between treated and not-yet-treated villages will be weakened. A second threat is differential mentor quality. Even after the maturity-based selection process, mentor-ready LC leaders may still vary in skill, effort, credibility, and capacity to transmit the intervention with fidelity. A third threat concerns spillovers, especially where mentors or neighboring communities interact outside the assigned sequence. Indirect exposure before formal rollout may attenuate measured effects if not tracked explicitly. These concerns are fully consistent with the broader methods literature on interference, which shows that spillovers can bias standard treatment estimates when they are ignored and that variation in exposure is substantively important in its own right (Aronow & Samii, 2017; Baird et al., 2018).
A fourth threat is record-quality change. Because the study is administrative-data-centered, treatment may improve what is recorded before it fully changes underlying behavior. Villages with better documentation may appear to have more disputes simply because they record more consistently, while mentored villages may show changes in reported case volumes partly because record keeping improves. A fifth threat is selective mentor activation, whereby some mentor-ready LC leaders may become much more active than others due to transport constraints, local motivation, institutional support, or social ties. This would generate variation in treatment intensity even within the same assigned wave. For that reason, the evaluation should treat mentoring logs, timing records, village adjacency, and basic indicators of mentor activity as part of the core design safeguards rather than as secondary implementation details. Earlier project documents already identified rollout fidelity, spillovers, displacement, and differential record quality as central threats to interpretation in this study, and the same logic remains fully applicable under the final mentorship-based design.

Estimands
At a high level, the study is designed to estimate a small set of clearly defined causal quantities tied to the mentorship-stage rollout. The main estimand is the intention-to-treat (ITT) effect of assignment to earlier mentorship exposure among eligible neighboring villages. This is the most policy-relevant and design-consistent estimand because it captures the effect of being assigned to receive the mentorship model under real implementation conditions, regardless of whether the full intended intensity of mentoring is ultimately delivered in every case. In a setting where some variation in mentor activity, timing, and local uptake is likely, the ITT provides the most credible and transparent estimate of what the program accomplishes when implemented through the actual assignment process.
Where implementation records are sufficiently strong, the study may also estimate a treatment-on-the-treated (ToT) effect. This would capture the effect of actual mentorship exposure rather than assignment alone, using randomized assignment as the source of exogenous variation where appropriate. The ToT is useful because it can help distinguish between a weak intervention model and weak implementation of an otherwise promising model. However, it should be treated as secondary to the ITT, since its credibility depends on the quality of exposure measurement and on a sufficiently clear distinction between assigned and actual treatment status. More generally, because the mentorship model is expected to diffuse through socially and geographically connected villages, the study will also pay attention to spillover effects and displacement effects. Spillover effects refer to changes in villages not formally assigned to early mentorship but potentially affected through proximity, informal mentoring, or broader diffusion of practices. Displacement effects refer to the possibility that cases shift across village boundaries or into alternative forums, so that apparent improvement in one location partly reflects relocation rather than net resolution of the underlying justice burden.

Identification strategy-Sampling Strategy, Sampling Frame, and Randomization
The study is organized around three nested universes, each with a distinct role in the evaluation design.
First, the universe of directly trained villages consists of the core villages whose LC I representatives receive direct CDFU training as part of the programme’s first implementation layer in Bushenyi, Sheema, and Rubirizi. These villages are not the main experimental sample. Instead, they constitute the first-stage implementation universe from which benchmarking evidence, maturity assessment, and mentor selection are derived.
Second, the universe of candidate neighboring villages consists of villages located within the practical mentoring reach of the directly trained core villages, and more specifically within the catchments of those core villages whose LC leaders are ultimately classified as mentor-ready. This universe is defined geographically and operationally rather than purely administratively: these are the villages that could realistically be reached by mentor-led support under the intended expansion model.
Third, the final analytic sample for the RCT is the subset of candidate neighboring villages that satisfy the study’s eligibility criteria and are retained in the final randomization frame. These villages form the experimental sample for the mentorship-stage evaluation. They are the units that can be assigned to earlier versus later mentorship exposure and therefore constitute the population through which the causal effect of the mentor-led diffusion model will be identified.
Sampling frame construction and Randomization Strategy
The randomization strategy for the mentorship-stage RCT is designed to be both methodologically credible and operationally simple. Once the final pool of mentor-ready LC leaders has been identified, the evaluation team will use geographic and administrative data to map the set of eligible neighboring villages linked to each mentor village or mentor catchment. These neighboring villages will then be randomly assigned into rollout waves. In the simplest version of the design, villages assigned to the first wave will receive mentorship first, while villages assigned to the second wave will serve as the not-yet-treated comparison group during the initial evaluation window. This creates a clear treatment-control contrast without requiring the permanent exclusion of any eligible neighboring village from the intervention.
Randomization will not be conducted as a single undifferentiated lottery across all villages. Instead, it should be stratified or balanced across a small number of dimensions that are likely to matter both for implementation and for outcome comparability. The most important dimensions are the following:
District. Villages should be balanced across Bushenyi, Sheema, and Rubirizi so that early and later mentorship exposure is not concentrated disproportionately in one district.
Hotspot status. Where hotspot classification is available, neighboring villages should be balanced across hotspot and non-hotspot categories to ensure comparability in underlying dispute intensity and justice demand.
Mentor cluster or catchment. Since neighboring villages are linked to specific mentor-ready LC leaders, assignment should be structured within mentor clusters or catchments so that each mentor has a defined set of early-wave and later-wave villages where relevant and feasible.
Parish or sub-county balancing. Where the number of villages allows it, the randomization should also seek approximate balance across parish or sub-county units in order to reduce the risk that administrative geography drives treatment differences.
In practical terms, this means that the randomization will proceed in three steps. First, the final neighboring-village frame will be constructed using administrative and geospatial information to identify which villages are eligible and linked to each mentor catchment. Second, villages will be grouped within the relevant stratification cells, most importantly district, hotspot status, and mentor cluster. Third, villages within those cells will be randomly assigned into Wave 1 or Wave 2, or into early versus later mentorship order if the final operational model requires more than two waves. This approach keeps the design transparent and reproducible while ensuring that treatment assignment remains closely tied to the real implementation geography of the project.
The main implication of this strategy is that the study identifies the effect of earlier mentorship exposure relative to later mentorship exposure among otherwise eligible neighboring villages.
The neighboring-village sampling frame will be constructed only after the direct-training phase and maturity assessment have identified the final pool of mentor-ready LC leaders. The frame will then be built outward from those mentors, using administrative and geospatial information to identify the villages that lie within each mentor’s feasible support area. In practical terms, this means that neighboring villages will be linked either directly to a specific mentor village or to a defined mentor catchment anchored in the directly trained core village.
The frame should be constructed using four core sets of information. The first is administrative identifiers, including district, sub-county or division, parish or ward, and village name, so that all candidate villages can be located unambiguously within the local administrative structure. The second is geographic proximity, which will be used to determine which villages qualify as true neighbors for mentorship purposes. This should rely on the best available geospatial and local administrative data to ensure that “neighboring” reflects actual mentoring feasibility rather than abstract map adjacency. The third is hotspot classification, where available, so that the frame preserves information on underlying dispute intensity and can later support balanced randomization. The fourth is feasibility constraints, including travel distance, transport costs, and the practical number of villages that a given mentor can reasonably support within the implementation period.
The resulting frame should therefore not be treated as a simple list of all nearby villages. It is a structured operational dataset that links each eligible neighboring village to a mentor or mentor cluster, records the core geographic and administrative identifiers needed for randomization, and preserves the variables required for stratification and implementation planning. This frame then becomes the basis for selecting the final analytic sample and assigning villages into mentorship waves.

Mentor-linked neighborhood structure
The neighboring-village structure of the study is organized around mentor-linked local clusters. Each cluster begins with a directly trained core village whose LC leader has been classified as mentor-ready through the maturity and verification process. Around that core village, a set of neighboring eligible villages is identified using administrative and geospatial information. In this way, neighboring villages are not treated as a single pooled population detached from implementation reality, but as villages nested within the practical catchment of a specific mentor or core village. This structure reflects the actual delivery model of the intervention, in which support is expected to diffuse outward through local proximity rather than through centralized assignment to arbitrary villages.
This mentor-linked structure matters for both implementation and analysis. Operationally, it ensures that the mentoring model remains feasible in terms of distance, time, and support intensity. Analytically, it creates a natural intermediate structure between the individual village and the full study sample, allowing the evaluation to account for mentor-specific delivery conditions, local clustering, and potential within-catchment spillovers. For this reason, the final dataset should preserve both the village-level unit and the mentor-cluster identifier, so that villages can be analyzed as individual observations while still being linked to the mentor structure through which treatment is delivered.
Final Analytical Sample
The final analytic sample for the RCT will be drawn from the neighboring-village frame after applying the study’s eligibility and feasibility criteria. In practical terms, this means that not every village appearing in the broad neighboring-village universe will necessarily enter the final sample. Villages will be retained only if they are clearly linked to a mentor-ready core village, satisfy the proximity criteria for realistic mentorship, have sufficiently reliable administrative identifiers, and can be incorporated into a rollout plan that is operationally feasible for CDFU and the mentor structure.
Where the neighboring-village frame is larger than what can be supported within the implementation period, the evaluation team will select the final sample in a way that preserves balance across the key design dimensions already identified: district, hotspot status, and mentor cluster or catchment, with parish or sub-county balance used where feasible. The goal is to retain a sample that is large enough for meaningful comparison while remaining closely aligned with the actual mentoring capacity of the programme. The final analytic sample should therefore be understood as the subset of eligible neighboring villages that can both sustain a credible randomized comparison and realistically receive the intervention under field conditions.
Assignment to mentorship waves
Once the final analytic sample has been confirmed, villages will be assigned to mentorship waves through a transparent and reproducible randomization process. The simplest design is a two-wave structure. Under this approach, neighboring villages within the final frame are randomly divided into an earlier mentorship group and a later mentorship group. Villages assigned to the first wave receive mentor support first, while villages assigned to the second wave serve as the not-yet-treated comparison group during the initial evaluation window. If implementation realities require a finer sequencing structure, the same logic can be extended to more than two waves, but the core principle remains unchanged: randomization determines the order of exposure among otherwise eligible neighboring villages.
Operationally, the randomization should proceed in a small number of clear steps. First, the final frame is sorted by the agreed stratification variables, especially district, hotspot status, and mentor cluster. Second, villages within those strata are assigned a random number using reproducible code and a fixed random seed. Third, villages are ordered by that random number and allocated into Wave 1 and Wave 2, or into the required number of rollout groups. Fourth, the resulting assignment is logged, archived, and communicated to CDFU in a format that is operationally usable but preserves the integrity of the design. This procedure keeps the assignment simple enough to implement in practice while ensuring that the treatment-control contrast remains transparent and defensible.

The randomization protocol will be implemented through a simple but fully reproducible procedure. Monitoring adherence to assignment
Adherence to assignment will be monitored by comparing the planned mentorship sequence with the actual mentorship sequence recorded during implementation. At minimum, the study will require a dated mentorship tracking system showing which mentor visited which village, when the visit occurred, what type of support was delivered, and whether follow-up support took place. These logs should be maintained by the implementation team and periodically shared with the evaluation team for verification.
Where possible, adherence will be checked through multiple sources rather than a single implementation record. These sources may include mentor activity forms, village-level confirmation by LC representatives, dated meeting or training registers, and any available record of post-mentorship activity in LCC books or administrative forms. The purpose is not only to verify that mentoring occurred, but to verify that it occurred in the assigned village and within the assigned time window. Any deviations from assignment should be logged systematically, since they will matter for later interpretation of treatment fidelity and for any secondary ToT analysis.
Statistical Power logic, and Minimum-Detectable-Effects assumptions
Detailed power calculations will be presented in the appendix, but the main text should clarify the planning logic and the core assumptions that will guide those calculations. Because this is a village-level clustered design, power will depend primarily on four factors: the baseline petty-case rate, the intracluster correlation coefficient (ICC), the number of villages available for comparison, and the minimum detectable effect (MDE) judged substantively meaningful. The cluster-trial literature makes clear that ICC is a central input for power and sample-size planning, but also that ICCs are highly context- and outcome-specific and should therefore be treated through scenario analysis rather than as a single fixed universal value. That same literature also shows that process variables often exhibit higher ICCs than substantive outcome variables, reinforcing the need to test low, medium, and high ICC scenarios in the appendix.
For planning purposes, the power appendix should start from the cleaned administrative baseline and use observed 2024/25 petty-case rates to estimate the mean and dispersion of the primary outcome. Given the current implementation model, a practical planning assumption is that only a subset of the 100 directly trained villages will generate mentor-ready LC leaders. A conservative scenario would assume a 50% mentor conversion rate, yielding roughly 50 active mentors. If each active mentor can support approximately 2 to 3 neighboring villages per wave, the initial treatment wave would include roughly 100 to 150 neighboring villages, with a comparable number of later-wave villages serving as the not-yet-treated comparison group where available. An upper-bound scenario could test larger catchments, for example up to 5 neighboring villages per mentor, but this should be treated as optimistic rather than central. In the absence of Uganda-specific prior ICC estimates for village-level petty-case outcomes, the appendix should report sensitivity to at least a low, moderate, and high ICC range (for example, 0.01, 0.05, and 0.10) and present corresponding detectable effect sizes, such as percentage reductions in petty-case inflow or incident-rate ratios for the main count outcome. This will allow the final design decision to be grounded in the cleaned baseline data while remaining transparent about uncertainty in the core statistical assumptions.

Data and Measurement Strategy
Primary Outcomes
The primary outcomes of the study are designed to capture the central policy question motivating the intervention: whether stronger local justice capacity reduces the unnecessary entry of petty matters into the formal justice system. For that reason, the primary outcome family is centered on formal-system case inflow, measured through administrative records rather than self-reported perceptions. This approach is consistent with the broader empirical literature on mediation, diversion, and community justice, which treats observable changes in case disposition and institutional routing as the most policy-relevant outcomes when the goal is to assess whether alternative dispute-resolution mechanisms alter the burden placed on formal justice institutions (Blattman et al., 2014; Grajzl et al., 2026; McCart et al., 2022).
The first primary outcome is the number or rate of petty cases entering police channels from each village/LCC catchment during a given observation period. The second is the number or rate of petty cases entering court channels over the same period. Because the intervention is designed to affect early case routing, both outcomes should be interpreted as measures of whether eligible minor disputes continue to escalate into formal institutions or are instead managed more appropriately at local level. In practice, the exact operationalization of “petty cases” will need to follow the case categories available in police and court registers, together with the legal and programmatic definitions used in the intervention materials. To reduce dependence on any single source, the study should also construct a composite formal-system entry measure, combining the police and court inflow indicators into a broader outcome that captures unnecessary formal escalation more generally. This composite measure is especially useful where police and court records differ in completeness or where some cases move through one channel more consistently than the other.
Secondary Outcomes
Secondary outcomes are intended to capture the mechanisms and institutional practices through which the intervention may affect the primary outcomes. The first group concerns LC throughput and local resolution. These outcomes should measure whether mentored LCCs are actually receiving, hearing, mediating, and resolving more petty cases locally, rather than merely shifting cases off paper or out of view. Measures in this group may include the number of cases recorded in LCC registers, the number of cases brought to hearing, the number resolved locally, and where feasible the time taken from case opening to local resolution. These indicators are important because a reduction in police or court inflow is substantively meaningful only if it is accompanied by evidence that eligible disputes are being handled more effectively within the local institution itself.
A second group of secondary outcomes concerns referral appropriateness and institutional quality. Here the study should capture whether LCCs are making more appropriate referrals to police, courts, probation, or other actors when cases exceed their local mandate, while also improving their own record-keeping and organizational functioning. This includes indicators such as whether referrals are documented, whether the stated reason for referral is consistent with case type, whether the LCC sits as a committee rather than through an individual chairperson alone, whether quorum is respected, and whether basic records of proceedings and outcomes are maintained. A final secondary dimension concerns gender- and child-sensitive handling, especially because the literature cautions that local justice institutions can improve access while still mishandling cases involving vulnerable groups or inappropriate informalization of serious matters (Mennen, 2010; Rockowitz et al., 2024). For that reason, where records permit, the study should track whether cases involving women, children, or other vulnerable parties appear to be handled with appropriate referral, documentation, and procedural caution, even if these outcomes are treated more as safeguards than as primary indicators of program success.

Therefore, the measurement strategy of this study follows an administrative-data-first approach. This means that the core outcome architecture is built around the routine records that most directly capture how petty disputes move through the justice system, especially Local Council Court records, police registers, and court records where available. This approach is consistent with the central purpose of the evaluation, which is to assess whether the intervention changes case-routing behavior and formal-system inflow rather than relying primarily on self-reported perceptions or recall-based measures. Because the main policy question concerns whether petty matters are handled differently once local institutional capacity is strengthened, the most relevant primary outcomes are those that capture actual changes in case flow.
At the same time, the study uses different measurement logics for Stage 1 and Stage 2. In Stage 1, measurement is primarily diagnostic and mechanism-oriented. It is designed to assess whether directly trained LC leaders improve in knowledge, institutional functioning, record keeping, referral practice, and overall mentor readiness. In Stage 2, measurement is primarily causal and outcome-oriented. It is designed to estimate whether the randomized rollout of mentorship to neighboring villages changes local case handling and reduces unnecessary petty-case inflow into police and courts. The same broad justice architecture links both stages, but the role of measurement differs: Stage 1 focuses on readiness and institutional quality, while Stage 2 focuses on comparative treatment effects in neighboring villages.
For that reason, the primary outcomes of the study are centered on changes in case flow, especially whether petty cases enter police and court channels less frequently once local handling improves. These measures are complemented by a set of secondary and supplementary tools that help interpret mechanisms, validate implementation, and assess whether observed changes reflect genuine institutional improvement rather than only documentation effects. Such tools may include maturity assessments, mentor activity logs, record-quality checks, procedural fairness or legitimacy modules, and targeted knowledge or vignette-based measures where needed. In combination, this measurement strategy is intended to balance parsimony and rigor: administrative outcomes remain the backbone of the evaluation, while supplementary instruments provide the contextual and mechanism-level evidence necessary to interpret those outcomes credibly.
Stage 1 measurement: Directly trained LC leaders
Measurement in Stage 1 is designed to assess whether the direct training delivered by CDFU produces the minimum institutional and behavioral changes required for the mentorship model to function credibly. For that reason, Stage 1 measurement is primarily focused on knowledge, readiness, and institutional quality, rather than on causal impact estimation. The first layer of measurement comes from the training process itself, through pre- and post-training assessments that capture whether directly trained LC leaders improve in knowledge of their legal mandate, jurisdiction, ADR mechanisms, and the broader role of LCCs in handling minor cases. This approach is consistent with justice-sector training evaluations that use pre/post knowledge tools to assess whether frontline actors acquire the basic legal and procedural capacities needed to change practice (Albezreh et al., 2022). It is also directly aligned with CDFU’s own training guide, which includes pre-tests and post-tests as part of the two-day workshop design.
The second layer of Stage 1 measurement is the maturity and readiness assessment, which is more important than attendance or test scores alone because it captures whether training has translated into functioning court practice. Here the study should rely on CDFU’s maturity tool to assess whether directly trained LCs have become operationally credible courts and potential mentors. This includes whether records are maintained, whether proceedings are documented, whether the court sits and functions as a committee, whether cases are handled through ADR where appropriate, whether referrals are made and recorded, and whether the community recognizes the court as a functioning local justice institution. In measurement terms, Stage 1 therefore combines individual-level learning indicators with institutional-level performance indicators. Record-keeping quality, committee functioning, and referral practice are especially important because they are not only outcomes of first-stage training, but also preconditions for mentor eligibility and for the later administrative measurement strategy of the RCT.
A third component of Stage 1 measurement concerns the norms and attitudes explicitly targeted by the training, especially perceptions toward incarcerated and formerly incarcerated persons. Because the intervention includes a norms-change component rather than only a case-handling component, Stage 1 measurement should also document whether directly trained LC leaders show greater willingness to support reintegration, more positive attitudes toward formerly incarcerated persons, and a stronger understanding of their role in supporting resettlement at community level. These dimensions matter programmatically in their own right, but they also matter analytically because mentor-ready LC leaders are expected to carry the full intervention package forward, including both procedural and norms-related content. For that reason, mentor eligibility in Stage 1 should be treated as a composite judgment based on legal-procedural competence, institutional functioning, and readiness to transmit the model onward, rather than on training participation alone.
Stage 2 measurement: Neighboring-village RCT
Measurement in Stage 2 is organized around the core causal question of the study: whether randomized exposure to mentor-led support changes how neighboring villages handle petty disputes and whether those changes reduce unnecessary entry into the formal justice system. For that reason, Stage 2 measurement is centered on village/LCC-by-period outcomes rather than on individual mentor characteristics. The main outcomes should capture changes in how disputes are routed, recorded, resolved, and referred at the level of the neighboring village or LCC catchment. This reflects the broader empirical literature, which suggests that the most policy-relevant effects of mediation, diversion, and community justice interventions are often visible in behavioral and administrative outcomes, such as dispute-routing patterns, case disposition, settlement, or downstream system contact rather than in attitudinal change alone (Blattman et al., 2014; Grajzl et al., 2026; McCart et al., 2022; Sherman et al., 2015).
Accordingly, the measurement logic in Stage 2 should prioritize changes in case-flow outcomes and local justice process outcomes. At the center are administrative indicators of petty cases entering police and court channels, alongside local indicators of case handling within the LCC itself, such as case volume, ADR use, local resolution, referral quality, and record completeness. These outcomes speak directly to the theory of change: if the mentorship model works, neighboring villages should classify cases better, mediate more effectively, refer more appropriately, and document their cases more consistently, with the result that eligible petty matters are handled locally more often and unnecessary formal escalation declines. Because the intervention may also affect whether communities experience LCCs as fairer or more legitimate, supplementary primary data may still be used to measure procedural fairness, trust, and willingness to use local forums, drawing on the procedural justice literature that links fair treatment and legitimacy to decision acceptance and cooperation (Tyler, 1988; Jackson, 2018; Creutzfeldt & Bradford, 2016). But these measures should remain supplementary to the administrative backbone of the RCT rather than displacing it.

Spillover and Displacement Measures
Because the intervention includes a Training-of-Trainers and mentoring component, the study will not assume that villages are affected only when they receive formal training themselves. Instead, the data strategy will explicitly distinguish between direct treatment, indirect exposure, and possible displacement. Direct treatment will be defined by assignment to a rollout wave and confirmed through implementation records. Indirect exposure will be approximated using information on mentoring links, rollout timing, and village adjacency, so that villages not yet formally treated but plausibly influenced by nearby treated villages can be identified analytically. This is important because some not-yet-treated villages may begin to experience changes in Local Council practice or case flows before their official rollout date if trained leaders provide support or if practices diffuse informally across neighboring areas.
Displacement will be tracked separately from positive spillovers. The concern here is not that untreated villages may improve because of diffusion, but that changes in treated villages may shift petty-case reporting or routing into nearby untreated villages or into different institutional channels. For that reason, neighboring not-yet-treated villages will be flagged in the data architecture so that changes in their police, court, and Local Council case patterns can be monitored alongside outcomes in directly-treated villages. Operationally, the study will classify villages into treatment-exposure categories using the rollout roster, hotspot status, village identifiers, and available adjacency information. These categories will later support descriptive and causal analyses of direct effects, indirect exposure, and potential redistribution of cases. This emphasis is fully consistent with the current proposal logic, which already identifies mentoring, neighboring exposure, and displacement as core features of the intervention environment rather than marginal technical issues.
Supplementary Primary Data
Although administrative data will remain the backbone of the evaluation, the proposal allows for a limited amount of targeted primary data collection where it adds clear value. The first potential use of supplementary primary data is to capture legitimacy and procedural fairness, since these are important parts of the intervention’s theory of change but are not directly observable in registers. Short community or user-facing modules, if retained within budget, would focus narrowly on perceptions of fairness, trust, and willingness to use Local Councils for eligible disputes. The purpose would not be to build a large survey component, but to generate a compact mechanism-oriented lens that helps interpret whether changes in forum choice are likely to be sustainable.
A second use of supplementary primary data is to assess whether the training is changing Local Council leaders’ decision-making in the intended direction. For this purpose, the proposal anticipates short institutional tools for LC leaders, such as knowledge checks or case vignettes, that can test how participants classify disputes, decide when to mediate, and determine when referral is required. These tools are especially useful because they connect directly to the intervention’s mechanism of improved triage and referral behavior.
A third use of supplementary primary data is validation. Because the evaluation relies heavily on administrative registers, some light-touch qualitative or survey-based verification may be included to help assess whether changes in recorded outcomes reflect real changes in dispute handling rather than only improvements in documentation. In the current proposal logic, these validation-oriented components would remain deliberately small and targeted (for example, short KIIs, FGDs, or user checks) rather than developing into a parallel survey-heavy design.
## NON-EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION (BENCHMARKING, PROCESS EVALUATION, AND LEARNING)
### BENCHMARKING AND LEARNING
While the impact evaluation component is designed to estimate whether the LC intervention causes a reduction in petty-case inflow into the formal justice system, the broader research agenda for this program extends beyond causal attribution alone. The program also requires a systematic evidence base that can document baseline conditions, track implementation quality, generate benchmarking data for key indicators, and support adaptive learning throughout the life of the intervention. For that reason, this proposal includes a complementary non-experimental component centered on descriptive benchmarking, qualitative inquiry, and process evaluation.
This part of the research design serves a different purpose from the experimental component. It is not intended to answer the causal question of whether the intervention worked relative to a counterfactual. Instead, it is designed to generate the baseline information and implementation evidence needed to assess program performance over time, interpret impact findings more accurately, and identify barriers, facilitators, and operational adjustments that matter for delivery. In practice, this means combining descriptive analysis of administrative and program data with selective qualitative and institutional inquiry. It also means aligning data collection with the indicator framework Legatum intends to use for program monitoring and results reporting, so that the baseline is analytically useful both for future impact estimation and for ongoing management.
Purpose of the Benchmarking and Learning Component
The first function of this component is to establish a rigorous baseline for key outcome and output indicators linked to the Advancing Justice Uganda program. This includes indicators directly related to the Local Council intervention, such as petty-case diversion and local ADR use, but also broader indicators that Legatum is interested in tracking across the justice chain. The value of this baseline is twofold. It creates a reference point against which future changes can be measured, and it helps clarify the starting conditions under which the intervention is being introduced.
The second function is to generate structured learning on implementation. A program can fail to produce measurable effects for many different reasons: weak rollout discipline, low uptake by Local Councils, poor mentoring performance, lack of trust among community members, weak data systems, or inconsistent referral practices. A purely outcome-based impact design does not always reveal which of these factors explain the results. The process evaluation component addresses that gap by documenting how implementation unfolds in practice and by identifying the operational conditions that enable or constrain the theory of change.
The third function is interpretive. The benchmarking and process evaluation component provides the contextual and institutional evidence needed to interpret both experimental and non-experimental findings responsibly. For example, if police and court inflow appears to fall in treated areas, complementary evidence on Local Council throughput, legitimacy, referral quality, and record-keeping helps clarify whether this reflects genuine local resolution, improved triage, or potentially problematic under-referral. In this sense, the non-experimental component is not an optional add-on; it is part of the architecture that makes the overall evaluation meaningful.
Learning and Benchmarking Agenda
The benchmarking and learning component will focus on a limited set of domains that are central to the intervention logic and to Legatum’s reporting framework. These domains include access to justice, perceived accountability and legitimacy, civic awareness of Local Council jurisdiction, gender and inclusion, institutional responsiveness, formal-system congestion trends, and cost and resource use.
In relation to access to justice, the study will examine whether dispute-resolution services and information appear to become more accessible in target areas over time. This will be assessed descriptively using partner monitoring records, selected survey-based evidence where available, and justice-sector administrative data. The aim here is not to estimate causal effects, but to document whether the implementation environment is shifting in ways that are consistent with the program’s intended direction.
A second domain concerns perceived accountability and legitimacy. Because the broader literature makes clear that local justice institutions only function as effective alternatives when they are seen as fair and credible, this domain will examine whether citizens and relevant users perceive Local Councils and formal institutions as more responsive, fair, and accountable over time. This may be measured through short perception modules, grievance records, or targeted qualitative inquiry depending on the final data plan.
A third domain concerns civic engagement and legal awareness. The intervention assumes that eligible petty disputes should be more likely to enter Local Council channels if people understand what Local Councils can and cannot do. Benchmarking in this area will therefore assess whether awareness of Local Council jurisdiction and the appropriate forum for petty disputes increases during implementation.
A fourth domain concerns gender and inclusion. This is not only a normative priority but an analytical necessity, given the risks identified in the literature around local bias, unequal treatment, and inappropriate informalization of sensitive cases. Benchmarking will therefore pay attention to whether the intervention appears to function equitably across women, youth, children, and persons with disabilities, and whether safeguards are operating as intended.
A fifth domain concerns institutional capacity and responsiveness. This part of the learning agenda focuses on whether Local Council leaders appear to improve concrete practices such as record-keeping, triage, mediation, and referral over time. It is closely linked to the process evaluation because it speaks directly to whether the training model is being operationalized as intended.
A sixth domain concerns formal-system congestion trends. Although the impact evaluation will focus on causal estimation for specific outcomes, the broader benchmarking agenda can also document descriptive trends in police and court caseload composition over time, while recognizing that many factors beyond the intervention may influence those trends.
The final domain concerns cost and resource use. Since Legatum is interested in the value-for-money implications of the intervention, the non-experimental component will also compile cost inputs needed for the later cost-effectiveness analysis, including personnel time, transport, training inputs, materials, and overhead allocation.

### PROCESS EVALUATION
The process evaluation component is designed to document how implementation unfolds in practice and how that affects both the plausibility and the interpretation of eventual results. It is especially important in this project because the intervention includes multiple operational layers: direct training, phased rollout, Training-of-Trainers diffusion, mentoring, and administrative case tracking. Even if the impact evaluation is well designed statistically, the study will remain difficult to interpret unless implementation processes are observed and understood.
A first objective of the process evaluation is to identify barriers and facilitators to implementation. This includes operational constraints such as rollout delays, staffing capacity, logistical bottlenecks, and the practical challenges Local Council leaders face in applying what they have been taught. It also includes social and institutional factors, such as community acceptance, local power dynamics, or the willingness of police and courts to recognize and support Local Council referral patterns.
A second objective is to assess the performance of the Training-of-Trainers and mentoring model. Because mentoring is one of the main pathways through which the intervention is expected to diffuse, the process evaluation will document whether it occurs as planned, whether it reaches the intended villages, and whether its intensity and quality vary across space. This is crucial both for substantive learning and for later interpretation of spillovers.
A third objective is to understand local institutional response. This includes how Local Council leaders, police actors, court officials, and community members react to the intervention, whether referral practices shift, whether Local Councils are perceived as handling disputes differently, and whether implementation creates unintended tensions or adaptations in the local justice ecosystem.
A fourth objective concerns data quality and record use. Since the broader evaluation relies heavily on administrative records, the process evaluation will also examine how registers are maintained, whether case documentation practices change after training, and whether record improvements may themselves be part of the intervention effect. This is analytically important because the same intervention that changes case handling may also improve what becomes visible in the data.
## COST-EFFECTIVENESS ANALYSIS (CEA)
The evaluation will include a structured cost-effectiveness analysis to quantify the resources required to achieve measurable reductions in petty cases entering the formal justice system. The CEA will be designed to support two complementary learning goals. First, it will generate partner-specific cost-effectiveness estimates to compare the performance of the two delivery models (CDFU and FCU) under realistic implementation conditions. Second, where intervention effects are sufficiently comparable, it will generate pooled estimates that summarize the overall cost-effectiveness of the LC training approach as a scalable prevention model. The intent is not only to rank partners, but to identify which delivery features drive value for money and what cost structure would look like under scale-up.
### Costing Method
The CEA will rely on an ingredients-based costing approach, which is transparent, auditable, and well-suited for multi-partner programs with heterogeneous delivery structures. For each partner, the evaluation team will compile a standardized cost inventory capturing the full set of resources required to deliver training and post-training support, including staff time (trainers, supervisors, coordination), transport and per diems, training materials, venue-related costs where applicable, communications and monitoring tools (including any ToT-related tracking support), and a clearly documented approach to allocating shared program management and overhead. Costs will be classified into fixed components (e.g., development/adaptation of materials, initial training setup) and variable components (e.g., per-LCC training delivery, travel, mentoring intensity), since this distinction is essential for interpreting cost-effectiveness under different scale scenarios.
### Effect Metrics and Sensitivity Analyses
The primary effectiveness metric will be the cost per petty case diverted from the formal system, defined as the estimated reduction in petty-case entries into police/court registries attributable to the intervention relative to the counterfactual, standardized to the relevant time window. A closely related secondary metric will be the cost per formal filing avoided, which is useful when registry systems record filings more consistently than case categories or dispositions. Where data permit, additional derived metrics can be reported to support operational decision-making (for example, costs per month of reduced formal inflow, or costs per percentage-point reduction in formal entry rates), but the Scope of Work will keep the headline indicators focused and interpretable. Because the program includes structured mentoring and diffusion components, the evaluation will also seek to incorporate spillover-adjusted cost-effectiveness estimates if spillovers can be measured credibly. Conceptually, this means that if trained LCCs improve outcomes not only in their own catchments but also in nearby or mentored LCCs, the relevant “effect denominator” expands and the implied cost-effectiveness improves.
Given uncertainty in both costs and measured effects (especially in administrative-data environments) the CEA will include a set of pre-specified sensitivity analyses. On the costing side, this will test alternative assumptions about overhead allocation, the classification of costs as fixed versus variable, and the alternative costing of staff time where opportunity costs differ from financial expenditures. On the effectiveness side, sensitivity checks will examine alternative definitions of the outcome (e.g., narrower vs broader “petty” categorizations where classification is imperfect), alternative aggregation windows (monthly versus quarterly), and alternative time horizons that reflect realistic diffusion and learning dynamics after training. Where the design supports it, cost-effectiveness will be reported both using the primary intention-to-treat effect and using compliance-adjusted estimates, with clear labeling so Legatum and partners can interpret the results appropriately for budgeting and scale decisions.
## OVERALL EVALUATION TIMELINE
The evaluation is structured as a multi-phase architecture that aligns the research design with the operational realities of the intervention. At the broadest level, the proposal distinguishes between three linked phases: an inception and baseline phase, a monitoring and implementation-tracking phase, and a later endline and impact-estimation phase.
The baseline phase is intended to establish the analytical and operational foundations of the study. This includes finalizing agreements, constructing and cleaning the village-level sampling frame, confirming data access arrangements, compiling pre-period administrative data, finalizing the rollout design, and generating the baseline evidence required both for benchmarking and for future causal analysis. The monitoring phase then follows the intervention as it unfolds, tracking whether the randomized rollout is implemented as assigned, whether mentoring and indirect exposure occur outside intended waves, and whether the administrative data system remains strong enough to support longitudinal analysis. The final phase of the architecture is the endline and impact-analysis stage, in which later treatment comparisons, spillover patterns, and cost-effectiveness estimates are brought together into the final impact-evaluation outputs.
This multi-phase structure is important because it makes clear that the evaluation is not a single one-off field exercise. It is a sequenced research program in which each phase creates conditions for the next. The baseline is not only descriptive; it enables randomization, validates the data systems, and defines the study universe. Monitoring is not only managerial; it protects internal validity by preserving the treatment-control contrast over time. Endline analysis is not an isolated reporting activity; it depends directly on whether the earlier phases were executed with sufficient discipline and documentation. In this sense, the workplan is part of the methodological design itself.
## Baseline / Inception Phase
This phase covers all the activities required to move the study from concept to implementation-ready evaluation design while also generating the first round of benchmark evidence. The first set of activities in this phase concerns start-up and design finalization. This includes establishing formal agreements with Legatum, CDFU, and relevant data-holding institutions; confirming the final evaluation scope; cleaning the village-level administrative frame; and aligning the rollout logic with the evaluation protocol. During this stage, the evaluation team will also finalize the randomization protocol, the measurement architecture, and the operational rules for handling deviations, spillovers, and data linkage issues.
The second set of activities concerns baseline data construction. This includes extraction and harmonization of pre-period administrative data from Local Council, police, and court records, along with any light-touch primary data modules that may be retained for mechanism measurement, validation, or benchmarking. The purpose of this work is to produce both a clean baseline dataset and a baseline report that not only describes conditions at the start of implementation but also demonstrates that the evaluation can be supported by the available data systems.
The final part of the baseline phase concerns reporting and validation. This includes baseline analysis, preparation of the draft report, discussion of findings with Legatum and CDFU, a validation and learning workshop, and finalization of the baseline deliverables. Under the current proposal logic, this phase should culminate in a baseline dataset and codebook, a baseline report, a finalized randomization protocol, and a partner-facing set of operational recommendations to support rollout discipline and data quality.
## Monitoring and Endline Phase Timeline
The next stage is a structured monitoring and implementation-tracking phase that runs alongside the staggered rollout of the intervention. Its purpose is to preserve the integrity of the design by documenting actual treatment timing, mentoring activity, deviations from the assigned rollout sequence, and changes in the administrative data environment over time. This phase would also generate periodic learning outputs, such as short implementation memos or midline briefs, particularly if the rollout extends across multiple waves and years.
The endline phase would occur only after a sufficient share of the rollout has been completed and enough post-treatment time has accumulated to estimate impacts credibly. This phase would involve the final extraction and cleaning of follow-up administrative outcomes, any closing primary-data collection if retained in the design, the final impact and spillover analysis, and the cost-effectiveness analysis. It would culminate in the endline impact report, cost-effectiveness report, and dissemination outputs.
The timing of this later phase will depend on how quickly rollout progresses and how much exposure time is needed to detect meaningful changes in petty-case inflow, Local Council throughput, and referral patterns. For that reason, the proposal does not treat the endline phase as fixed to a mechanical calendar date. Instead, it is tied to implementation and exposure milestones, ensuring that the final analysis is timed to the actual dynamics of the intervention rather than to an arbitrary reporting deadline.
## Decision Gates and Dependencies
The proposed workplan depends on several decision gates that must be crossed in sequence for the later phases to remain feasible and credible. The first is data access. The evaluation cannot proceed in its intended form unless the team secures timely and sustained access to Local Council registers, police records, and court records in a format that can be linked to the village-level frame. This is the foundational dependency for both the baseline and later impact analysis.
The second is the final sampling frame. Randomization, stratification, and later outcome measurement all depend on a cleaned and validated village/LCC roster. Until that roster is confirmed, including hotspot classification and administrative identifiers, the study cannot finalize its analytic sample or assignment structure.
The third is formal agreement with CDFU on the rollout logic. Because the evaluation uses a staggered randomized rollout design, the study depends on CDFU accepting and implementing the agreed sequencing of villages into treatment waves. This is not simply an operational detail; it is the central requirement that makes the counterfactual credible.
The fourth is ethical and administrative approval. Even in an administrative-data-centered design, the project still requires data governance agreements, confidentiality protections, and, where relevant, ethics clearance for any primary-data activities. Delays in these approvals could postpone both baseline data collection and the start of implementation-linked monitoring. The revised SoW already recognized that approvals should be initiated early to avoid bottlenecks later in the timeline.
## KEY RECOMMENDATIONS
The following actions are recommended to strengthen the rigor, credibility, and operational feasibility of the Advancing Justice Uganda baseline study. The recommendations focus on enhancing causal identification, improving statistical power, strengthening measurement systems, and ensuring high implementation fidelity. Collectively, they aim to ensure that the study generates policy-relevant and decision-useful evidence to inform scale-up of the Local Council strengthening model, inform programing and measure program performance. Generally strong coordination between the evaluation team and CDFU is highly recommended, establishing clear coordination mechanisms between evaluation and implementation teams. Specific recommendations include;
- CDFU should work with the evaluation team to ensure implementation fidelity to strengthen mentorship delivery systems by developing structured tools to capture mentorship frequency, duration, and content.
- CDFU should minimize contamination and deviation, by establishing clear operational boundaries between treatment and comparison groups.
- CDFU and the evaluation team should monitor adherence and introduce rapid response mechanisms where deviations occur.
- CDFU should Apply consistent, verifiable criteria for selecting mentor LCs, and conducting periodic reassessments to maintain the training quality.
- Evaluation team should integrate mixed-methods approaches, complementing quantitative data with qualitative insights (KIIs, FGDs, case tracking). And use qualitative data to interpret causal mechanisms and contextual factors.
- The evaluation team should ensure risk mitigation and analytical rigor, managing spillover and contamination Risks by mapping geographic and social proximity between villages, and incorporate spillover effects explicitly in analysis models.
- CDFU and the evaluation team should monitor treatment Intensity variation, capturing variation in mentorship exposure and incorporating intensity measures into impact analysis.
- The evaluation team should align evaluation with implementation capacity, ensuring the evaluation design reflects realistic delivery constraints.
