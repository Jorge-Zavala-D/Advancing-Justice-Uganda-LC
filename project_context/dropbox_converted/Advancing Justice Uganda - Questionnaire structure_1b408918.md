1) What instruments you likely need
You do not need four completely different questionnaires.
The best design is:
One common LC survey core used in both phases and both rounds
One endline-ready institutional assessment / audit block that mirrors the CDFU maturity tool and can also be partially fielded at baseline
Phase-specific add-on modules:
Phase 1 add-on: direct training exposure, mentor readiness, willingness/capacity to mentor
Phase 2 add-on: mentorship receipt, dosage, fidelity, contamination/spillovers
So operationally, I would recommend:
Phase 1 baseline instrument
Phase 1 endline instrument
Phase 2 baseline instrument
Phase 2 endline instrument
But all four should be built from the same core structure, with only a few modules switched on/off depending on phase and round.
2) Main measurement principle for the surveys
The surveys should not try to measure everything. Your primary outcomes are already administrative: petty-case inflow into police and courts. So the LC surveys should focus on what admin data cannot measure well:
knowledge and legal-procedural competence
classification and triage
ADR and mediation practice
referral behavior
committee functioning
record-keeping quality
procedural fairness orientation / perceived legitimacy
safeguards for women, children, and serious cases
attitudes toward incarceration and reintegration
mentor readiness / mentorship exposure
That is exactly where the theory of change and the literature converge.
3) Recommendation on respondent strategy
To keep the instrument within ~30 minutes, I strongly recommend that the primary respondent be:
the directly trained LC representative / LC I Chairperson in Phase 1
the most knowledgeable LC representative in the neighboring village in Phase 2
And that some sections include interviewer verification items based on records or observation.
This is important because several constructs from the maturity tool are not best measured through self-report alone.
So the instrument should be understood as:
survey questions
plus a short interviewer audit / observation block
rather than a pure opinion questionnaire.

Detailed proposed outline of the survey instrument
MODULE 0. Interview metadata, respondent eligibility, and interview context
Rationale. This module is administrative but essential. It ensures that the correct respondent is interviewed, that the village and LC unit are uniquely identified, and that the interview can later be linked to the administrative and randomization datasets.
Indicators
District
Sub-county / division
Parish / ward
Village / LCC catchment
Village ID / LCC ID
Mentor cluster ID, if applicable
Interview date and round
Survey phase: Phase 1 or Phase 2
Baseline or endline
Respondent name/code
Respondent role in LC
Whether respondent is the directly trained person / mentor / neighboring LC respondent
Length of interview
Presence of records during interview
Whether any other LC member was present during interview

MODULE 1. Respondent profile and LC role
Rationale. The literature and your own theory of change suggest that the effectiveness of local justice actors depends partly on who they are, their experience, and their position in the institution. This module gives you baseline comparability, descriptive profiling, and variables for heterogeneity analysis.
Indicators
Position in LC/LCC
Whether currently active in case handling
Years in current role
Total years in LC service
Gender
Age category
Education level
Literacy / ability to complete records in English/local language
Prior justice or mediation training before this program
Prior exposure to police/court/probation coordination
Whether respondent has ever handled land/family/assault/debt disputes
Time available per week for LC work
Contactability / phone access

MODULE 2. Institutional set-up and court composition
Rationale. This module captures whether the LCC is institutionally functional as a committee rather than only as an individual chairperson. That is central both to the CDFU maturity tool and to the literature on local justice quality and procedural regularity.
Indicators
Number of current LC/LCC members
Number of active members
Vacancies in the committee
Presence of women in the committee
Whether the committee meets regularly
Frequency of meetings/sittings
Whether quorum is usually achieved
Whether there is a regular place for hearings
Whether parties are usually informed in advance
Whether the LCC keeps a formal register
Whether the LCC has access to stationery/books/materials needed for hearings

MODULE 3. Recent caseload and case mix
Rationale. This module is not your primary outcome source, but it is important for validation and for understanding the local justice environment. It also helps connect the survey to the administrative case-flow outcomes and to forum-choice theory.
Indicators
Number of cases received in the last 1 month / 3 months
Main types of cases received
Approximate share of petty disputes among all cases
Approximate share of serious/ineligible cases encountered
Number of unresolved pending cases
Number of cases brought directly by community members
Number of cases received by referral from another actor
Most common dispute type currently seen by the LCC
Whether case volume has increased/decreased recently
Perceived main reasons for higher/lower case inflow

MODULE 4. Legal mandate, jurisdiction, and classification knowledge
Rationale. This is one of the most important modules. The intervention assumes that better classification and triage are central mechanisms. The literature also suggests that improved dispute routing depends on correctly distinguishing what can be handled locally and what must be referred.
Indicators
Knowledge of which types of cases the LCC is allowed to hear
Knowledge of which cases must be referred immediately
Knowledge of legal/jurisdictional limits of LC authority
Knowledge of committee/quorum requirements
Knowledge of documentation requirements
Knowledge of steps required before resolving a case
Knowledge of whether/when ADR should be attempted
Knowledge of when police/courts/probation should be involved
Knowledge of bail/bond/community service concepts, if retained in training
Short case vignettes testing:
petty land/boundary dispute
household/family dispute
debt dispute
minor assault
child protection case
sexual or severe violence case
For each vignette:
Can LCC handle?
Should mediate first?
Should refer immediately?
To whom?
This module should be one of the main places where you replicate baseline/endline competence change.

MODULE 5. ADR and mediation practice
Rationale. The literature on community mediation and restorative justice shows that it is not enough to know ADR exists; the quality of mediation practice matters. This module captures how LC members actually approach dispute resolution.
Indicators
Whether mediation is usually attempted before escalation
Whether both sides are usually heard
Whether parties can speak without interruption
Whether respondent tries to remain neutral
Whether respondent seeks reconciliation, negotiated agreement, or authoritative decision
Whether agreements are written down
Whether follow-up is done after agreement
Whether respondent separates parties when needed
Confidence in mediating petty disputes
Confidence in de-escalating conflict
Main barriers to successful mediation
Main reasons mediation fails
Use of community dialogue or reconciliation methods
Whether parties usually comply with mediated agreements

MODULE 6. Referral practice and coordination with formal institutions
Rationale. This is another core mechanism module. The intervention is meant to improve not only local resolution, but also the interface between LCCs and police/courts/probation. The regional literature strongly emphasizes coordination failures as a major implementation bottleneck.
Indicators
Whether referrals are made when cases exceed local mandate
Frequency of referrals in recent period
Main destinations of referral:
police
court
probation/child protection
community development or other service actors
Whether referrals are documented in writing/register
Whether respondent explains to parties why referral is necessary
Whether respondent follows up after referral
Whether police/court officials respond or provide feedback
Ease/difficulty of coordinating with police
Ease/difficulty of coordinating with courts
Main barriers to referral
Main barriers to receiving feedback from referred cases

MODULE 7. Record-keeping and case management quality
Rationale. This module is essential because your entire evaluation relies heavily on administrative data. It should combine self-report with interviewer verification. It directly links to the maturity tool and to the validity of your primary outcomes.
Indicators
Existence of a case register
Existence of a proceedings book
Whether entries are up to date
Whether dates of case opening are recorded
Whether parties’ names/details are recorded
Whether case type is recorded
Whether outcome/resolution status is recorded
Whether referral status is recorded
Whether closure date is recorded
Whether agreements are attached or written
Whether records can be retrieved easily
Whether records are complete for recently handled cases
Whether records are kept securely/confidentially
Interviewer verification:
register seen
proceedings book seen
latest cases updated
closure/referral visible
basic consistency of sample entries
This should replicate much of the maturity tool in a structured way.

MODULE 8. Committee sitting and decision-making process
Rationale. The program’s theory is institutional, not merely individual. The maturity tool specifically emphasizes whether the LCC functions as a committee. This module captures procedural regularity and internal functioning.
Indicators
Whether cases are usually handled by full committee or by one person
Whether quorum is respected in practice
Whether different members participate actively
Whether decisions are discussed collectively
Whether one member dominates decisions
Whether women members participate
Whether records are signed/validated by multiple members
Whether meetings are held for case review
Whether there is internal disagreement on how to handle cases
Confidence that committee decisions are consistent across cases

MODULE 9. Procedural fairness orientation and perceived local legitimacy
Rationale. The procedural justice literature is highly relevant here. Even if your strongest legitimacy measures may eventually come from community-side data, you still need LC-side measures of fairness norms, self-reported practice, and perceived trust.
Indicators
Importance of giving each party a chance to speak
Importance of impartial treatment
Importance of respectful treatment
Importance of explaining decisions
Importance of consistency across similar cases
Importance of honesty and trustworthy motives
Whether respondent believes community sees LCC as fair
Whether respondent believes community trusts the LCC
Whether community members are willing to bring petty disputes to LCC
Perceived corruption risks in local justice
Perceived reasons some people bypass LCC and go to police/courts directly

MODULE 10. Gender, child protection, and serious-case safeguards
Rationale. This is a critical safeguards module. The literature warns that local justice can improve access but still mishandle women-, child-, or violence-related cases. This module should be short but non-negotiable.
Indicators
Ability to identify cases that should not remain at LCC
Whether sexual violence cases must be referred
Whether child abuse cases must be referred
Whether serious assault must be referred
Whether confidentiality is maintained in sensitive cases
Whether women can speak safely in proceedings
Whether children/vulnerable persons require special handling
Whether respondent knows where to refer sensitive cases
Whether respondent feels confident making such referrals
Main barriers to appropriate referral in sensitive cases

MODULE 11. Perceptions toward incarcerated and formerly incarcerated persons; reintegration norms
Rationale. This module is specific to your intervention content and should definitely be included. It directly maps to the norms/reintegration component of the training and to Legatum’s wider interest in reintegration conditions.
Indicators
Belief that formerly incarcerated people can reintegrate successfully
Willingness to support their return to community life
Belief that communities should help reintegration
Belief that ex-prisoners should be excluded/avoided
Belief that former inmates are likely to reoffend
Comfort mediating disputes involving formerly incarcerated persons
Willingness to help connect them to local support/resources
Perception of community stigma toward formerly incarcerated persons
Whether LC has recently handled reintegration-related cases or tensions

MODULE 12. Training exposure, confidence, and capacity to apply learning
Rationale. This is where you capture direct uptake of the intervention content. In Phase 1 endline it is crucial interpreting mentor readiness. In Phase 2 endline it helps distinguish no-effect due to weak treatment receipt from no-effect due to theory failure.
Indicators
Whether respondent understood the material
Confidence applying what was learned
Whether training changed day-to-day practice

MODULE 13. Mentor readiness, mentoring willingness, and mentoring capacity
Rationale. This module is essential only for Phase 1 endline. It should capture whether directly trained LC leaders are ready and willing to transmit the model onward.
Indicators
Willingness to mentor neighboring villages
Confidence in training other LC members
Ability to explain legal mandate and referral rules to others
Ability to demonstrate record-keeping to others
Ability to coach on mediation/ADR

MODULE 14. Mentorship receipt, dosage, fidelity, and spillover exposure
Rationale. This is the key Stage 2 endline implementation module. It allows you to measure whether the neighboring village actually received the intended treatment, how much of it, and whether there was contamination before official rollout for wave 2.
Indicators
Whether village received any mentor visit/support
Whether support came from officially assigned mentor or informally from another village
Whether materials were shared informally across villages
This module should be in Phase 2 endline in a contamination-detection form for control neighbor villages before wave 2 starts and in wave 1 treated villages in full dosage/fidelity form.

My recommendation on final survey architecture
I would not build four completely different instruments.
I would build:
Instrument Core
Common to all four rounds:
Modules 0–11
Add-on A: Training / Mentor Readiness
For Phase 1, especially endline:
Modules 12–13
Add-on B: Mentorship Receipt / Spillovers
For Phase 2:
Module 14
plus short training exposure items from Module 12
That gives you:
maximum comparability
lower programming burden
easier panel analysis
stronger alignment with the theory of change

Recommended total length
To stay within ~30 minutes, I would aim for:
Core LC survey: ~45–55 items
Short interviewer audit block: ~10–15 checks
Phase-specific add-on: ~8–12 items
So total:
Baseline rounds: ~50–60 items
Endline rounds: ~60–70 items
That is realistic.
Trying to push toward 100 substantive items will likely hurt quality, especially if respondents need to consult registers.

Most important methodological caution
Some of your most important constructs are not best measured by self-report alone:
record-keeping quality
committee functioning
referral documentation
mentor readiness
case-management quality
So for those, the instrument should explicitly include:
respondent answers
plus enumerator verification / observation items
That will make your measures much more defensible and much more aligned with both the CDFU maturity tool and the literature on implementation fidelity and institutional performance.
