1) Background and Program Context (revised & tightened)
1.1 Program Overview: Advancing Justice Uganda
The Legatum Foundation is implementing “Advancing Justice Uganda” in partnership with six Civil Society Organizations (CSOs), with the overarching ambition of improving access to justice, strengthening accountability across the justice chain, and reducing systemic bottlenecks that drive congestion and unfair delays.
The program’s core objectives (as framed in the baseline SoW) include:
Prove a model, articulated through integrated pathways that aim to prevent unnecessary entry into the criminal justice system, improve access to legal services, and support post-release reintegration.
Norms change, to shift public and institutional attitudes toward justice-involved populations and promote dignity, rights-based practice, and accountability.
Network and alliance building, to sustain reform-oriented coalitions and strengthen system-wide advocacy.
Scope prioritization for this SoW (Legatum request):
While the broader program includes multiple components (e.g., legal aid, prison-based support, rehabilitation/economic empowerment, and communications), this Scope of Work prioritizes the “Local Council (LC) prevention pathway”—i.e., strengthening community-level dispute resolution to prevent petty cases from entering police/courts in the first place, consistent with Legatum’s direction and the implementers’ operational focus for the impact evaluation.

1.2 Problem Statement (Uganda justice bottlenecks)
Uganda’s justice system faces a set of interlocking constraints that jointly produce delays, congestion, and avoidable incarceration. As articulated in the program theory of change, the system is characterized by excessive pre-trial detention, severe case backlogs, and the systematic denial of fair and timely hearings, spanning the three core pillars of the justice chain: policing, the judiciary, and prisons.
A central bottleneck relevant to this evaluation is the upstream inflow of petty cases into formal justice institutions. Minor disputes and low-level offences—many of which could be handled through alternative or community-based mechanisms—often enter the formal system, contributing to unnecessary arrests, longer remand durations, and increased pressure on courts and prisons.
This “pipeline” problem is not only about efficiency; it is also about legitimacy and access. When the formal system is overloaded, the consequences are typically borne most heavily by those with fewer resources (e.g., limited access to legal representation, higher costs of case-follow-up, and greater vulnerability to long remand). The program’s baseline framing explicitly links these dynamics to chronic overcrowding and prolonged remand, driven by constraints such as delayed investigations, limited legal aid, and slow court processing.
Why “prevention” is strategically pivotal:
Even strong downstream legal aid and prison-based interventions will struggle to decongest the system sustainably if petty cases continue entering at scale. The prevention pathway addresses congestion closer to its source by shifting eligible disputes toward timely, local resolution and ensuring appropriate routing (i.e., diversion for petty matters; referral for serious cases), consistent with the program’s core logic.

The intervention prioritized in this evaluation is a structured training and post-training support package for Local Council Courts (LCCs)/Local Council leaders, delivered using an official training manual (with updates aligned to the Ministry of Local Government handbook).
The prevention mechanism is explicit: by strengthening LC capacity to classify, mediate, and resolve petty cases locally—while improving appropriate referral for serious matters—the intervention aims to divert eligible cases before they reach police stations and courts, reducing pressure on formal institutions and mitigating avoidable detention and delay.
Implementing partners and delivery modalities:
Two partners are delivering the LC training intervention, with distinct geographic footprints and operational approaches:
Community for Development Foundation Uganda (CDFU) covering Bushenyi, Sheema, and Rubirizi.

3) Evaluation Purpose, Learning Agenda, and Key Questions (reframed)
3.1 Primary Purpose
The primary purpose of this evaluation is to generate rigorous causal evidence on whether strengthening community-level dispute resolution through Local Council Court (LCC) training and structured post-training support reduces the upstream inflow of petty cases into the formal justice system in Uganda. This focus reflects Legatum’s explicit guidance to prioritize the LC prevention pathway as the core intervention area for an impact evaluation.
The evaluation is designed to serve both accountability and learning objectives. From an accountability perspective, it will estimate the extent to which the intervention produces measurable reductions in the formal system’s workload and associated downstream burdens. From a learning perspective, it will identify the mechanisms through which the intervention succeeds or fails, and the conditions under which impacts are larger or more equitable—producing actionable evidence for improving implementation and informing scale-up decisions.
3.2 Primary Impact Evaluation Question
The core impact evaluation question is intentionally narrow and policy-relevant:
Does LC/LCC training and structured support reduce the flow of petty cases entering the formal justice system (police and courts), relative to a credible counterfactual?
This question will be answered using a causal identification strategy suited to community-level governance interventions and designed to account explicitly for phased implementation and potential spillovers arising from the Training-of-Trainers (ToT) diffusion component.
3.3 Secondary Questions (mechanisms, heterogeneity, safeguards)
To ensure that impact estimates translate into implementable learning, the evaluation will be structured around a compact set of secondary questions that explain how and for whom the intervention works, and whether it improves outcomes without creating harms or inequities.
On mechanisms, the evaluation will assess whether the intervention changes the upstream “processing” of disputes in ways consistent with the theory of change—specifically whether training improves (i) classification and triage (distinguishing cases within LC jurisdiction from those requiring formal handling), (ii) mediation capacity and resolution quality (ability to reach durable agreements without escalation), and (iii) referral appropriateness and coordination (serious cases being referred promptly and appropriately, while petty cases are resolved locally). These mechanisms align directly with the content and intent of the official training manual and the operational emphasis on diversion through strengthened local dispute resolution.
On heterogeneity, the evaluation will test whether impacts vary systematically across contexts and delivery models. This will include differences by baseline dispute volume and formal-system reliance, geographic accessibility and proximity to police/courts, and the distinct partner implementation architectures (CDFU versus FCU), which differ in training intensity, district footprint, and the structure of ToT mentoring.
On safeguards and equity, the evaluation will examine whether diversion is achieved in ways that preserve procedural fairness and protect vulnerable groups. In particular, it will assess whether LC processes improve or worsen perceived legitimacy and fairness, and whether sensitive categories of disputes—especially SGBV-related and child-related cases—are handled in a gender-sensitive manner with appropriate referral to the formal system when required, rather than being inappropriately “absorbed” into local informal resolution. This dimension is central both to ethical compliance and to ensuring that measured reductions in formal-system inflow do not mask harmful diversion dynamics for victims or marginalized groups.
3.4 Non-LC Components (scoped as non-RCT learning studies)
The Advancing Justice Uganda program includes additional pillars beyond the LC prevention pathway, including prison- and court-facing activities and broader norms and coalition-building components. Consistent with Legatum’s guidance and the feasibility constraints discussed with implementers, these elements will not serve as the causal centerpiece of this Scope of Work.
Instead, the evaluation team will propose a complementary “learning layer” for non-LC components consisting of targeted process monitoring, descriptive benchmarking, and qualitative learning to document implementation quality, uptake, and operational constraints, and to maintain coherence with the program-level theory of change. Where administrative indicators are readily available, the team will also propose routine trend monitoring to support internal learning and triangulation, while explicitly distinguishing these descriptive analyses from the causal claims generated by the LC-focused impact evaluation.

4) Theory of Change and Hypotheses (updated to LC-centric ToC)
4.1 LC-Centric Theory of Change
The evaluation is anchored in a tightened theory of change that isolates the Local Council prevention pathway as the primary mechanism for reducing formal justice congestion. The core premise is that a meaningful share of disputes and minor offences can be handled at the community level when local justice actors are sufficiently capable, confident, and perceived as legitimate, and when the boundary between “petty” and “serious” cases is applied consistently.
The intervention trains and supports Local Council Court leaders using an official manual, with post-training mentoring and diffusion through a Training-of-Trainers (ToT) model. This package is expected to increase LC leaders’ ability to (i) correctly classify and triage cases according to jurisdiction and severity, (ii) apply more effective mediation and settlement practices that prevent escalation and repeat disputes, and (iii) make appropriate referrals when a case requires formal handling (including sensitive cases such as SGBV and child protection matters).
If these intermediate changes occur, the immediate system-level implication is a reduction in the number (and potentially the share) of petty cases that are routed to police or formal courts. This reduces demand pressure on the formal justice system, allowing scarce resources (police time, court slots, prosecutorial attention) to be reallocated toward more serious cases and reducing the downstream drivers of congestion. The prevention pathway therefore targets congestion “upstream,” complementing downstream efforts that focus on accelerating case processing for individuals already inside the system.
A defining feature of the intervention architecture is that learning is not expected to remain confined to directly trained LCs. Both partners embed a ToT diffusion mechanism whereby trained leaders mentor other LCs, potentially expanding the intervention’s reach beyond assigned units. This diffusion is programmatically desirable (for scale and sustainability), but it also implies that impacts may arise through both direct effects (on trained LCs) and indirect/spillover effects (on nearby or network-linked LCs that receive mentoring or knowledge transfers).
Finally, the theory of change recognizes a critical enabling condition: diversion effects depend not only on LC capacity but also on whether community members view LC processes as procedurally fair, unbiased, and capable of producing enforceable outcomes. Where legitimacy is low—or where local institutions are perceived as captured, discriminatory, or unsafe—citizens may continue to route disputes to police/courts even if LCs are trained. For that reason, the evaluation will treat procedural fairness and safeguards as integral to the causal story rather than as peripheral considerations.
4.2 Testable Hypotheses
The LC-centric theory of change motivates a set of testable hypotheses aligned with administrative outcomes, intermediate process measures, and—where feasible—perception-based indicators.
H1 (Caseload diversion): LCCs/villages assigned to receive LC training and structured support will experience a reduction in petty-case inflow to the formal justice system (police and/or courts), relative to a credible counterfactual. This is the primary hypothesis and corresponds directly to the prevention objective highlighted by Legatum.
H2 (Substitution / mechanism consistency): The decline in formal-system inflow will be accompanied by evidence of substitution toward local resolution, reflected in either (or both) (i) higher volumes of cases processed and resolved through LC mechanisms and/or (ii) improved referral quality—meaning that a greater share of cases routed upward are those that legitimately require formal handling, while petty matters are retained locally.
H3 (Procedural justice and legitimacy): Where citizen perceptions are measured, treated areas will exhibit improved perceived legitimacy, trust, and procedural fairness of LC processes, consistent with the view that improved performance and clearer procedures can shift forum-choice behavior toward LCs. This hypothesis is also directly connected to safeguard concerns: improvements must not come at the expense of fairness or protection of vulnerable groups.
H4 (System spillovers via ToT diffusion): Because the intervention includes ToT mentoring, improvements will not be confined to directly trained LCCs. Outcomes may improve in nearby or linked “non-treated” LCCs through knowledge diffusion, producing measurable spillover effects (e.g., reduced formal inflow or improved LC processing in those units as well). This hypothesis is especially relevant given the explicit ToT mentoring targets in both partner models and will inform both the evaluation design and interpretation of estimates.

5) Evaluation Design and Identification Strategy (major upgrade)
5.1 Preferred Design (LC intervention)
This Scope of Work proposes an evaluation design that is fully aligned with Legatum’s request to prioritize the Local Council prevention pathway as the causal centerpiece. Because the intervention is delivered to groups of local justice actors operating within a defined community catchment, and because implementation is expected to be phased rather than simultaneous, the preferred approach is a cluster-based randomized design, implemented through a randomized phase-in (stepped rollout).
Under this approach, eligible Local Council Courts (LCCs) (or their village-level catchments) are randomly assigned to receive the training and structured support earlier versus later. This creates a clear counterfactual during the rollout window, while remaining operationally and ethically feasible because all sites ultimately receive the intervention once rollout is completed. The resulting design allows the evaluation to compare trends in key outcomes between LCCs that have already received the intervention and those scheduled to receive it later, while accounting for broad time trends.
This preferred design also matches the reality that both implementing partners already anticipate multi-year implementation and sequential training waves across districts. It therefore minimizes disruption to partner plans while still generating credible impact estimates.
5.2 Key Design Challenges
The evaluation design must explicitly address two challenges that are central to the intervention’s architecture and to the credibility of causal estimates.
First, the Training-of-Trainers (ToT) diffusion component introduces a real risk of contamination between treated and untreated LCCs. This risk is especially salient where mentoring is driven by geographic proximity or informal networks, as appears likely under the CDFU model. If trained leaders provide mentoring or case-tracking support to leaders in LCCs that were assigned to receive training later, the contrast between treatment and control is reduced and simple comparisons will underestimate true program effects.
Second, the intervention is not delivered through a single uniform model: the two partner approaches differ in training intensity, targeting scale, and ToT structure. A single pooled design that ignores these differences would risk conflating partner-specific delivery effects with “program effects.” Instead, the evaluation strategy must preserve internal validity within each partner’s footprint while also enabling structured learning about how implementation architecture shapes results.
5.3 Design Options to Present (and decide with Legatum/partners)
Because final operational details (numbers of eligible LCCs by district, timing of rollout waves, and the extent to which ToT mentoring can be structured) will determine what is feasible, this Scope of Work proposes a set of design options to be finalized jointly with Legatum and the implementers. The intention is to keep the design rigorous while ensuring it is implementable under real-world constraints.
Option A: Randomized phase-in within each partner (recommended default).
The most straightforward option is to randomize the timing of training among eligible LCCs within each partner’s districts. LCCs would be assigned to early vs. later waves within the planned rollout calendar. This preserves partner autonomy and avoids the need to deny the intervention to any community. To strengthen credibility, assignment would be balanced within relevant administrative groupings (e.g., by district and, where possible, by parish-level clusters) so that early and later groups are comparable at baseline. This option also naturally supports a design in which the evaluation uses repeated administrative outcomes measured monthly or quarterly.
Option B: Cluster randomization with geographic buffers.
If ToT mentoring is expected to occur through proximity-based selection, one way to protect the counterfactual is to create “buffer zones” between treated and comparison LCCs during the evaluation window. Practically, this means avoiding the assignment of “early treatment” to an LCC that is adjacent to an LCC assigned to receive training later, or excluding highly exposed comparison LCCs from primary comparisons. This option can materially reduce contamination risk, but it may also reduce the number of usable comparison units, which can affect statistical precision. Because this is a Scope of Work, the key point is that buffers can be used as a practical safeguard against spillovers when diffusion is likely and difficult to monitor.
Option C: Diffusion-aware design elements (encouragement / saturation) if feasible.
If partners are willing and able to structure some aspects of mentoring—for example, by pre-specifying which trained LCCs are expected to mentor which additional LCCs, or by varying “mentoring intensity” intentionally—then the evaluation can move beyond simply treating spillovers as a threat and instead learn from them directly. This option would allow the study to estimate not only the direct impacts of training but also the added value (and cost-effectiveness) of diffusion mechanisms. This design element is more complex, and would only be recommended if it can be implemented without disrupting program delivery and without excessive tracking burdens.
In all cases, the final design decision will depend on: (i) the finalized LCC sampling frame and training schedule by district, (ii) the practical ability to monitor mentoring and exposure, and (iii) Legatum’s tolerance for design complexity relative to timeline and budget.
5.4 Estimands
To keep the evaluation transparent and aligned with implementer realities, the Scope of Work defines a small set of estimands that correspond to what the design can credibly identify.
The primary estimand will be the Intention-to-Treat (ITT) effect: the difference in outcomes between LCCs assigned to earlier rollout versus later rollout, regardless of the degree of training uptake or mentoring realized in practice. This is the most policy-relevant and implementation-robust estimand, and it is the safest basis for reporting impacts in environments where compliance and diffusion may not be perfectly controllable.
A secondary estimand may be the Treatment-on-the-Treated (ToT) effect, where feasible. This would require reliable measurement of who actually received training, when, and what level of post-training support occurred. If those compliance indicators can be captured through training attendance records and mentoring logs, the evaluation can provide complementary estimates that reflect the effect of actual exposure.
Finally, where exposure can be credibly approximated (e.g., based on distance to trained LCCs, mentoring links, or observable diffusion patterns), the evaluation will seek to estimate spillover effects. In the Scope of Work, the emphasis is not on sophisticated modeling, but on the principle that diffusion is a core feature of the intervention and should be measured and reported rather than ignored.

6.3 Randomization Procedure
The evaluation will rely on random assignment of rollout timing, implemented at the level of the LCC/village catchment (or a higher cluster level if spillover risks require it). The final unit of randomization will be selected based on the operational mapping of diffusion and contamination risks, but the default remains LCC-level assignment within partner footprints because it maximizes statistical power and aligns with the intervention’s administrative locus.
Randomization will be conducted separately within each partner’s operational footprint to ensure that impacts are not confounded by cross-partner differences in delivery. Within each partner, assignment will be structured to ensure balance along the most important administrative and baseline predictors of outcomes. At minimum, stratification will be done by district, and where feasible by parish or by pre-intervention caseload intensity (if baseline administrative data provide a proxy). Stratification improves comparability between early and later rollout groups and is particularly valuable in governance and justice settings where baseline levels of formal-system entry may vary substantially across areas.
The randomization process will follow a reproducible protocol suitable for external review and later academic reporting. This includes producing a documented randomization script, generating assignment tables that can be shared with partners for implementation, and maintaining a locked record of the final assignment. Where Legatum and partners prefer, randomization can be conducted through a “public” or transparent process (e.g., a witnessed lottery or joint call), but the default approach is a controlled and fully documented randomization implemented by the evaluation team, with transparency ensured through documentation and version-controlled reproducibility materials.
Operationally, the study will also include clear rules around implementation integrity: the assigned rollout schedule must be feasible for partners, and deviations from schedule must be logged systematically. This is essential both for credible impact estimation and for later cost-effectiveness analysis, since intensity and timing of delivery drive both costs and outcomes.
6.4 Power and Minimum Detectable Effects
This Scope of Work includes a preliminary plan for statistical power assessment to ensure the evaluation can detect policy-relevant effects under realistic assumptions. Because the evaluation is cluster-based and relies heavily on administrative time-series outcomes, power will depend not only on the number of LCCs but also on how outcomes vary across time and across clusters.
The evaluation team will produce a formal power and Minimum Detectable Effect Size (MDES) assessment during the technical proposal phase. To do so, three inputs are required early:
First, baseline levels and dispersion of the primary outcome: the typical number or rate of petty cases entering police/courts from each catchment over a defined period. This can be estimated from historical administrative data if those records exist and can be cleaned, or from the baseline diagnostic phase.
Second, an estimate of intra-cluster correlation (ICC) or, more broadly, the degree to which outcomes are correlated within administrative groupings and over time. If ICC cannot be reliably estimated prior to baseline, the evaluation will use conservative assumptions and refine them once baseline data are collected.
Third, the number of repeated time periods available for analysis. Because the design uses a phased rollout and relies on administrative records, having multiple months or quarters of pre- and post-training data materially increases precision and reduces MDES. The feasibility of extracting these repeated measures consistently across LCC registers and formal system registries is therefore a key operational determinant of the design.
At the Scope of Work stage, the implication is straightforward: finalizing the sampling frame and confirming accessible administrative time-series data are not only measurement tasks, but the core prerequisites to confirm the evaluation’s feasibility and calibrate a design that is adequately powered for Legatum’s learning priorities.

9) Cost-Effectiveness Analysis (CEA)
9.1 CEA Objectives
In line with Legatum’s request, the evaluation will include a structured cost-effectiveness analysis to quantify the resources required to achieve measurable reductions in petty cases entering the formal justice system. The CEA will be designed to support two complementary learning goals. First, it will generate partner-specific cost-effectiveness estimates to compare the performance of the two delivery models (CDFU and FCU) under realistic implementation conditions. Second, where intervention effects are sufficiently comparable, it will generate pooled estimates that summarize the overall cost-effectiveness of the LC training approach as a scalable prevention model. The intent is not only to rank partners, but to identify which delivery features drive value for money and what cost structure would look like under scale-up.
9.2 Costing Method
The CEA will rely on an ingredients-based costing approach, which is transparent, auditable, and well-suited for multi-partner programs with heterogeneous delivery structures. For each partner, the evaluation team will compile a standardized cost inventory capturing the full set of resources required to deliver training and post-training support, including staff time (trainers, supervisors, coordination), transport and per diems, training materials, venue-related costs where applicable, communications and monitoring tools (including any ToT-related tracking support), and a clearly documented approach to allocating shared program management and overhead. Costs will be classified into fixed components (e.g., development/adaptation of materials, initial training setup) and variable components (e.g., per-LCC training delivery, travel, mentoring intensity), since this distinction is essential for interpreting cost-effectiveness under different scale scenarios.
9.3 Effect Metrics
The primary effectiveness metric will be the cost per petty case diverted from the formal system, defined as the estimated reduction in petty-case entries into police/court registries attributable to the intervention relative to the counterfactual, standardized to the relevant time window. A closely related secondary metric will be the cost per formal filing avoided, which is useful when registry systems record filings more consistently than case categories or dispositions. Where data permit, additional derived metrics can be reported to support operational decision-making (for example, costs per month of reduced formal inflow, or costs per percentage-point reduction in formal entry rates), but the Scope of Work will keep the headline indicators focused and interpretable.
Because the program includes structured mentoring and diffusion components, the evaluation will also seek to incorporate spillover-adjusted cost-effectiveness estimates if spillovers can be measured credibly. Conceptually, this means that if trained LCCs improve outcomes not only in their own catchments but also in nearby or mentored LCCs, the relevant “effect denominator” expands and the implied cost-effectiveness improves. Whether and how this adjustment is implemented will depend on the feasibility of constructing a defensible exposure measure in the administrative data.
9.4 Sensitivity Analyses
Given uncertainty in both costs and measured effects—especially in administrative-data environments—the CEA will include a set of pre-specified sensitivity analyses. On the costing side, this will test alternative assumptions about overhead allocation, the classification of costs as fixed versus variable, and alternative costing of staff time where opportunity costs differ from financial expenditures. On the effectiveness side, sensitivity checks will examine alternative definitions of the outcome (e.g., narrower vs broader “petty” categorizations where classification is imperfect), alternative aggregation windows (monthly versus quarterly), and alternative time horizons that reflect realistic diffusion and learning dynamics after training. Where the design supports it, cost-effectiveness will be reported both using the primary intention-to-treat effect and using compliance-adjusted estimates, with clear labeling so Legatum and partners can interpret the results appropriately for budgeting and scale decisions.

10.1 Data Quality Protocols
Because the evaluation is anchored in administrative registers, data quality assurance will focus on (i) standardizing extraction, (ii) auditing completeness and consistency, and (iii) maintaining a clear chain of custody from source registers to cleaned analytical datasets. If any supplementary primary data are included (short surveys, vignettes, KIIs/FGDs), the evaluation will apply standard survey QA protocols, including enumerator training, piloting, programmed checks in data capture tools, and a defined plan for back-checks and spot checks. These procedures are not intended to expand field burden, but to ensure that any primary data collected are credible and usable for triangulation and mechanism interpretation.
10.2 Ethics
The ethical approach will be proportional to the evaluation’s data collection modalities and risk profile. Where the evaluation relies exclusively on administrative records that do not include direct identifiers in the analytic dataset, the primary ethical requirements center on confidentiality, data minimization, and secure storage. The evaluation will implement clear procedures for secure transfer and storage of data, restricted access protocols, and de-identification rules prior to analysis. If identifiers are required temporarily for cleaning or linkage, they will be stored separately with controlled access and removed from analysis files as early as feasible.
Where direct interaction with individuals occurs (e.g., surveys, interviews, group discussions, or vignette-based assessments), informed consent procedures will be used, along with clear explanations of voluntary participation, non-retaliation, and the absence of any effect on access to services. The evaluation will also include a structured plan for managing sensitive information, including guidance to enumerators and moderators on minimizing harm and ensuring privacy during data collection.
The specific IRB/ethics review pathway will be confirmed during the technical proposal phase and will reflect Legatum’s requirements, partner institutional processes, and the regulatory environment in Uganda. The Scope of Work anticipates that approvals may be pursued through an appropriate Ugandan ethics body and/or a partner-linked pathway where relevant, with the objective of starting the process early to avoid timeline bottlenecks.
10.3 Safeguarding for Sensitive Cases
Although the causal evaluation focus is on preventing petty cases from entering the formal system, the program operates in a legal pluralism environment where informal or semi-formal institutions sometimes encounter cases that should not be diverted—particularly sexual and gender-based violence (SGBV), child protection matters, and other serious offenses requiring formal referral and specialized services. The evaluation will therefore include safeguarding provisions to ensure that measurement and program learning do not unintentionally incentivize inappropriate diversion.
At minimum, the Scope of Work will require partners to maintain and apply clear referral protocols consistent with Ugandan law and safeguarding standards, including documentation of referral pathways to police, courts, probation/child protection authorities, and relevant service providers. Where feasible, the evaluation will include limited monitoring indicators—drawn from registers or process logs—to verify that safeguarding rules are understood and applied in practice. This safeguarding layer is essential both for ethical integrity and for correct interpretation of results: a measured reduction in formal entry is only meaningful if it reflects appropriate diversion of petty matters rather than misclassification or suppression of serious cases.
